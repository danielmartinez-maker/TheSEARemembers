## Chapter 206: The Storm’s Mistake

Clarisse knew the early-winter storm had come early because the first rain hit the work board.

One cold drop struck the corner of Ianthe’s clean copy. It left a dark, round mark between “fence drainage” and the blank column for who had agreed to help. Clarisse reached out and moved the page beneath the porch roof.

The second drop landed on her sleeve.

“Inside,” Chiron said from the doorway.

“I’m inside.”

“Farther inside.”

Clarisse stepped back from the board. The wind shoved rain beneath the eaves, slanting it across the porch in silver lines. A gust caught the lower corner of the temporary canvas cover over the rack. It snapped once, hard enough to make the tied rope sing.

Ianthe watched the canvas. “The wind was supposed to turn after noon.”

“It turned now,” Clarisse said.

“It’s not a full turn.”

“Still wet.”

Ianthe’s mouth tightened. She took her pencil and crossed out the noon mark, then replaced it with *before the front*. It was neat writing. That did not make it more useful.

Chiron came around the board with his cloak fastened at the throat. “No one goes on the awning or the roof. We secure what can be reached from the ground. If the water rises over the lower path, we stop and move inside.”

Clarisse knew the route he meant. The low path ran from the fence toward the canoe shed, past a shallow drainage cut that had been due for a proper inspection since the first heavy rain. A board had been set beside the cut to keep loose gravel from washing out. Someone—probably Beckendorf—had propped it at an angle toward the lower ditch.

It was not pretty. It had worked.

“We can get the canvas roll under cover before the ground goes soft,” Ianthe said. She tapped the schedule. “Then the dry line and tags. The fence observation stays last.”

“Who agreed to move the canvas?” Clarisse asked.

“Beckendorf said he could help if the weather changed.”

“He said he could help with the rack.”

“The rack holds the canvas.”

“That’s not the same job.”

Ianthe looked at the sheet as if the difference might be written there. “It’s the same materials.”

“It’s a different path and a different lift.”

“I can move it with one other person.”

“Then you should ask the other person.”

“I already did.”

“Who?”

“Eli.”

Clarisse turned toward the covered walk. Eli stood near the tool shelf, tying a loose strap around a bundle of empty grain sacks. He did not look up.

“Eli,” Clarisse called. “Did you agree to move the canvas roll?”

He pulled the knot tight before answering. “I said I could help stack dry sacks inside.”

Ianthe glanced at the schedule. Her pencil hovered over his name. “I thought the roll was part of that.”

“You thought wrong,” Clarisse said.

“I asked him.”

“You asked him about sacks.”

The pencil pressed a small dent into the page. Ianthe made no mark. Clarisse could tell she was trying to hold still.

“What do you want me doing?” Ianthe asked.

“Ask Chiron what still needs doing.”

“I know what needs doing.”

“Then ask who’s doing it.”

The canvas cracked again in the wind. This time the upper tie slipped half an inch through its loop. Clarisse felt the old reflex—to grab the nearest person and give them a task before the weather could get worse. It worked in a fight. It did not work as a general rule.

Chiron had already sent Beckendorf to check the inside brace from the ground. Percy and Annabeth were moving the lighter supplies from the open shelf into the kitchen passage. Clarisse had volunteered to watch the lower path because she knew where the stones shifted after rain. She had not volunteered for a new command post.

“I’ll take the line and check the path,” she said. “Beckendorf can look at the canvas tie. Eli has the sacks. Nobody carries anything through the low cut until we know where the water is going.”

Leandros came up the covered walk with a coil of rope over his shoulder. His hair was still dry, but the cuffs of his jacket had darkened from the rain.

“The lower cut is already backing up,” he said. “It needs a channel.”

“It needs an inspection,” Clarisse said.

“It needs water to move.”

“Those are not the same thing.”

“I can see where it’s pooling.”

“You can see the top of the ground.”

Leandros’s eyes moved to the board by the cut. “That board is blocking the flow.”

“Leave it where it is.”

“If the water reaches the shed, the rope will be soaked.”

“Then we move the rope after we check the route.”

“By then it’ll be too late.”

He shifted the coil higher on his shoulder, already looking past her toward the fence. He sounded certain. Clarisse had heard that certainty in younger campers who’d picked up a spear once and thought the rest of training was paperwork.

She stepped into the path. “No one goes down there yet.”

Leandros stopped, but only because she was in the way.

The wind pushed cold rain across the yard. Under the porch roof, the work sheet shivered against its pin. Ianthe steadied the page with her palm, then looked from Leandros to the low path.

“What if he’s right about the water?” she asked.

“Then we’ll see it when we inspect.”

“And if that’s too late?”

“Then we’ll have to work faster.”

Ianthe’s jaw set. She turned back to the paper and began moving the time blocks. Clarisse watched her draw a new line through Eli’s name.

Eli saw it. “I didn’t agree to that.”

“I’m changing the plan because the weather changed,” Ianthe said.

“Then change the plan after you ask.”

“You said you could help.”

“I did.”

“With the sacks.”

“I said sacks.”

Clarisse heard herself take a breath through her nose. “The storm isn’t the only thing that changed the plan.”

Ianthe’s eyes flashed. “I know.”

“Do you?”

A gust shook the canvas hard enough to pull one corner down. Beckendorf called from the covered brace, “Nobody out here until I say the tie’s loose!”

Clarisse held her position. The lower path had gone dark with rain, but the shallow channel was not yet spilling. There was still time to inspect it from the dry side. There was not time to pretend a schedule could make the ground behave.



---

Clarisse followed Chiron into the Big House kitchen, where Percy and Annabeth had set a wooden crate between the table and the back door.

It held dry string, chalk, two wrapped bundles of canvas strips, and the labels Annabeth had copied from the winter list. The crate was not heavy, but rain was already blowing through the door whenever someone opened it.

“Can we put it in the pantry?” Percy asked.

“The shelves are full,” Annabeth said. She was checking the labels, her hair tied back with a blue cord. “The inside passage stays dry if we keep the door shut.”

“Then I’ll take it.”

“Not yet. Beckendorf said he was bringing the brace through.”

Percy rested both hands on the crate. “I can wait.”

Annabeth looked over the list. “Thank you.”

Clarisse watched the exchange. Percy had asked. Annabeth had answered. He did not lift the crate to prove he could.

“I could take one end,” she said. “If it’s staying here.”

“It is,” Annabeth said. “The other stack goes inside once the path is clear.”

“Which path?”

“Kitchen passage. Not the lower one.”

“Good.”

Percy glanced toward the back door. “You can tell I was about to ask if we should carry this through the yard.”

“You were looking at the rain.”

“I was looking at the shortest path.”

“Same thing.”

He smiled at her. “Do you want me to close the door?”

“Please.”

He swung it shut, and the room got quieter.

Clarisse set the damp schedule on the far end of the table. “Who’s taking the low path?”

“I’m checking it,” she said. “With Beckendorf. From the upper side.”

“Do you need me?”

“Not unless you want to copy labels indoors.”

Percy looked at Annabeth, then back at Clarisse. “I can copy.”

“I can copy my own labels,” Annabeth said.

“I know. I can copy the old ones.”

“Those are different sizes.”

“I can write small.”

Annabeth studied him for half a second. “All right. The old tags are on the shelf. Use the gray string.”

Percy stood and went for the shelf. He did not ask whether she needed him to rescue the whole list. Annabeth handed him a dry pencil and went back to her labels.

Clarisse felt a small, irritated envy—not of them, exactly. More of how ordinary the exchange looked. It took no speech. They moved around each other as if they expected to remain in the room after the disagreement.

“What?” Annabeth asked.

“Nothing.”

“That face isn’t nothing.”

“Your boyfriend’s learning to wait.”

Annabeth looked toward Percy, who had his head bent over the old tags. “He’s always known how.”

“Does he?”

“He forgets when he’s worried.” She smiled without looking away from the page. “He’s working on it.”

Percy glanced up. “I can hear you.”

“I know,” Annabeth said.

He returned to the labels.

Clarisse bent over the map Annabeth had brought from Cabin One. The low path and the canoe shed were drawn in dark pencil. The drainage cut appeared as a thin blue mark, with the lower ditch on the other side of the fence. There was a note in the margin from the last inspection: *Runoff unclear after hard rain; do not alter the board without Beckendorf.*

“Who wrote that?” Clarisse asked.

“Beckendorf,” Annabeth said. “After the first storm.”

“Did anyone check it?”

“Not yet. The ground stayed dry enough.”

“Then we don’t alter the board.”

“I know.”

Annabeth rolled the map halfway, keeping the note visible. “Ianthe’s schedule puts the fence check after the awning.”

“Of course it does.”

“She thinks the wind drops for an hour.”

“It might.”

“She has the weather part right. The ground part is still unknown.”

Clarisse tapped the blue line. “The board stays.”

“I’ll tell Chiron.”

“No. I’ll tell him. You have the labels.”

Annabeth gave her a look, neither offended nor grateful. “You’re deciding the jobs too.”

“I volunteered.”

“So did I.”

Clarisse did not answer. The point landed anyway. She took the map and walked it to Chiron, who had been measuring the rain against a line in the porch post.

“The map says leave the board,” she said.

“It does.”

“Then Leandros doesn’t touch it.”

“Tell him to stay with us until we inspect.”

“He won’t like that.”

“That isn’t a condition of the assignment.”

Chiron leaned over the map. “We’ll check from the fence side. If the water’s close to the shed, we move the dry line through the upper walkway. Nobody crosses the cut.”

“And if it’s not?”

“We still don’t change the channel in the rain.”

Clarisse nodded.

Behind her, Ianthe had come inside. She had pulled the work page free and was flattening it on the table. The blank column for consent was smudged from the rain. She was copying the list into a smaller notebook.

“What are you doing?” Clarisse asked.

“Making a backup.”

“Of what?”

“The schedule.”

“Why?”

“The page is wet.”

“The list is still readable.”

“I can see that.”

“Then why copy it?”

“Because it might not stay that way.”

Ianthe was not wrong. The paper had a dark crescent where the rain touched it. Her pencil marks had blurred near the time column.

“Make a copy of the tasks,” Clarisse said. “Not the times.”

“I need the times.”

“Not if the weather’s already changed them.”

“I can revise it.”

“After you ask the people doing the work.”

“I know how to ask.”

Clarisse watched her make the first new line. “Then do it.”

Ianthe lifted the pencil. “I’m trying to keep everyone from standing around waiting for someone else to decide.”

“That’s not what they’re doing.”

“You haven’t heard the whole camp complain about not knowing where to go.”

“I’ve heard them complain when somebody tells them where to go without asking.”

Ianthe set the pencil down harder than necessary. “So I shouldn’t make a plan.”

“You can make a plan.”

“Then what’s the problem?”

“Thinking the plan answers for people.”

Ianthe’s gaze flicked to Clarisse’s face. “You say that like you don’t tell people what to do.”

“I do.”

“At least you admit it.”

“Don’t confuse admitting a flaw with making it a virtue.”

Ianthe looked at the wet page. Her hands were very still.

Clarisse had no idea if the line helped. It sounded like the sort of thing Chiron said after the argument was over. She had been trying to say it while the storm was still happening, which was a different skill and one she did not have.

Leandros came in behind them. He set the coil on the floor beside the door.

“Water’s crossing the lower stones,” he said. “If we wait, it’ll go over the threshold.”

“Did you go down there?” Chiron asked.

“No. I can see from the covered corner.”

“Good. Stay here. We’ll look from the upper side.”

“If it reaches the shed, we lose the dry lines.”

“We may move them before that.”

“Then let me take them.”

“You can move them when Clarisse confirms the route.”

Leandros looked at the map. “The route is obvious.”

“The map says it isn’t,” Annabeth said.

His mouth tightened. “Maps don’t show the water.”

“Neither does a guess from the porch,” Clarisse said.

The rain clicked against the window. A gust pressed the glass inward with a quiet groan. Somewhere outside, a tie snapped loose and a camper called for Beckendorf.

Chiron straightened. “Clarisse. We’ll inspect from the covered side. Beckendorf stays with the canvas. Annabeth, keep the labels in the kitchen passage. Percy can help if he wants. Ianthe, take the task list and mark what we actually complete.”

Ianthe’s pencil was already in her hand. “And the schedule?”

“Set it aside.”

She stared at him.

“We have enough paper for what we know,” Chiron said. “We don’t have to pretend we know what the storm will do next.”

Ianthe slid the schedule into her folder. She did not crumple it or tear it. Clarisse noticed. She also noticed that Ianthe did not answer.

Outside, the canvas pulled again. Beckendorf’s voice came through the rain: “The knot’s holding. The upper brace isn’t.”

Clarisse reached for the map.



---

The upper path was a strip of packed dirt between the Big House porch and the equipment yard. Rain ran in narrow rivulets along its edges. Clarisse kept to the center, where the ground was still firm, and held the folded map beneath her jacket.

Beckendorf walked beside her with a short-handled shovel over one shoulder. Leandros came behind them, carrying the rope coil. Chiron stayed under the porch with the work list.

“Why do you need the rope?” Clarisse asked.

“For the line in the shed. If we move it, we need to keep it off the ground.”

“We’re not moving it until we inspect.”

“You said we might move it.”

“I said we might.”

Leandros glanced at the sky. “It’s going to get worse.”

“Probably.”

“Then we should do the useful thing while we can.”

“Useful to who?”

“To the camp.”

Clarisse stopped before the path bent toward the fence. The drainage cut was partly visible beyond the row of hazel bushes. The water moved fast along its shallow groove, but it remained inside the channel. The rough board at the edge still directed it toward the ditch.

Beckendorf crouched by the fence post. “The ground’s soft under the lower brace. We leave the board where it is.”

Leandros leaned over his shoulder. “It’s backing up.”

“Not here,” Beckendorf said. “The top’s moving. The lower end’s slow.”

“Because it’s blocked.”

“Because the ditch is carrying water.”

“The ditch is carrying it toward the shed.”

“No. It’s taking it past the shed.”

The difference was a few feet and a different angle. Clarisse could see the water split around a root and rejoin at a shallow stone lip. It wasn’t elegant. It wasn’t stuck.

“What if we pull the board back and open the low side?” Leandros asked.

“What if we don’t?” Clarisse said.

“Then the water keeps coming.”

“Where it’s supposed to.”

“You don’t know that.”

“Neither do you.”

Beckendorf scraped mud from the post with the edge of the shovel. “The board’s been there since the last hard rain. It held.”

“Then why is it still on the list?”

“Because we haven’t checked the ground under it.”

Leandros’s jaw set. “We can’t wait for perfect ground.”

“No one asked for perfect,” Clarisse said. “We’re checking before we move anything.”

She disliked how much she sounded like an instructor. She disliked that Leandros looked at her as if the word *check* meant *wait until somebody older says yes*. But the board had a job, and the rain was not a vote.

A gust dragged rain sideways. The path blurred, then cleared.

Clarisse took two steps toward the fence, stopping above the wet slope. From there she could see the lower ditch. Water moved through it and disappeared behind the far stand of pine. Nothing had reached the shed threshold.

“Not yet,” she said.

Leandros looked past her toward the canoe shed. “The line is still out.”

“Under the eaves.”

“And the wind is pushing rain inside.”

“Then we move it through the upper path when Chiron says.”

“By then the labels will be gone.”

“They’re under the roof.”

“The roof has a missing panel.”

He was right about that. The canoe shed’s open side let rain blow in, and one roof panel sat crooked above the rear rack. It was part of the maintenance list, not something to climb up and fix in a storm.

“Then we put a cover over the rack from the dry side,” Clarisse said. “Ground-level tie. Two people. No one moves the board.”

Leandros shifted the rope coil. “I can do the tie.”

“You can ask Beckendorf where he wants it.”

“You need to ask him too?”

“I need to know which post won’t pull loose.”

Beckendorf stood and looked at the two of them. “I’ll set the tie. Clarisse, watch the path. Leandros, keep the rope off the mud.”

It was simple. No title. No lead. No handoff from one of Poseidon’s sons. A task that started with the person who knew the post.

Leandros’s cheek moved. “Fine.”

Beckendorf walked back toward the shed. Clarisse stayed above the cut.

“Can you see the mark?” she called to him.

“Which one?”

“The shallow notch in the post.”

“Yeah.”

“Use that. Not the lower post.”

“I know.”

Leandros let out a breath through his nose. “You don’t trust anyone until they’ve done the thing exactly the way you would.”

Clarisse turned. “That’s not true.”

“Isn’t it?”

She could have said he had moved a paddle across a doorway, ignored Percy’s questions about rope, and spoken as if every correction were an insult. All of that would have been true. It also would have sounded like she had been keeping a list of his failures.

“I trust the work when I can see what it needs,” she said.

“That’s not the same as trusting people.”

“No.”

“Then we agree.”

“No. We agree those aren’t the same.”

For a moment, he seemed ready to argue. Then a new gust swept over the yard and made the canvas crack against the brace. Clarisse looked toward it.

Beckendorf had the tie looped around the upper post. The cover hung low enough to block the sideways rain but left the shed opening clear. He had done it without a ladder.

“Hold the end there,” he called to Leandros. “Keep it away from the channel.”

Leandros looked down at the rope in his hands, then at the water. “All right.”

Clarisse walked to the shed at the dry edge, careful not to step on the soft ground. A stack of repair canvas and the dry line sat on the rack. The labels were tied in a small bundle with gray string. One card had already curled from the damp.

“Bring the labels in first,” she said.

“Which way?” Eli asked from the porch.

“Upper path. The kitchen passage is dry.”

Ianthe came out behind him with her notebook open. “The schedule says the line comes first. The labels can go in the crate.”

“The labels are wet now,” Eli said.

“The line is under the roof.”

“The roof is open on that side.”

“I know.”

“Then why is the line first?”

“Because it’s longer to carry.”

“It’s lighter.”

Ianthe looked at the page. The labels were a small item; the dry line took two hands. She had set the order by time, not weight.

Clarisse waited to see whether Ianthe would change the task before checking who was carrying it. She did not.

“Eli,” Ianthe said, “can you take the labels?”

“I’m moving the sacks.”

“After that.”

“After the sacks I’m supposed to help with the brace.”

“I can do that,” Clarisse said.

“You’re already on the path.”

“I am.”

Ianthe looked annoyed, but she held the question. Eli considered the bundle, then the sacks.

“I can take the labels after I finish these,” he said. “If someone else takes the sacks inside.”

“Who?” Ianthe asked.

“Ask,” Clarisse said.

Ianthe shot her a look, then turned to Percy, who stood in the doorway with the crate. “Can you move the sacks into the pantry?”

“If Annabeth’s done with the table.”

“Not until the labels are on the dry side,” Annabeth answered from inside.

“Then I’ll wait,” Percy said.

Ianthe’s pencil paused above her notebook. “This is taking longer than the schedule.”

“The schedule doesn’t carry anything,” Clarisse said.

It sounded crueler than she intended. Ianthe’s eyes lifted, and Clarisse knew she had landed the phrase like a slap.

“Then tell me what would be useful,” Ianthe said.

“Get out of the doorway so Eli can finish the sacks.”

Ianthe stepped back. Eli picked up the first bundle and carried it inside.



---

By the time he returned, the first gust had pulled the water out of the cut.

Not all of it. The upper flow still followed the shallow groove. But the lower ditch had risen enough that water lapped over its far bank and spread across the grass.

Clarisse could hear the change before she saw it: a low rush beneath the rain, like a stream meeting a hollow log. She turned toward the fence.

“Chiron!”

He was already coming out with the map.

“Stay on the path,” he said. “Beckendorf, keep the cover tied. Clarisse, tell me where it’s crossing.”

“Not here yet. Lower ditch is filling.”

Leandros stepped closer to the cut. “If we open the board, it can go straight through.”

“Don’t touch it,” Beckendorf called.

“The water’s rising.”

“Don’t touch it.”

The urgency in Beckendorf’s voice made Clarisse’s shoulders tighten. No one was in immediate danger. The yard was wet, the ground slick, and the shed had an open side, but the stream remained below the path.

Leandros looked at the water, then at the board. He moved as if he had decided the answer before the rest of them finished speaking.

Clarisse caught his sleeve. “Stay.”

He pulled free. “I’m not going to stand here and watch it reach the shed.”

“You’re going to wait until we know where the ditch is taking it.”

“It’s taking it nowhere. It’s backing up.”

“Beckendorf said—”

“I heard him.”

He went down the first part of the slope, still above the cut. Clarisse followed, planting her boots where the packed dirt was firm. “Leandros!”

He reached the board and hooked the rope around its outer edge. The line ran back to his hands. The idea was practical: pull the board out from the mud without putting his fingers under it.

“Stop,” Chiron said.

Leandros pulled.

The board shifted half a foot. Water surged through the gap, carrying loose gravel and leaves. For one second the direction looked right—down, toward the ditch. Then the current hit the stone lip, split around the root, and cut sharply toward the shed.

Clarisse saw the mistake before Leandros did.

“Back!” she shouted.

The water ran along the path below the rack. A small crate of dry rope tags slid across the ground and caught against the shed step. The cover over the line held, but the gust pushed rain beneath it. Two coils unrolled toward the edge. The bottom of the canvas roll darkened as runoff pooled beneath the rack.

Beckendorf grabbed the cover tie before the wind could pull it down. Clarisse reached the crate first and lifted it onto the wooden bench.

“Move the dry line!” she called. “Upper path. Now.”

“I’ll do it,” Leandros said.

“No. You hold the cover where Beckendorf tells you.”

He started to answer. A heavier gust snapped the canvas against his shoulder.

“Now!”

Leandros braced his boots and grabbed the second tie. Beckendorf directed his hands: higher, then around the post, then hold. Leandros did each thing without adding another suggestion.

Clarisse and Eli carried the dry coils from the shed by the upper path. Eli moved fast but kept to the clear stones. At the kitchen passage, Percy caught the first coil and laid it on the top shelf. Annabeth held the door wide and moved the crate of labels out of the way.

The water reached the lower rack and stopped there. It did not rise above the wooden platform. The rope coils on top stayed dry. The two at the edge were damp, not ruined. The canvas would need to be lifted and dried. The tags inside the small crate had blurred where rain got through the corner.

No one was hurt.

That did not make the choice harmless.

Clarisse watched the runoff run toward the shed. The board lay at an angle in the cut, half-buried in mud. The water had cleared the original channel and found another way—straight across the low ground where the repair supplies sat.

“Did I tell you to move the board?” Chiron asked.

Leandros kept hold of the tie. “No.”

“Did Beckendorf?”

“No.”

“Did Clarisse?”

“No.”

“Then you moved it against the instruction.”

“It was blocking the water.”

“It was directing it away from the shed.”

“It looked like it was backing up.”

“It was moving through the cut.”

Leandros looked toward the flow. His shoulders were rigid beneath the soaked jacket. “I can put it back.”

“Not yet,” Chiron said. “The ground is soft. We’ll reset it when the rain eases and Beckendorf can inspect the footing.”

“The line’s wet now.”

“Some of it,” Clarisse said. “And the tags need to be rewritten.”

“I know.”

“You don’t get to decide that you fixed the problem before the work is counted.”

“I didn’t say I fixed it.”

“You were about to.”

He looked at her. “You think I wanted it to flood?”

“No. I think you wanted to be right fast enough that nobody could stop you.”

His face tightened. A line of rain ran down his cheek and disappeared at his collar. He turned away.

Clarisse did not follow. The water needed attention more than his expression did.

“Eli,” she said, “did the lower ditch connect to the side run?”

“It does after the stones,” he said. “That’s why it turned. The roots are farther out than they look.”

“Can you show me from the path?”

“Yeah.”

“Only from the path.”

“I know.”

He pointed without stepping down. Clarisse could see the thin channel past the last pine, where the ditch ran wide and shallow. The water was finding it now. It would ease if they left the board alone until the slope firmed.

“Good catch,” she said.

Eli shrugged, not seeking praise. “I’ve cleared leaves there before.”

Ianthe arrived carrying her notebook above her head. The rain had dotted the page, smearing the new times into gray streaks.

“The canvas was supposed to move after the lower path check,” she said. “I put it at eleven.”

“It’s ten,” Clarisse said.

“I know.”

“Your plan left it here until after an inspection you hadn’t done.”

“The weather window was supposed to last.”

“It didn’t.”

“I saw the wind change.”

“You changed the times. You didn’t move the supplies.”

“I thought we still had a window.”

“You used it to keep the order.”

Ianthe looked toward the rack. “I thought the eave would hold.”

“It usually does,” Beckendorf said. “Not when the rain comes sideways.”

“They should have been covered.”

“They were. The water came the other way.”

Ianthe looked at the crate on the bench. The paper labels had run where the rain touched them. Her notebook clung to her fingers.

“We can recopy those,” she said.

“We will.”

“I can do it.”

“After we know what got wet.”

“I can make another list.”

“After we count it.”

The words left Clarisse’s mouth before she could soften them. Ianthe’s face closed.

“Do you want me to do nothing?”

“No. I want you to wait until the work is clear.”

“You keep saying wait.”

“Because you keep moving before the rest of us know what we’re doing.”

Ianthe’s eyes flickered toward Leandros, who remained at the tie. “He moved the board.”

“He did.”

“And you’re talking to me.”

“Your schedule moved the supplies later. The water got there now. Both things matter.”

“I didn’t tell him to move the board.”

“No one said you did.”

“I’m not responsible for every decision he makes.”

“I didn’t say that either.”

A long burst of rain muffled the pause. The canvas held. Beckendorf kept both hands on the tie. Leandros’s grip had turned white around the rope.

Clarisse picked up the crate and passed it to Eli. “Take it inside. Keep it level.”

Ianthe stepped toward it, then stopped herself. She opened her mouth and shut it again.

Eli looked at her. “I’ve got it.”

“Okay,” she said.



---

The rain changed from slanting lines to a steady sheet.

Clarisse backed up the path, keeping the map tucked inside her jacket. The loose board had changed the water’s angle, but it had not broken the cut. If they moved it again while the ground was saturated, the second shift could open a wider gap.

“Leave it,” Chiron said. “We’re not making another change in the storm.”

Clarisse nodded. Beckendorf still held the canvas tie. Leandros had the second end. The dry line had been moved to the kitchen passage, but the coils closest to the shed were damp through. The small crate of tags sat on the bench, its top sheet mottled blue-gray where the ink had run.

“How much got wet?” Chiron asked.

“Two coils, maybe three,” Clarisse said. “The rest are inside.”

“And the canvas?”

“Bottom strip. It can dry.”

“Then we have a repair task, not an emergency.”

Leandros looked at the soaked rope. “The coil under the eave was supposed to move at eleven.”

Clarisse turned to Ianthe. “Is that what your schedule said?”

Ianthe’s fingers tightened around her notebook. “Yes.”

“What time is it?”

“Ten.”

“Then why was the move after the drain inspection?”

“Because the inspection was supposed to happen first. The weather window was clear until noon.”

“And when the rain started early?”

“I moved the times.”

“After the supplies were already in the low shed.”

“I didn’t know we would be inspecting this side before the move.”

“We told you the path would be inspected before anyone crossed it.”

“I heard that. I thought the crew could take the upper side.”

“Then the route needed to be on the schedule.”

“I didn’t know the route was the problem.”

“You didn’t ask.”

Ianthe’s stare held for a moment. Then she looked down at the map, where the upper path curled around the yard.

“No,” she said. “I didn’t.”

Clarisse had expected a defense. The answer made her feel more annoyed, not less. She had wanted Ianthe to hear the rule before it cost them anything. Now the rule was wet and the paper evidence lay on the bench.

Chiron took the notebook from Ianthe only after she let go of it. He turned to the morning plan. The marked times had blurred into each other, but the order was still visible: inspect the fence, then move the dry line, then check the canvas.

“You planned the inspection before the supplies,” he said.

“Yes.”

“What made you put it in that order?”

“The lower path was supposed to stay dry until the front reached camp. The line was under the eave. I thought the fence needed the first clear interval.”

“Did Beckendorf tell you the shed was safe in a hard rain?”

“No.”

“Did Clarisse?”

“No.”

“Did Eli?”

“I didn’t ask him about that.”

Chiron nodded. He did not raise his voice. “Then the schedule assumed something the people who use the path could have told you.”

Ianthe glanced at the water. “If I’d moved the line first, it would have been safe.”

“Maybe,” Chiron said. “That’s why you ask before deciding which task is first.”

The words sounded different from Clarisse’s. They always did when Chiron said them. It made Clarisse want to leave before anyone thought she had been the lesson.

Leandros released the tie. Beckendorf caught it and secured the loose end to the post.

“The water went where it had room,” Leandros said. “The board put it against the shed.”

“It put it toward the ditch,” Beckendorf said.

“The ditch is full.”

“The lower stretch is full. The side run isn’t.”

“You could have said that earlier.”

“I did.”

“You said leave it.”

“I said leave it because we hadn’t checked the footing.”

“You didn’t say the water would reach the rack.”

“I didn’t know the water would reach the rack. That’s why we inspect.”

Leandros folded his arms. The rain ran from his sleeves onto the packed path. “If there had been a better route, we wouldn’t be having this conversation.”

“There was a better route,” Clarisse said. “You could have waited.”

“That isn’t a route.”

“It is when the water’s moving.”

“Water doesn’t wait.”

“People do.”

He looked toward her. “Not if there’s work to do.”

Clarisse almost snapped back that the work had been there before he arrived. That he had not been the only one who knew what a wet line could do. That when he asked why they waited, he never asked who would clean up if his guess was wrong.

Instead she looked at the coils. “This work got wet because you moved the board. The schedule made the supplies late. Both are true.”

“I didn’t ask you to put it in the schedule.”

“You still chose to pull the board.”

“I thought it would help.”

“Thinking it would help is not checking.”

The sentence sounded too close to one from the morning. Clarisse hated repetition even when it was accurate.

Beckendorf came back to them, rubbing rain off his palms. “The cover held. The rack didn’t. We need to lift the bottom canvas and check the wood before it swells.”

“Can we do that now?” Clarisse asked.

“Not until the water drops.”

“Then we move inside.”

“Good.”

Leandros stepped toward the coils. “I can take the damp ones.”

“No,” Beckendorf said. “I’ll wrap them before we carry them.”

“I can tie them.”

“You can carry them when I say.”

The words were flat, not angry. Leandros looked like he might refuse just to show that the line was not a command he recognized. Then he glanced at the coils, the water, and the half-buried board.

“Tell me when,” he said.

Beckendorf reached for a wide strip of canvas. “When the path clears.”

Clarisse carried the box of tags inside. The wet sheets had bled through onto one another. She separated them carefully on a tray so the ink would not transfer to the dry tags. Some of the names were still legible. Others would need to be rewritten from the master list.

Annabeth moved the crate to a clear part of the kitchen table. Percy brought over a second tray. He did not touch the wet papers until Annabeth nodded toward the dry corner.

“Those three can be copied,” she said. “These two need the original list.”

“I’ll take the three.”

“Use the gray string.”

“I remember.”

“I know.”

He smiled and sat down.

Clarisse left the work to them. She had no patience for copying small letters. She could keep the porch and path clear, which was the work she had actually agreed to.

On the board outside, the plan sheet sagged under the rain. Chiron took it down and handed it to Ianthe.

“Keep this one,” he said. “Don’t rewrite it yet.”

“It’s wet.”

“It still tells us what we planned.”

“It doesn’t tell us what happened.”

“No. We’ll add that after we count the damage.”

Ianthe pressed her lips together. The page clung to her hand. Clarisse saw how much she wanted to throw the whole thing away and make a cleaner copy. The ugly paper was the evidence that the plan existed before the storm.

Ianthe folded it once, carefully, and placed it inside a dry folder.



---

The storm stayed hard enough to keep everyone under cover.

Clarisse took the porch post nearest the path and watched the water. It moved past the shed in a wavering sheet, then pulled back into the ditch. The flow had not reached the kitchen entrance. That was good. The part that bothered her was the way the board sat in the cut, loose at one end. A second surge could shift it again.

Chiron was speaking with Beckendorf just inside the doorway. They agreed to wait until the rain eased before checking the footing. No one would cross the low path in the meantime.

Ianthe stood at the work table with the wet schedule folded beside her. She had opened the dry notebook to a blank page. Every few moments, she looked toward the window and made a short mark.

“What are you recording?” Clarisse asked.

“Wind direction. Rain intensity. The minutes between gusts.”

“Why?”

“So I know when we can move the cover.”

“Beckendorf is watching the cover.”

“I know. This is separate.”

Ianthe’s pencil made another line. A ribbon tied to the outside railing snapped, then slackened.

“The gusts are farther apart,” she said. “There’s a small calm interval every four minutes.”

“Can you tell when it starts?”

“I can tell when the pressure drops.”

“Could you tell Beckendorf?”

“I was going to.”

“Then tell him.”

Ianthe looked at Clarisse, waiting for the correction. Clarisse had nothing else to add.

Ianthe crossed to the doorway. “Beckendorf, the gusts are coming about four minutes apart. The rope goes slack just before one.”

“Is it long enough to adjust the cover?”

“Maybe twenty seconds.”

“I can use that.”

“Do you want me to call it?”

“Please.”

Ianthe stepped onto the porch and watched the loose canvas. Her shoulders were squared against the wind. “Not yet. The tie is pulling.”

Beckendorf kept one hand near the post, not touching the knot. Leandros stood behind him with the coil held at his waist. He did not reach for it until Beckendorf asked.

The ribbon snapped three times, then went still for a breath.

“Now,” Ianthe said.

Beckendorf pulled the canvas higher and secured the tie through the notch. It took less than the calm interval. The loose edge stopped striking the brace.

“Good,” he said.

Ianthe gave a brief nod. She looked satisfied, but she did not turn the moment into an argument for restoring her schedule.

Clarisse stayed by the post. She could see the whole lower path from there, not just the part close to the shed. The rain darkened the grass, and the ditch ran full. Eli stood behind her with a bundle of dry sacks against his chest.

“Are those going inside?” she asked.

“When the door’s clear.”

“The kitchen passage is clear.”

“Percy’s moving the tags.”

“I’ll get them.”

“I can take them.”

“Your hands are full.”

“I can put the sacks down.”

Clarisse looked at him. “Do you want to?”

He glanced at the wet path, then back at the bundles. “No. I just don’t want you to tell me I can’t.”

She understood that kind of answer. It was how she had felt at thirteen whenever someone said they were keeping her safe.

“Then don’t put them down,” she said. “I’ll ask Percy.”

Eli shifted the sacks higher. “Okay.”

Clarisse went inside. Percy had finished copying the first set of labels. Annabeth was checking each one against the master list.

“Eli needs a second person to take the sacks through the kitchen passage,” Clarisse said.

“I can,” Percy answered.

“Ask him.”

Percy glanced toward the porch. “Eli, do you want help with the sacks?”

“Yeah. If you can take the front bundle.”

“I can.”

They lifted the sacks and carried them inside. Clarisse watched from the doorway. No one pushed the crate over the threshold or tried to save time by using the yard.

When she came back, Ianthe had drawn a new column in her notebook: *conditions*. She had not filled it yet.

“Are you changing the schedule?” Clarisse asked.

“Not yet. I’m writing down what was wrong with it.”

“Could you also write what was useful?”

Ianthe raised her eyes. “The order was wrong.”

“That doesn’t mean the weather notes were wrong.”

“They were right about the wind.”

“Then keep that part.”

Ianthe glanced at the wind ribbon. “It wasn’t enough.”

“It helped with the cover.”

“It helped Beckendorf.”

“It helped the cover.”

Ianthe looked at her notebook. “Those aren’t the same.”

“No,” Clarisse said. “They aren’t.”

The words turned back on her. She did not like it.

Ianthe wrote *wind interval useful; route assumptions unverified*. Her handwriting stayed level even though the page trembled.

Leandros moved at the edge of Clarisse’s vision. He started toward the door, then stopped when Beckendorf said, “Wait.”

He looked down at the rope coil and remained where he was.

Clarisse had not expected obedience to impress her. It did not. But she noticed it.



---

The rain eased after another half hour.

It did not stop. The drops thinned, and the wind dropped enough for Chiron to allow them to move the soaked coils. Beckendorf checked the post from the upper side, then the lower board from where he stood. He kept both feet on the packed path.

“Don’t pull the board back yet,” he said. “The dirt under it needs to firm.”

Clarisse agreed. “We can move the line first.”

“Two people at a time. Keep it above the ground.”

Leandros stepped forward. “I can take the far end.”

“Can you hold it without dragging it?”

“Yes.”

“Then take the far end. Don’t cross the cut.”

Leandros’s mouth twitched. He looked like he had another answer in him. Instead, he took his position where Beckendorf pointed.

Clarisse and Beckendorf lifted one wet coil onto a plank. Leandros kept the rope from slipping, moving his hands along the line as the weight shifted. His grip was sure. He could feel when the coil began to twist and corrected it before it slipped.

“Keep the loose end out of the mud,” Beckendorf said.

“I have it.”

“Up.”

Leandros raised it.

They carried the coil through the upper path, then set it on a dry rack inside the shed. Clarisse checked the tag. The ink had run, but the length number was still legible.

“Good,” Beckendorf said.

The second coil had taken more water. They unrolled it over a crossbar, so it would dry evenly. Leandros knew the right way to keep it from kinking. He showed Eli once, then stopped when Eli said he had it.

“Don’t pull against the coil,” Leandros said.

“I know.”

“It’ll tangle.”

“I said I know.”

Leandros let go. Eli worked the rope free on his own. It took longer, but it came away clean.

Clarisse counted the damaged tags. Three needed to be copied. The canvas strip would dry if they lifted it and spread it inside. The rack itself was still sound. The lower path was muddy but open enough for the next morning’s inspection from above.

Not destroyed. Not a disaster. A morning’s work made longer because no one had waited to check.

Chiron wrote the facts on the dry part of the task sheet: two damp coils, three tags to recopy, one canvas strip to dry, fence drainage still pending. He added that the lower board must stay until the ground inspection.

Leandros watched him write.

“You’re putting my name there?” he asked.

“I’m recording what happened,” Chiron said.

“That’s not what I asked.”

“Then ask the question you mean.”

Leandros looked at the board outside, half-hidden by rain. “Are you saying I’m responsible for all of it?”

“I’m saying you moved the board after you were told not to. The schedule also left supplies in the lower shed until after the inspection. Both facts belong in the record.”

“I didn’t write the schedule.”

“No,” Chiron said. “You made a separate choice.”

Clarisse leaned against the workbench. The wood was damp under her sleeve. “No one’s deciding what kind of person you are from this.”

Leandros looked at her. “You sound sure.”

“I’m sure I’m talking about one board and some wet rope.”

“And the trust?”

“That doesn’t get written on a repair list.”

He seemed to be waiting for her to say what it did get written on. Clarisse did not have an answer that would make this easy. She had not known him long enough to decide what one bad call meant. She had known enough to remember that he had made it after being corrected.

Leandros turned toward the dry coils. “We should hang these higher.”

“Beckendorf will show us where.”

“I know how to hang rope.”

“Then this should be fast.”

His eyes narrowed, but he picked up the coil and followed Beckendorf’s direction.

Clarisse watched him go. She had not asked him to apologize. She had not told him it was fine. Both things could wait.



---

The kitchen passage smelled of hot lentils and wet wool.

Annabeth had taken the smaller table near the stove and spread the labels there, each dry tag on one side and the rewrites on the other. Percy sat across from her with a bowl between his hands. He had changed out of his rain jacket and pulled the sleeves of his shirt to his wrists.

“Did all the labels need rewriting?” Clarisse asked.

“Three,” Annabeth said. “The rest just needed drying.”

Percy lifted a tag. “This one says ‘line, east rack, twenty feet.’”

“It says ‘line, east rack, twenty-seven feet.’”

“I read it upside down.”

“You’re holding it upside down.”

He turned it around. “That’s an important detail.”

Annabeth took the tag from him and laid it flat. “You can do the gray string instead.”

“Good. I’m excellent at string.”

Clarisse heard the quiet fondness in Annabeth’s voice. Percy reached for the bowl, and she nudged it farther from the tags before he could set his wet hand beside the paper. He moved the bowl without comment.

The soup pot was almost empty. Chiron had put bread on a plate under a clean towel. Nobody was calling it a victory meal. It was just what had been made before the rain came down.

Ianthe stood by the counter with her notebook open to the page where she had written the wind intervals. The old schedule remained folded inside the folder. She had not asked anyone to read the new notes.

“Did you bring a bowl?” Clarisse asked.

“I was going to wait.”

“For what?”

“Until the count is finished.”

“The count can wait.”

“I said I was going to wait.”

“You can eat before the list is done.”

Ianthe’s eyes moved to the table. “Is there enough?”

“Yes.”

“Then I’ll have a bowl.”

She filled it herself and took the chair farthest from the wet tags. Annabeth shifted the bread towel so the edge did not hang over the table.

Ianthe held her spoon without eating. “How much time did it cost?”

“An hour,” Chiron said. “More if the labels have to be matched again.”

“I can rewrite them tonight.”

“You can do it tomorrow after they dry.”

“I can do it tonight.”

“Why?”

“Because the storm damaged them.”

“Because you want to finish the schedule,” Clarisse said.

Ianthe’s gaze sharpened. “I want to fix what I did.”

“You don’t know what you did yet.”

“I know the times were wrong.”

“The times weren’t the only problem.”

Ianthe lowered her spoon. “I didn’t move the board.”

“No one said you did.”

“I left the line in the shed until after the drain check.”

“You planned that.”

“I thought it would be safe.”

“It wasn’t.”

“I know that now.”

Clarisse did not answer. The words were right, but they did not repay the work. She had seen people say the correct sentence too quickly and then expect everyone else to feel better.

Chiron sat at the end of the table. “Tomorrow’s inspection will tell us how to move the supplies and whether the board can be reset. We’ll dry the line tonight, not repair the fence in the dark.”

Ianthe picked up her spoon again. “And the schedule?”

“We’ll discuss it tomorrow,” he said.

“Do you want me to bring the notebook?”

“Yes. The weather notes may be useful.”

She nodded. That seemed to settle one small part of the day. It did not erase the bent plan or the wet tags.

Leandros entered with his jacket over one arm. He had washed mud from his hands but not from the cuffs. A strand of rope fiber clung to his sleeve.

“There’s room?” he asked.

Clarisse moved her bowl half an inch. “There’s a chair.”

He took the chair opposite her.

For a while, they ate without talking. Clarisse’s fingers were stiff around the spoon. The soup was thick and warm, full of lentils and pieces of carrot. Someone had salted it too much. She ate it anyway.



---

After the bowls were cleared, Chiron left the list on the side table with a stone on the corner.

The storm kept working at the roof. The noise had become ordinary enough that Clarisse could hear the little drips under the window and the quick scratch of Percy’s pencil as he finished the string tags. Annabeth checked each one and handed it back.

Ianthe sat with her notebook closed. The wet schedule was in the folder beside her.

“Can I ask Eli something?” she said.

Clarisse looked at her. “You can ask Eli.”

“I mean about the drainage.”

“Then ask him.”

Ianthe glanced toward the door. Eli had taken the last of the sacks inside and was hanging his jacket near the stove.

“Eli,” she said. “Can I ask you about the lower run?”

He looked up. “Sure.”

“Does it always go around the pine?”

“After the leaves clear out.”

“Who clears them?”

“Depends who’s on path duty.”

“Did you know the water would turn toward the shed?”

“I knew it could if the board moved.”

“You knew that today?”

“I knew it last storm.”

Ianthe looked at her notebook. “You didn’t put that on the schedule.”

“I didn’t know there was a schedule.”

She looked at him again. “You’re right.”

Eli shrugged. “I told Clarisse.”

“I didn’t ask you.”

“No.”

Clarisse watched Ianthe write it down. She did not write his name beside a task. She wrote *Eli: lower run turns after leaves clear; board stays until inspection*. Then she stopped.

“Is that right?” she asked.

“Mostly. It can turn if there’s a lot of rain. It’s why Beckendorf put that rock there.”

“Which rock?”

“The one under the lip.”

Ianthe looked up at Clarisse. “I didn’t see it.”

“You were looking at the water from above.”

“I thought the board was the problem.”

“It was part of the way the water moved,” Eli said. “It wasn’t blocking it.”

Ianthe’s pencil hovered, then she wrote *ask Beckendorf about rock before marking route.* She did not label it a priority.

Leandros sat with his hands on the table. He had not touched the wet tags or the notebook. His attention moved to Eli, then to the map, then back to Clarisse.

“At the marina,” he said, “the current pushed against the outer boards. You opened a gap so it went around the slip.”

“That’s a different kind of water,” Clarisse said.

“I know that now.”

“Do you?”

He met her gaze. “I know I moved the board. I thought the water would go the same way. It didn’t.”

The admission was small. It did not include an apology, and Clarisse did not ask for one.

“Tomorrow we’ll see how the ground sits,” she said. “Until then, no one moves it.”

“I heard.”

“You heard today too.”

“I know.”

He looked down at the table, at the small mark where his wet sleeve had left a crescent on the wood. He picked up the towel and wiped it away.

Clarisse had wanted him to hear her. She had also wanted to be the one who said what happened. Those were not the same thing, and she was not sure she knew how to hold both without turning the first into a command.

“Did you need the towel?” Leandros asked.

“No.”

He set it down within reach.

At the other end of the table, Percy handed Annabeth the last dry tag. She leaned over to check it. His shoulder touched hers. Neither moved away. They had not made the storm about their relationship or turned their teamwork into a show. It was a small thing to watch while the day settled.

Ianthe closed her notebook. “I’ll bring the weather notes to the inspection.”

“If Chiron asks,” Clarisse said.

“I know.”

“No names in the boxes until people answer.”

“I know that too.”

“Then show it tomorrow.”

Ianthe’s expression flickered, but she nodded. She did not argue that she had already learned it. She did not promise it would never happen again.

Chiron came back in and collected the task page. “The fence check is postponed until the ground firms. The line and canvas stay inside overnight. We’ll see about the board in the morning.”

“Who’s on it?” Ianthe asked.

“Tomorrow’s crew will decide after breakfast.”

Ianthe did not reach for her pencil.

Clarisse carried the empty bowls to the wash basin. Her hands were clean now, but they still smelled faintly of wet rope. In the yard, the lower ditch ran full and brown beneath the pines. The shifted board remained in place, half-buried and not fixed. It had not finished becoming a problem.

Neither had the storm.



---

By dawn, the rain had stopped.

The ditch ran high and brown, but the water no longer crossed the low path. Clarisse stood above the drainage cut with her hands in her jacket pockets. The air smelled like wet leaves and cold soil. The small board remained where Leandros had pulled it, held in place by mud and one stone.

Beckendorf knelt on the upper side, studying the worn edge. Eli pointed from the path to the pine roots.

“It turns there,” he said. “Then the roots split it.”

“Show me where it joins again,” Beckendorf said.

Eli pointed farther down the ditch.

Leandros arrived after them with a narrow plank under one arm. He stopped short of the cut.

“I thought we were resetting the board,” he said.

“We’re checking the footing first,” Clarisse answered.

“I can help.”

“Ask Beckendorf.”

Leandros turned to him. “Can I hold the marker while you check under the edge?”

Beckendorf looked at the plank, then at the ground. “You can hold the line from here. Don’t put weight on the bank.”

“All right.”

It was a small change in his voice. Clarisse heard it, though she wasn’t sure what he thought the difference meant. Asking didn’t make the answer yes. It did leave room for one.

Ianthe came down from the covered path with her notebook. She had left the schedule in the Big House. This page had only three notes: timing of the gusts, when the rain began, and where the water crossed.

“Do you want me to record the inspection?” she asked Chiron.

“After Beckendorf tells us what he finds.”

She nodded and waited. Clarisse had seen her struggle more with waiting than with any particular correction.

The lower side of the board had a soft hollow beneath it where the rain had pulled gravel away. The rock Eli had mentioned was still in place, but the soil around it had loosened.

“If we set the board back now, the next hard rain will push it over,” Beckendorf said. “We need gravel under the edge and the ditch cleared when the ground dries.”

“That’s not a big repair,” Ianthe said.

“No,” Clarisse said. “It’s still a repair.”

Ianthe wrote it down. “Temporary support today. Clear ditch later. Then test after rain.”

“After Beckendorf says the ground is ready,” Chiron added.

She changed the last line.

Leandros held the rope as Beckendorf measured the board’s old angle. The line stayed taut but did not pull against the bank.

“Can you keep it there?” Beckendorf asked.

“Yes.”

“Don’t adjust the tension.”

“I won’t.”

Clarisse watched the angle. The board sat awkwardly, one corner sunk in mud. It looked less useful than it had yesterday, though the water was moving through it again.

“I thought it would send the water straight down,” Leandros said.

“It did,” Eli answered. “Until it hit the roots.”

Leandros looked toward the pine. “I couldn’t see the turn.”

“No,” Clarisse said.

He held the rope steady. “I should’ve looked from this side.”

It was not an apology. It was not a promise. Clarisse let it be what it was.

“Next time, look from here,” she said.

“Next time, I’ll ask where the water goes.”

Eli shrugged. “You can ask me.”

“I will.”

The answer did not make Eli smile. It did not make him leave. He stayed at the edge and showed Beckendorf where the first line of gravel had washed out.

Clarisse watched Chiron make a note on the winter list. The schedule was still wrong. The storm was gone. The damaged tags had been copied, the wet coils were drying, and the fence drainage repair had not been completed.

Ianthe’s pencil paused above her notebook. “Do you want this listed as my planning error or as a weather delay?”

Clarisse glanced at her. “Write what happened.”

“I planned the move after the inspection because I thought the path would stay dry.”

“That’s what happened.”

“And Leandros changed the board.”

“That happened too.”

Ianthe wrote both. She left space beneath each note.

Clarisse pulled her hands from her pockets. “Don’t combine them into one reason.”

“I won’t.”

“Good.”

The line between them stayed slack enough to hold without drawing the board farther into the mud. Leandros kept his hands still until Beckendorf finished measuring.

When Chiron said they were done, no one called the work finished. They marked the ground for a later repair and carried the loose supplies back toward the dry shed. Clarisse followed with the new list.

At the shed, Beckendorf counted the coils again. “Two damp,” Beckendorf said. “The outer turns are wet through.”

“The outer length is wet through,” Leandros said.

“It can still be used after it dries,” Beckendorf told him. “We’ll check the fibers before we put it away.”

Leandros nodded. He carried the outer length to the rack, waited for Beckendorf to point to the higher bar, then laid it across without letting the rope scrape the wall.

Clarisse took the list from Chiron. The count matched. The tags were rewritten. The canvas was spread inside. The fence remained braced and the board remained unreset.

At the porch, Ianthe stopped beside her. “I’ll ask before I change the next order.”

“Ask the people on it.”

“Yes.”

“And leave room for the weather to change.”

“I’ll try.”

Clarisse nodded. She didn’t tell Ianthe that was enough. It wasn’t. But it was a start that had not been written in advance.
