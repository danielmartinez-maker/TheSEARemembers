## Bonus Interlude: The Sunday Afternoon

Sunday, the fifth of November, and the cold had broken for exactly one day.

The sun came down off the ridge and made the frost on the grass go silver, and the lake lay flat and bright under a sky that had decided, for once, to be kind, and the whole camp had the loosened feel of a place that had been holding its breath through October and was finally allowed to let it out.

Thalia Grace came up the west path in the middle of the afternoon with her hands in her pockets and her breath going up and the tenth lantern dark on its hook at the bottom of the hill because it was daytime and the lanterns only came on at night, and she did not go to the shooting line or the forge or the pavilion, because she had decided, somewhere on the path, that she was going to the camper's cabin at the end, and she had decided it was going to be in the daylight this time, with the sun on her back and the door wide open and nobody pretending it was anything other than what it was.

She had not told him she was coming. She had not told anybody. She had gotten up at seven the way she always did, and she had gone to the shooting line at eight the way she always did, and she had shot left-footed the way Chiron had told her to, and she had been worse at it, the way Chiron had said she would be, and she had not cared, because Rhys had been at the line with his bow unstrung and his hands in his pockets and his eyes on her, and she had caught him looking three times, and each time he had not looked away, which was the whole of the conversation.

At noon she had gone to the Big House and let Chiron look at the heel, and he had pressed it and she had not flinched, which was its own kind of flinching, and he had said, "Better. Another week left-footed and you'll be right," and she had said, "I'll be right at the hall Tuesday," and he had said, "You'll be right at the hall Tuesday and you'll be better by the winter," and that had been the whole of that conversation too.

The days between that first Monday night and the Sunday she had decided to claim had been longer than she expected. Not because she was miserable, which was how Percy had put it when he found her pacing in the rain on Thursday and then immediately claimed he had meant the weather. She simply had a new thing she wanted, and being required to go about her ordinary work while wanting it was an unfamiliar kind of inconvenience.

On Tuesday, the first time she wore the support wrap to the Tempest Hall, it caught under the edge of a bench and nearly sent her into Percy's stack of folded towels.

"Don't you dare," she said as he opened his mouth.

"I hadn't decided what I was going to say."

"You were going to say something about flying."

"I was thinking about the towels."

"You've never thought about towels in your life."

"I've had a complicated relationship with towels since Atlantis."

Annabeth, who had been measuring the distance between two lines of floor marks, looked up just long enough to say, "If you knock over those towels, you're folding them again. Both of you."

That had been the end of it. Thalia spent most of the evening on the near side of the pool, where the spectators sat, giving advice to campers who had come expecting to watch rather than participate. Rhys stayed for the whole session, but he did not sit beside her. He took three younger campers through the safety markers, listened when Clarisse challenged a rule, and stopped one of the Apollo boys from treating the shallow end as a competition.

She found herself noticing all sorts of things about him she had failed to notice at the archery range. He could be patient with a nervous child and thoroughly impatient with somebody showing off. He remembered everyone's name but lost his own pencil four times. When the swimmers left, he helped Silena haul a heavy crate out of a passage rather than wait for somebody to ask. When he came over to say goodbye, his attention stayed on Thalia's face despite the noise behind them.

"You have an opinion," he said.

"I have several. Your cousin can't follow a line on the floor."

"He follows it. He thinks the rules are wrong."

"He's going to break his head."

"I told him that."

"What did he say?"

"He asked whether I'd broken mine."

Thalia snorted. "Fair."

Rhys looked down at the wrap for no longer than an instant. "How was the foot?"

"Still attached."

"That's a start."

"Don't sound so impressed."

"I was impressed by the part where you helped somebody else shoot without insisting you should be shooting."

She could have taken that as a compliment. Instead she heard a report on her behavior, delivered in a voice gentle enough to be difficult to fight.

"Is everyone going to keep a ledger on me?"

He stopped smiling. "No. I'm not your keeper, Thalia."

"Good."

"And if you want me to stop asking, tell me that."

There it was: an answer that left the choice with her rather than doing the work of making it. She was still annoyed when he went back to collecting equipment. She was also aware that she had been unfair to him.

On Wednesday she sought him out at the target shed and said so. It took roughly fifteen seconds, which felt excessive.

He accepted the apology. Then he asked whether she would help him rebuild the smallest beginner's bow before the next lesson, and she accepted in turn. Neither of them attempted to make the exchange ceremonial. They spent the next hour arguing over the straightness of the replacement limb, with a ruler between them and a small pile of rejected shavings beside the bench.

It was a much better afternoon than Tuesday had been.

---

Thursday brought rain and a letter.

The letter was from Beryl, folded into a narrow envelope that had gone soft at one corner. Thalia recognized the handwriting before she looked at the name. She waited until after lunch to open it, then sat at the end of the Cabin One table while Annabeth fitted numbered slips of paper into a small tin box.

Beryl said the diner had asked her to cover extra mornings. She said she was trying to do better. She said she missed her girls. Between two sentences about coffee and bus schedules, she had written that she hoped Annabeth was listening to her big sister.

Thalia read that sentence three times.

Annabeth didn't ask what the letter said. She had learned not to do that. She kept arranging the slips by number, pressing the curled corners flat with the pad of her thumb.

"She says the diner gave her more shifts," Thalia said.

"That's good."

"Maybe."

Annabeth looked up. "Do you want me to read the rest?"

"You can if you want."

"I want to know what she said. That isn't the same thing as wanting to answer."

Thalia passed her the page.

Her sister read slowly. By the time she got to the end, her expression had changed so little that another person might have missed it. Thalia knew the difference between concentration and the kind of composure Annabeth used when she was trying not to give somebody else the satisfaction of seeing her upset.

"She still thinks I do everything you tell me," Annabeth said.

"She always liked that version."

"Did you?"

Thalia considered lying. "Sometimes. Especially when the alternative involved you climbing onto something tall."

Annabeth put the letter on the table. "I listen when you're right. I just don't want to have to pretend you're right first."

They were quiet a while. Outside, water ran down the glass and gathered on the sill where the old frame never quite shut properly.

"I don't want to be her," Thalia said.

"You aren't."

"That's not something you can settle with one sentence."

"I wasn't trying to settle it. I was answering."

Thalia watched her put the letter back into its envelope. The family they had arrived in was full of agreements that neither sister had ever made, habits that belonged to years Annabeth could not completely remember, and expectations that Thalia had carried long enough to mistake for herself. Even on good afternoons, they bumped against all of it.

"There's somebody I've been spending time with," Thalia said.

Annabeth looked at her over the envelope. "Rhys?"

"You knew?"

"I have eyes."

"Your eyes could mind their own business."

"They do. Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"Do you want me to ask questions, or do you want me to keep pretending I don't know?"

Thalia had expected teasing. She wasn't sure what to do with the offer of privacy.

"Keep pretending for now," she said. "I might change my mind."

"Fine."

Annabeth slid the envelope back across the table. She did not ask for a name, a promise, or a detailed explanation of the last Monday night. Instead she opened the tin box again and began counting the slips, and Thalia sat with her until the rain eased enough that they could go to supper without getting soaked.

Thursday evening, the rain was still running down the window of Rhys's cabin when Thalia appeared at his door.

She had said she might come. He had said he'd be free after six. Neither of them had clarified the part that followed, which meant she had spent the last quarter hour deciding whether she was late to an appointment that wasn't an appointment.

He opened the door before she could work out whether knocking would make the whole business feel ridiculous.

"You look soaked."

"Your powers of observation are improving."

"Come inside."

"You could have said that first."

He stepped aside. She crossed the threshold and took off her coat, sending a fan of rainwater onto the mat. The cabin smelled of tea and wool drying near the stove. He had swept the floor. She noticed that he had also moved a wooden chair nearer the fire and put an extra cup on the table.

"You prepared," she said.

"I boiled water."

"You moved furniture."

"An elaborate courtship ritual."

"Should I be impressed?"

"It depends on what you were expecting."

She caught the edge of his smile and felt something inside her ease. The trouble with the letter from Beryl wasn't that it had ruined the day. It had made the day feel borrowed, like the smallest pleasant thing might come with some distant adult standing outside to announce that she'd misunderstood it. She didn't want to bring that feeling into this room. She also didn't know how to leave it outside.

Rhys took the wet coat from her hand. "Tea?"

"Not yet."

He looked at her. "All right."

She crossed the small space between them and kissed him. She had thought about it on the walk uphill; she had decided to do it before she arrived, and the decision was enough to make her feel brave until the moment their mouths met and bravery stopped having anything to do with it. He put the coat down over the back of the chair without breaking away. Her hands found his shoulders. He was warm beneath the work shirt, and she was suddenly furious that she had wasted fifteen minutes on the path wondering whether he wanted her here.

He drew back just enough to speak. "Long day?"

"Terrible."

"Do you want to talk about it?"

"No."

"Do you want me to stop asking questions?"

She considered. "Just the boring ones."

He smiled, and she kissed him again before he could enjoy the joke. The rain kept falling behind the window. It could rain for a week. She had no particular objection to staying where she was.

She'd always considered herself a practical person. Her practicality generally involved lifting heavy objects, knowing where to stand in a fight and recognizing when somebody was bluffing. It had not prepared her for the sheer nuisance of discovering that a person could occupy her thoughts while she was doing something completely unrelated. Rhys had managed it with very little effort.

He touched the damp ends of her hair. "You're freezing."

"I'm not."

"Your hands are cold."

"They're usually cold."

"These are ambitious."

"Calder, if you keep talking about the weather, I will go home."

He caught her hands between his and rubbed warmth into them. "Then I'll have to be interesting."

She watched him work his thumbs over her fingers and felt the ridiculous urge to say something soft. Instead she lifted their joined hands and kissed the inside of his wrist. His expression changed immediately. It wasn't his practiced smile from the range or the grin he used when he'd won an argument. She liked the surprise in it.

"Interesting enough?" she asked.

"That was unfair."

"Good."

He drew her closer. His mouth met hers with enough certainty that she forgot the coat dripping on the chair, forgot that the tea would get cold, forgot she had spent the afternoon angry at the handwriting in a letter. Then he pulled back and waited, because she had made a small involuntary sound when her heel shifted.

"Foot?"

"A little."

"We can sit."

"I was doing something."

"I noticed."

She looked toward the narrow cot and back at him. "Sitting doesn't sound like a terrible idea."

He didn't give her time to reconsider. They moved together, laughing at the awkwardness of the chair between them, until the blanket caught beneath Thalia's knee and sent them both into a tangle of wool and elbows. He managed to catch the cup before it fell off the bedside shelf. She laughed harder than the near disaster deserved.

"Very impressive," she said.

"I'm usually more graceful."

"Liar."

"Occasionally."

He set the cup aside, and she moved close again. This time she took care with her bad foot, arranging herself comfortably instead of treating pain as an insult to be conquered. He accepted the adjustment without commentary. That was becoming one of the things she wanted most from him, even when she was still learning how to ask.

The laughter quieted. They kissed until neither seemed interested in testing the other's wit, then settled beneath the blanket as the rain thickened against the glass. She wasn't sure when she'd begun to feel at ease here. Perhaps when he'd stopped trying to guess what she wanted and learned to let her tell him. Perhaps when she'd stopped preparing to take every touch as a claim.

The evening drew in around them. When the lamp needed turning down, Rhys reached for it, and she stopped him with a hand against his cheek.

"Leave it for a moment," she said.

He did. She wanted to see his face. In the soft light he looked younger, almost shy, the careful confidence of the instructor replaced by something much less defended. She kissed him again. This time she wasn't hurrying.

They had an entire room, a closed door and the strange luxury of not having to prove themselves to anybody. She chose to stay close, and he chose it with her.

Much later, when the rain had eased and the room had become quiet, he asked whether she'd like a fresh cup of tea.

"Are you trying to send me away?" she asked from beneath the blanket.

"I'm trying to prevent you from dying of cold."

"I'm doing very well."

"You're wearing my socks."

"They were available."

"Yes, mysteriously."

She stretched one foot toward the stove, leaving the injured one supported by the pillow, and examined the enormous striped sock with distaste.

"These are appalling."

"My mother made them."

"Then they're extremely good socks."

He laughed. "I'll tell her you said so."

"You will do no such thing."

He got up to refill the kettle, and she watched him cross the room. There was something distinctly satisfying about the sight of him going about a ridiculous household task when they'd spent so much of the evening wrapped up in each other. He was not less desirable with a kettle in his hand. Quite the opposite, which was an inconvenient piece of information about herself.

"Stop looking at me like that," he said, not turning around.

"Like what?"

"Like you're considering another bad decision."

She smiled into the blanket. "Can't imagine where you'd get that idea."

He brought two cups over and sat beside her. She shifted enough to give him room without losing the warmth he'd left behind.

"Can I ask about your day now?" he said.

"You can."

"Will you answer?"

"Possibly."

She considered telling him about the letter. She could have said her mother's name and invited him into a story that was still difficult even when she knew every part of it. He would have listened. But the story belonged to her, and tonight she didn't want to hand it over simply because they were becoming close.

"Annabeth was difficult," she said instead.

"That sounds like a family talent."

"She's getting too good at reading me."

"Terrifying."

"She thinks she knows what's good for me."

"And does she?"

"Sometimes. Which is worse."

Rhys rested his hand beside hers rather than over it. When she took it, he smiled into his cup.

"Does she know about us?"

"She knows I'm spending time with you."

"Is that a problem?"

"Not for her."

"And you?"

Thalia looked around the cabin. His spare shirt hung over a chair. The blue mended glove lay near the tools. Her wet coat was drying by the stove, unmistakable proof that she'd chosen this place over an evening alone. She wanted him. She liked being with him. She liked that there was no answer he could demand she hadn't already chosen to give.

"I haven't decided how much of this I want everybody to know," she said.

"Then we don't announce anything."

"You're being suspiciously reasonable."

"Only when I've been properly motivated."

She leaned over to kiss him and tasted the strong tea he had insisted on making. He put the cup down very carefully before returning the kiss.

Before she went, Rhys stopped her at the door with the striped socks in his hand.

"You forgot these."

"I didn't forget them."

"They're still on your feet."

"Then the system is working."

"Thalia."

She looked up at him, trying not to smile. He had a faint crease between his eyebrows that appeared whenever he was concentrating, whether on an arrowhead or on figuring out what to say to her.

"They're yours," he said.

"I know."

"Do you want to keep them?"

She was unreasonably pleased by the question. He could have told her to take them; he could have made a joke and turned the moment into one more example of how he knew what she wanted. Instead he was waiting.

"Yes," she said. "For a while."

"Then keep them."

She stepped closer and kissed him, slowly enough that neither of them could mistake it for a reward given in passing. When she drew back, he was still looking at her.

"Are you always going to be this difficult to leave?" she asked.

"Only when you keep giving me good reasons to ask you to stay."

"That's a very dangerous answer."

"I've made peace with it."

Thalia touched the open collar of his shirt, straightening it without thinking. He glanced down at her hand and then back at her face.

"I want to see you again," she said.

"You will."

"That's not quite what I meant."

His smile gentled. "I want that too."

She nodded once, satisfied. The straightforward answer felt better than any elaborate promise. She gave him one last kiss, slipped her boots over the borrowed socks, and took her coat from the hook. He watched her, not trying to slow her down. She liked that almost as much as she liked being asked to stay.


By the time she left, the rain had stopped. Her coat was mostly dry, and she had borrowed the striped socks with the explicit promise that she would return them. She doubted she would keep that particular promise. Rhys walked her to the threshold and rested one hand on the doorframe.

"Sunday?" he asked.

"Maybe."

"You've got a fierce commitment to uncertainty."

"I'm full of surprises."

"Good ones, sometimes."

She stepped up to kiss him once more. "Don't get ahead of yourself."

He smiled as she turned away. It was only halfway down the path that she realized she had spent the evening wanting to be there without once pretending the reason was a broken piece of equipment. That was progress. She would never say so aloud.

---

On Friday the sky cleared. Thalia and Annabeth went down to the work shed with their boots muddy and argued about drainage channels for forty minutes. It was a useful argument. Thalia had spent half her life trying to make sure her sister survived. Annabeth had spent a good part of this one trying to make sure that surviving did not become the whole definition of being alive. They could talk about drainage because the other conversation had stopped being an emergency.

---

The following week brought no spectacular change. There were mornings when Thalia caught Rhys looking at her from the opposite end of the range and evenings when he was too busy to come by Cabin One. They exchanged a few words over equipment, sometimes a touch that lasted only a second when no one was watching. She discovered that having a private life with somebody did not require every meeting to be private. It could be carried into an ordinary day in the smallest ways: knowing what a half smile meant, hearing him call her by name, remembering where his hand had rested against hers when they had been alone. She missed him on the days they barely spoke. She hated how pleased she was when he found time to walk beside her toward supper. Neither feeling required an immediate explanation, and neither turned her into somebody else.

By the following Saturday, the hall schedule had acquired six new names. Three were Apollo campers, two belonged to children who had never been in the water after dark, and the sixth was Clarisse, written in such heavy pencil that it left an indentation through the paper.

Thalia found Rhys checking the new list outside the pavilion.

"You're recruiting," she said.

"Trying to. Winter gets long."

"People can entertain themselves."

"Some can. I've seen what happens when the younger ones spend a week arguing about whose bunk is warmer."

She leaned against the post beside him. "You've been doing this longer than I thought."

"Coming back for winter? A few seasons. Organizing things? Only when someone else is tired of doing it."

"Why?"

"My mother works shifts. I was the oldest kid home. If I wanted something to happen, I usually had to put the chairs out myself."

It was the first time he had offered a piece of his life outside camp without using it to make a point about hers. Thalia turned her head to look at him.

"You don't always look like somebody who's had to put chairs out."

"That's because I am very good at putting them out."

"What happens when winter's over?"

"I'll go back to help her for a while. I haven't decided what comes next."

She did not ask whether that decision included her. The question would have been too easy to turn into a trap for both of them. Instead she took the paper, looked at the empty volunteer slots, and wrote her name beside the next beginners' session.

"You could have asked," Rhys said.

"You were going to."

"I was considering it."

"And I saved you the trouble."

He folded the list and tucked it under his arm. "The first sunny afternoon all week is tomorrow. Are you planning to use it?"

"I haven't decided."

"Very mysterious."

"I have an image to protect."

He laughed and went inside to collect the chairs. She watched him for a moment and then followed, because he was right about one thing: somebody had to put them out.


By Saturday evening the sky had cleared completely. The temperature had fallen with it, and the windows of the north cabin were beginning to frost along the edges when Thalia reached the porch.

Rhys was outside carrying a bucket of ashes toward the fire pit. He spotted her from halfway down the path.

"You're back."

"I'm allowed to walk along a public road."

"Is that what this is?"

"The north road is public to campers."

"Very legalistic."

She watched him empty the ashes, then return with the bucket. Her heel was better than it had been, and she'd stopped needing to plan each step. She still resented the wrap. It made her feel as though her own body had joined the list of people who enjoyed telling her what to do.

Rhys set the bucket beside the door. "Want to come in?"

She wanted that very much. She had spent a significant portion of the day trying not to picture this room: the narrow cot, the spare blanket, the workbench he always left cluttered no matter how often he cleared it. The picture arrived anyway, especially during the moments when her attention was supposed to be elsewhere.

"Maybe," she said.

He raised an eyebrow. "You're getting very committed to that word."

"I like a versatile answer."

"Does it mean yes?"

"Not always."

"Then I'll wait."

He leaned against the doorframe. She moved close enough to stand beside him, shoulder to shoulder, facing the black path down toward the lake. For a while neither spoke. The cold made the tips of her ears sting. Rhys had left his shirt collar open despite the temperature, which was a foolish choice that distracted her more than she'd have liked.

"I have a question," he said.

"You're full of them."

"Do you actually enjoy making me guess?"

Thalia stared into the dark. She could easily make a joke. This time she was too interested in the answer he might give if she didn't.

"Sometimes," she said. "Sometimes I'm waiting to see if you'll decide you want me here badly enough to say it."

"I've said it."

"Once."

"More than once."

"I might have missed a few."

He turned to face her. "I want you here, Thalia. Not because you need a reason to visit. Not because I need help with a bow. I like it when you come around, even if you're only stopping by to insult my coffee."

"Your coffee deserves it."

"Absolutely."

"Then why ask the question?"

"Because if you don't want to come in, I don't want you thinking you have to. And if you do, I'd rather you tell me than spend an hour making both of us guess."

She looked at him. There it was again, that irritating clarity of his. Her sister could be equally direct, but Annabeth's directness had an entirely different edge. Rhys spoke as though the answer genuinely belonged to her. It made her want to say things she usually kept to herself.

"I like being here," she said.

"Good."

"And I like knowing you want me."

"Also good."

"And I don't know what I'm doing half the time."

"That makes two of us."

She turned toward him. "You don't act like it."

"Should I start?"

"Maybe. For variety."

He stepped closer and paused. She put a hand against his chest, feeling the warmth through his shirt. His heart was beating quickly enough that she knew he'd been telling the truth.

"I can hear you thinking," he said.

"Then you're in trouble."

"Probably."

She drew him down to kiss her. They were on the porch, in a place where anybody turning off the north path might see them. For a moment she felt the old instinct to check whether anyone was watching. She ignored it, but she was not entirely comfortable with the ignoring. She didn't want a performance. She wanted him.

Rhys seemed to understand the difference. He kissed her slowly, one hand resting near her waist, and when she drew back he didn't pull her close again to prove a point.

"Inside?" he asked.

She looked at the dark path. "For a little while."

He opened the door. The cabin was warmer than the porch, and she was relieved to be able to take off her coat. He hung it beside his, then went to turn up the lamp.

"Leave it," she said.

He stopped. "Too bright?"

"Not bright enough."

He turned the wick until the room filled with a warmer light. She watched him do it and recognized that she had another answer for the question she hadn't been brave enough to ask earlier. She wanted him to look at her. She wanted to see him looking, and she wanted the choice to be hers.

He came back toward her. She caught the front of his shirt before he could say anything. Their kiss began with a smile and became serious before either of them had the chance to make another joke. When he drew back she followed, annoyed at the small distance.

"You're impatient," he said.

"You've only just noticed?"

"No. I'm starting to enjoy it."

She kissed him again, and this time she took her time. She hadn't known how much she'd like learning the expressions he made when he was trying not to laugh, or the way his confidence could give way to something unexpectedly tentative when he asked for an answer and actually waited for it.

They settled on the cot with a blanket over their knees. He placed the second pillow by her injured foot before she had to ask. For once she didn't make a comment about it. She sat facing him, their knees touching, and watched the little gold circle of lamplight on the sleeve of his shirt.

"Is this awkward?" she asked.

"Which part?"

"The part where I'm sitting here trying to think of something interesting to say."

"I'm finding it fascinating."

"You're impossible."

"Sometimes we could just sit here."

"That sounds dangerously domestic."

"Don't tell anyone."

She laughed and leaned into him. He put an arm around her, and the ease of it surprised her. She'd been so focused on the moments when they kissed that she'd forgotten what it might feel like to simply stay. To talk about nothing for a while. To lean on someone because she wanted to.

He rested his cheek against her hair. "You're quiet."

"I'm thinking."

"Should I be worried?"

"Not yet."

They sat together as the stove cooled. After a while he asked if she wanted more tea. She shook her head. He brushed a strand of hair back from her face, and she turned toward him and kissed him again. The kiss made her forget what she'd been about to say, which was either a sign of disaster or a genuine improvement in her evening.

When she pulled away, he looked as if he wanted to ask another question. She forestalled it.

"I like this," she said.

He smiled. "I was going to say the same thing."

"Don't get used to agreeing with me."

"I'll make a special effort."

She traced the line of his jaw with her thumb. He was not a prize she'd won by being difficult, and she was not a problem for him to solve by being patient. They were two people who had made a choice and were still discovering how to live with it. The fact that they wanted each other made the learning rather more enjoyable.

At nine, Thalia checked the clock and groaned.

"What's wrong?"

"I told Annabeth I'd be back before she went to sleep."

"Did you tell her where you were going?"

"No."

"Will she be angry?"

"Probably not. She'll be unbearably reasonable, which is worse."

Rhys rose to fetch her coat. She caught his hand before he could reach the hook.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"What about it?"

She thought about the forecast, the brief window of sunlight, the work she could leave for another day. There was no reason she couldn't choose an afternoon for herself.

"I might come by."

He smiled but didn't press. "I'd like that."

"I know."

"Would you like me to pretend to be surprised?"

"At least make an effort."

She stood and pulled on the coat. He opened the door, and the cold air came in around their feet. She turned and kissed him once, then again because the first kiss had been unnecessarily short.

"Good night, Calder."

"Good night, Grace."

She went down the path slowly, taking care on the frosty gravel. The lamps beyond the cedar brake were burning. She could feel the warmth of his hands long after she'd left the porch, and by the time she reached the fork she had made up her mind about Sunday.

She was going to surprise him.



She had tried to keep her mind on anything except the last time she had been alone with him, and the effort had made her disagreeable all morning.

There were details she hadn't meant to remember so clearly. Not the heroic ones people would tell around a campfire. The way he'd asked her to move a pile of bowstrings because she'd put her elbow on them without thinking. The little sound of recognition when she'd laughed at something he said instead of finding an argument. How different it had felt to stand beside him without a target between them, with neither a score nor an audience to tell her what she was supposed to do next.

The nights in between had made her miss his company in a way she did not particularly enjoy admitting. She liked the impatient, ordinary desire to see him again. She didn't like how easily it could make her read a perfectly innocent look across a shooting line as a private invitation.

Now she was acting on one deliberately. She didn't intend to spend the afternoon talking about a target shed. She didn't intend to explain away her visit as concern for her heel. She wanted to be alone with him, to kiss him until they both forgot to make jokes, and then to find out whether they were capable of sharing a quiet hour without inventing another excuse.



After Chiron's examination, she had meant to go directly to the north path. Instead she went back to Cabin One and stood in front of the open trunk at the foot of her bed, looking at her own clothes as though she'd never seen them before.

It was absurd.

She owned three perfectly good shirts. Two were too warm for the afternoon, and the third was the one she'd worn to the range that morning. She could wear the third. Rhys wouldn't care. The problem was that she cared, for reasons she was unwilling to examine too closely.

Annabeth was nowhere in sight. Her notebook lay open on the table beside a ruler and a piece of folded tracing paper. On the margin of a drawing she'd written a reminder to check the east wall before frost set in. She laughed. He brushed his lips against her forehead, then her cheek, and she caught his face between both hands before he could turn the gesture into teasing. The next kiss was slower. The kind that had nowhere urgent to go. Her shoulders gradually stopped holding themselves so stiffly, and he seemed to notice without congratulating her for it.

At last the cold reached them from the glass. Rhys took her hand and drew her toward the cot, where a blanket lay folded along the foot. Thalia sat first, placing the injured heel where it wouldn't take her weight. He settled beside her. They were close enough that their knees touched.

"Are we going to talk about tomorrow?" he asked.

"Do we have to?"

"Not if you don't want to."

"Then no."

"All right."

She shifted toward him and rested her head briefly on his shoulder. He placed his arm around her without ceremony. She could tell he was resisting the temptation to ask why she had gone quiet again. He was learning that silence didn't always need to be solved.

After a moment she lifted her head. "I want this to be the same tomorrow."

He considered. "It won't be exactly the same."

"Unhelpful."

"We'll be outside. There'll be people to teach. You'll be furious at somebody for moving a target."

"I will be furious at you."

"Probably."

"But I don't want you acting as if today didn't happen."

"I won't."

She studied his face for a trace of insincerity. There wasn't one. That was inconvenient. It left her with nothing to fight.

"Good," she said.

He touched a loose strand of hair beside her temple. "Will you?"

"Will I what?"

"Act as if it didn't happen?"

She looked at him for several seconds, long enough that his expression became uncertain. He was capable of asking difficult questions without sounding demanding, but she could see the question mattered to him.

"No," she said. "Not if I can help it."

"You don't have to tell everybody."

"I know."

"And you don't have to pretend I’m a stranger."

"Nobody would believe it. You've spent six weeks ruining my archery lessons."

He laughed. She drew closer and kissed him again, lingering this time because it pleased her to see how quickly his expression changed when she did. It was flattering to want someone and discover he wanted her just as plainly, without either of them needing to describe it to an audience.

They stayed that way as the light dwindled. Sometimes they talked. Sometimes she interrupted him to kiss him. Once he reached for the blanket and she caught his wrist, only to realize he was trying to keep her bad foot warm. She let him, then offered him the dignified privilege of not mentioning it.

Later, she changed into the spare shirt he had left by the cot. He turned away while she did, although she told him he was being absurdly formal about a person who had spent the afternoon kissing him. He answered that she could call him absurd if she liked. She did, several times, before settling beneath the quilt and drawing him closer.

The room was familiar in a new way now. Not because she had memorized every object, but because she knew she could say what she wanted there without somebody treating the admission as weakness. She could reach for him without an excuse. She could ask him to wait. She could change her mind and he would change with her.

Their closeness made the night pass without ceremony. The lamp burned low. She kept his hand in hers and allowed herself the rare pleasure of staying exactly where she wished to be.

At some point, when she had stopped paying attention to the clock, Rhys shifted to put another piece of wood on the fire. She caught his sleeve.

"Wait."

"The stove needs feeding."

"In a minute."

He looked at her, then smiled. "You keep telling me that."

"And you keep listening."

"Somebody has to."

She pulled him down beside her and kissed him once more. The wood could wait. So could the morning. For this small part of the night, she wanted to be greedy with time, and she could see from the way he smiled that he was happy to let her.




---

She woke at four the way she always did, and this time she did get up, because it was still dark and he was warm and the cabin was warm and there was a quilt over both of them and his arm was across her waist and his breath was even and she lay there for a moment in the dark and listened to him breathe and the stove tick and the frost come down on the window, and the heel still hurt a little but it was getting better and she was going to let it get better and she was going to shoot left-footed for another week and she was going to be right at the hall Tuesday and he was going to be there and she was going to catch him looking and she was not going to care.

At half past four she slid out from under his arm with the care of a thief and dressed in the dark, buckles quiet, and stopped at the chalk slate by the door and added a line beneath the laundry instructions, and got out into the cold before he could wake up.

Outside, the north wind had the whole hill, and the path home was gravel under her boots, nine amber lanterns and the tenth, and her breath going up white into the dark, and the sun was coming.

DON'T MOVE THE TARGETS FOR ME, said the new line on the slate. Beneath it, in smaller letters, she had written: UNLESS THE NOVICES ARE TERRIBLE.

She was grinning when she reached the fork in the path. The next time Annabeth asked whether she wanted to talk, Thalia thought she might actually say yes.
