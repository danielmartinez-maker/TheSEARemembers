## Bonus Interlude: The Sunday Afternoon

Sunday, the fifth of November, and the cold had broken for exactly one day.

The sun came down off the ridge and made the frost on the grass go silver, and the lake lay flat and bright under a sky that had decided, for once, to be kind, and the whole camp had the loosened feel of a place that had been holding its breath through October and was finally allowed to let it out.

Thalia Grace came up the west path in the middle of the afternoon with her hands in her pockets and her breath going up and the tenth lantern dark on its hook at the bottom of the hill because it was daytime and the lanterns only came on at night, and she did not go to the shooting line or the forge or the pavilion, because she had decided, somewhere on the path, that she was going to the camper's cabin at the end, and she had decided it was going to be in the daylight this time, with the sun on her back and the door wide open and nobody pretending it was anything other than what it was.

She had not told him she was coming. She had not told anybody. She had gotten up at seven the way she always did, and she had gone to the shooting line at eight the way she always did, and she had shot left-footed the way Chiron had told her to, and she had been worse at it, the way Chiron had said she would be, and she had not cared, because Rhys had been at the line with his bow unstrung and his hands in his pockets and his eyes on her, and she had caught him looking three times, and each time he had not looked away, which was the whole of the conversation.

At noon she had gone to the Big House and let Chiron look at the heel, and he had pressed it and she had not flinched, which was its own kind of flinching, and he had said, "Better. Another week left-footed and you'll be right," and she had said, "I'll be right at the hall Tuesday," and he had said, "You'll be right at the hall Tuesday and you'll be better by the winter," and that had been the whole of that conversation too.

The days between that first Monday night and the Sunday she had decided to claim had been longer than she expected. Not because she was miserable, which was how Percy had put it when he found her pacing in the rain on Thursday and then immediately claimed he had meant the weather. She simply had a new thing she wanted, and being required to go about her ordinary work while wanting it was an unfamiliar kind of inconvenience.

On Tuesday, the first time she wore the support wrap to the Tempest Hall, it caught under the edge of a bench and nearly sent her into Percy's stack of folded towels.

"Don't you dare," she said as he opened his mouth.

"I hadn't decided what I was going to say."

"You were going to say something about flying."

"I was thinking about the towels."

"You've never thought about towels in your life."

"I've had a complicated relationship with towels since Atlantis."

Annabeth, who had been measuring the distance between two lines of floor marks, looked up just long enough to say, "If you knock over those towels, you're folding them again. Both of you."

That had been the end of it. Thalia spent most of the evening on the near side of the pool, where the spectators sat, giving advice to campers who had come expecting to watch rather than participate. Rhys stayed for the whole session, but he did not sit beside her. He took three younger campers through the safety markers, listened when Clarisse challenged a rule, and stopped one of the Apollo boys from treating the shallow end as a competition.

She found herself noticing all sorts of things about him she had failed to notice at the archery range. He could be patient with a nervous child and thoroughly impatient with somebody showing off. He remembered everyone's name but lost his own pencil four times. When the swimmers left, he helped Silena haul a heavy crate out of a passage rather than wait for somebody to ask. When he came over to say goodbye, his attention stayed on Thalia's face despite the noise behind them.

"You have an opinion," he said.

"I have several. Your cousin can't follow a line on the floor."

"He follows it. He thinks the rules are wrong."

"He's going to break his head."

"I told him that."

"What did he say?"

"He asked whether I'd broken mine."

Thalia snorted. "Fair."

Rhys looked down at the wrap for no longer than an instant. "How was the foot?"

"Still attached."

"That's a start."

"Don't sound so impressed."

"I was impressed by the part where you helped somebody else shoot without insisting you should be shooting."

She could have taken that as a compliment. Instead she heard a report on her behavior, delivered in a voice gentle enough to be difficult to fight.

"Is everyone going to keep a ledger on me?"

He stopped smiling. "No. I'm not your keeper, Thalia."

"Good."

"And if you want me to stop asking, tell me that."

There it was: an answer that left the choice with her rather than doing the work of making it. She was still annoyed when he went back to collecting equipment. She was also aware that she had been unfair to him.

On Wednesday she sought him out at the target shed and said so. It took roughly fifteen seconds, which felt excessive.

He accepted the apology. Then he asked whether she would help him rebuild the smallest beginner's bow before the next lesson, and she accepted in turn. Neither of them attempted to make the exchange ceremonial. They spent the next hour arguing over the straightness of the replacement limb, with a ruler between them and a small pile of rejected shavings beside the bench.

It was a much better afternoon than Tuesday had been.

---

Thursday brought rain and a letter.

The letter was from Beryl, folded into a narrow envelope that had gone soft at one corner. Thalia recognized the handwriting before she looked at the name. She waited until after lunch to open it, then sat at the end of the Cabin One table while Annabeth fitted numbered slips of paper into a small tin box.

Beryl said the diner had asked her to cover extra mornings. She said she was trying to do better. She said she missed her girls. Between two sentences about coffee and bus schedules, she had written that she hoped Annabeth was listening to her big sister.

Thalia read that sentence three times.

Annabeth didn't ask what the letter said. She had learned not to do that. She kept arranging the slips by number, pressing the curled corners flat with the pad of her thumb.

"She says the diner gave her more shifts," Thalia said.

"That's good."

"Maybe."

Annabeth looked up. "Do you want me to read the rest?"

"You can if you want."

"I want to know what she said. That isn't the same thing as wanting to answer."

Thalia passed her the page.

Her sister read slowly. By the time she got to the end, her expression had changed so little that another person might have missed it. Thalia knew the difference between concentration and the kind of composure Annabeth used when she was trying not to give somebody else the satisfaction of seeing her upset.

"She still thinks I do everything you tell me," Annabeth said.

"She always liked that version."

"Did you?"

Thalia considered lying. "Sometimes. Especially when the alternative involved you climbing onto something tall."

Annabeth put the letter on the table. "I listen when you're right. I just don't want to have to pretend you're right first."

They were quiet a while. Outside, water ran down the glass and gathered on the sill where the old frame never quite shut properly.

"I don't want to be her," Thalia said.

"You aren't."

"That's not something you can settle with one sentence."

"I wasn't trying to settle it. I was answering."

Thalia watched her put the letter back into its envelope. The family they had arrived in was full of agreements that neither sister had ever made, habits that belonged to years Annabeth could not completely remember, and expectations that Thalia had carried long enough to mistake for herself. Even on good afternoons, they bumped against all of it.

"There's somebody I've been spending time with," Thalia said.

Annabeth looked at her over the envelope. "Rhys?"

"You knew?"

"I have eyes."

"Your eyes could mind their own business."

"They do. Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"Do you want me to ask questions, or do you want me to keep pretending I don't know?"

Thalia had expected teasing. She wasn't sure what to do with the offer of privacy.

"Keep pretending for now," she said. "I might change my mind."

"Fine."

Annabeth slid the envelope back across the table. She did not ask for a name, a promise, or a detailed explanation of the last Monday night. Instead she opened the tin box again and began counting the slips, and Thalia sat with her until the rain eased enough that they could go to supper without getting soaked.

Thursday evening, the rain was still running down the window of Rhys's cabin when Thalia appeared at his door.

She had said she might come. He had said he'd be free after six. Neither of them had clarified the part that followed, which meant she had spent the last quarter hour deciding whether she was late to an appointment that wasn't an appointment.

He opened the door before she could work out whether knocking would make the whole business feel ridiculous.

"You look soaked."

"Your powers of observation are improving."

"Come inside."

"You could have said that first."

He stepped aside. She crossed the threshold and took off her coat, sending a fan of rainwater onto the mat. The cabin smelled of tea and wool drying near the stove. He had swept the floor. She noticed that he had also moved a wooden chair nearer the fire and put an extra cup on the table.

"You prepared," she said.

"I boiled water."

"You moved furniture."

"An elaborate courtship ritual."

"Should I be impressed?"

"It depends on what you were expecting."

She caught the edge of his smile and felt something inside her ease. The trouble with the letter from Beryl wasn't that it had ruined the day. It had made the day feel borrowed, like the smallest pleasant thing might come with some distant adult standing outside to announce that she'd misunderstood it. She didn't want to bring that feeling into this room. She also didn't know how to leave it outside.

Rhys took the wet coat from her hand. "Tea?"

"Not yet."

He looked at her. "All right."

She crossed the small space between them and kissed him. She had thought about it on the walk uphill; she had decided to do it before she arrived, and the decision was enough to make her feel brave until the moment their mouths met and bravery stopped having anything to do with it. He put the coat down over the back of the chair without breaking away. Her hands found his shoulders. He was warm beneath the work shirt, and she was suddenly furious that she had wasted fifteen minutes on the path wondering whether he wanted her here.

He drew back just enough to speak. "Long day?"

"Terrible."

"Do you want to talk about it?"

"No."

"Do you want me to stop asking questions?"

She considered. "Just the boring ones."

He smiled, and she kissed him again before he could enjoy the joke. The rain kept falling behind the window. It could rain for a week. She had no particular objection to staying where she was.

She'd always considered herself a practical person. Her practicality generally involved lifting heavy objects, knowing where to stand in a fight and recognizing when somebody was bluffing. It had not prepared her for the sheer nuisance of discovering that a person could occupy her thoughts while she was doing something completely unrelated. Rhys had managed it with very little effort.

He touched the damp ends of her hair. "You're freezing."

"I'm not."

"Your hands are cold."

"They're usually cold."

"These are ambitious."

"Calder, if you keep talking about the weather, I will go home."

He caught her hands between his and rubbed warmth into them. "Then I'll have to be interesting."

She watched him work his thumbs over her fingers and felt the ridiculous urge to say something soft. Instead she lifted their joined hands and kissed the inside of his wrist. His expression changed immediately. It wasn't his practiced smile from the range or the grin he used when he'd won an argument. She liked the surprise in it.

"Interesting enough?" she asked.

"That was unfair."

"Good."

He drew her closer. His mouth met hers with enough certainty that she forgot the coat dripping on the chair, forgot that the tea would get cold, forgot she had spent the afternoon angry at the handwriting in a letter. Then he pulled back and waited, because she had made a small involuntary sound when her heel shifted.

"Foot?"

"A little."

"We can sit."

"I was doing something."

"I noticed."

She looked toward the narrow cot and back at him. "Sitting doesn't sound like a terrible idea."

He didn't give her time to reconsider. They moved together, laughing at the awkwardness of the chair between them, until the blanket caught beneath Thalia's knee and sent them both into a tangle of wool and elbows. He managed to catch the cup before it fell off the bedside shelf. She laughed harder than the near disaster deserved.

"Very impressive," she said.

"I'm usually more graceful."

"Liar."

"Occasionally."

He set the cup aside, and she moved close again. This time she took care with her bad foot, arranging herself comfortably instead of treating pain as an insult to be conquered. He accepted the adjustment without commentary. That was becoming one of the things she wanted most from him, even when she was still learning how to ask.

The laughter quieted. They kissed until neither seemed interested in testing the other's wit, then settled beneath the blanket as the rain thickened against the glass. She wasn't sure when she'd begun to feel at ease here. Perhaps when he'd stopped trying to guess what she wanted and learned to let her tell him. Perhaps when she'd stopped preparing to take every touch as a claim.

The evening drew in around them. When the lamp needed turning down, Rhys reached for it, and she stopped him with a hand against his cheek.

"Leave it for a moment," she said.

He did. She wanted to see his face. In the soft light he looked younger, almost shy, the careful confidence of the instructor replaced by something much less defended. She kissed him again. This time she wasn't hurrying.

They had an entire room, a closed door and the strange luxury of not having to prove themselves to anybody. She chose to stay close, and he chose it with her.

Much later, when the rain had eased and the room had become quiet, he asked whether she'd like a fresh cup of tea.

"Are you trying to send me away?" she asked from beneath the blanket.

"I'm trying to prevent you from dying of cold."

"I'm doing very well."

"You're wearing my socks."

"They were available."

"Yes, mysteriously."

She stretched one foot toward the stove, leaving the injured one supported by the pillow, and examined the enormous striped sock with distaste.

"These are appalling."

"My mother made them."

"Then they're extremely good socks."

He laughed. "I'll tell her you said so."

"You will do no such thing."

He got up to refill the kettle, and she watched him cross the room. There was something distinctly satisfying about the sight of him going about a ridiculous household task when they'd spent so much of the evening wrapped up in each other. He was not less desirable with a kettle in his hand. Quite the opposite, which was an inconvenient piece of information about herself.

"Stop looking at me like that," he said, not turning around.

"Like what?"

"Like you're considering another bad decision."

She smiled into the blanket. "Can't imagine where you'd get that idea."

He brought two cups over and sat beside her. She shifted enough to give him room without losing the warmth he'd left behind.

"Can I ask about your day now?" he said.

"You can."

"Will you answer?"

"Possibly."

She considered telling him about the letter. She could have said her mother's name and invited him into a story that was still difficult even when she knew every part of it. He would have listened. But the story belonged to her, and tonight she didn't want to hand it over simply because they were becoming close.

"Annabeth was difficult," she said instead.

"That sounds like a family talent."

"She's getting too good at reading me."

"Terrifying."

"She thinks she knows what's good for me."

"And does she?"

"Sometimes. Which is worse."

Rhys rested his hand beside hers rather than over it. When she took it, he smiled into his cup.

"Does she know about us?"

"She knows I'm spending time with you."

"Is that a problem?"

"Not for her."

"And you?"

Thalia looked around the cabin. His spare shirt hung over a chair. The blue mended glove lay near the tools. Her wet coat was drying by the stove, unmistakable proof that she'd chosen this place over an evening alone. She wanted him. She liked being with him. She liked that there was no answer he could demand she hadn't already chosen to give.

"I haven't decided how much of this I want everybody to know," she said.

"Then we don't announce anything."

"You're being suspiciously reasonable."

"Only when I've been properly motivated."

She leaned over to kiss him and tasted the strong tea he had insisted on making. He put the cup down very carefully before returning the kiss.

Before she went, Rhys stopped her at the door with the striped socks in his hand.

"You forgot these."

"I didn't forget them."

"They're still on your feet."

"Then the system is working."

"Thalia."

She looked up at him, trying not to smile. He had a faint crease between his eyebrows that appeared whenever he was concentrating, whether on an arrowhead or on figuring out what to say to her.

"They're yours," he said.

"I know."

"Do you want to keep them?"

She was unreasonably pleased by the question. He could have told her to take them; he could have made a joke and turned the moment into one more example of how he knew what she wanted. Instead he was waiting.

"Yes," she said. "For a while."

"Then keep them."

She stepped closer and kissed him, slowly enough that neither of them could mistake it for a reward given in passing. When she drew back, he was still looking at her.

"Are you always going to be this difficult to leave?" she asked.

"Only when you keep giving me good reasons to ask you to stay."

"That's a very dangerous answer."

"I've made peace with it."

Thalia touched the open collar of his shirt, straightening it without thinking. He glanced down at her hand and then back at her face.

"I want to see you again," she said.

"You will."

"That's not quite what I meant."

His smile gentled. "I want that too."

She nodded once, satisfied. The straightforward answer felt better than any elaborate promise. She gave him one last kiss, slipped her boots over the borrowed socks, and took her coat from the hook. He watched her, not trying to slow her down. She liked that almost as much as she liked being asked to stay.


By the time she left, the rain had stopped. Her coat was mostly dry, and she had borrowed the striped socks with the explicit promise that she would return them. She doubted she would keep that particular promise. Rhys walked her to the threshold and rested one hand on the doorframe.

"Sunday?" he asked.

"Maybe."

"You've got a fierce commitment to uncertainty."

"I'm full of surprises."

"Good ones, sometimes."

She stepped up to kiss him once more. "Don't get ahead of yourself."

He smiled as she turned away. It was only halfway down the path that she realized she had spent the evening wanting to be there without once pretending the reason was a broken piece of equipment. That was progress. She would never say so aloud.

---

On Friday the sky cleared. Thalia and Annabeth went down to the work shed with their boots muddy and argued about drainage channels for forty minutes. It was a useful argument. Thalia had spent half her life trying to make sure her sister survived. Annabeth had spent a good part of this one trying to make sure that surviving did not become the whole definition of being alive. They could talk about drainage because the other conversation had stopped being an emergency.

---

The following week brought no spectacular change. There were mornings when Thalia caught Rhys looking at her from the opposite end of the range and evenings when he was too busy to come by Cabin One. They exchanged a few words over equipment, sometimes a touch that lasted only a second when no one was watching. She discovered that having a private life with somebody did not require every meeting to be private. It could be carried into an ordinary day in the smallest ways: knowing what a half smile meant, hearing him call her by name, remembering where his hand had rested against hers when they had been alone. She missed him on the days they barely spoke. She hated how pleased she was when he found time to walk beside her toward supper. Neither feeling required an immediate explanation, and neither turned her into somebody else.

By the following Saturday, the hall schedule had acquired six new names. Three were Apollo campers, two belonged to children who had never been in the water after dark, and the sixth was Clarisse, written in such heavy pencil that it left an indentation through the paper.

Thalia found Rhys checking the new list outside the pavilion.

"You're recruiting," she said.

"Trying to. Winter gets long."

"People can entertain themselves."

"Some can. I've seen what happens when the younger ones spend a week arguing about whose bunk is warmer."

She leaned against the post beside him. "You've been doing this longer than I thought."

"Coming back for winter? A few seasons. Organizing things? Only when someone else is tired of doing it."

"Why?"

"My mother works shifts. I was the oldest kid home. If I wanted something to happen, I usually had to put the chairs out myself."

It was the first time he had offered a piece of his life outside camp without using it to make a point about hers. Thalia turned her head to look at him.

"You don't always look like somebody who's had to put chairs out."

"That's because I am very good at putting them out."

"What happens when winter's over?"

"I'll go back to help her for a while. I haven't decided what comes next."

She did not ask whether that decision included her. The question would have been too easy to turn into a trap for both of them. Instead she took the paper, looked at the empty volunteer slots, and wrote her name beside the next beginners' session.

"You could have asked," Rhys said.

"You were going to."

"I was considering it."

"And I saved you the trouble."

He folded the list and tucked it under his arm. "The first sunny afternoon all week is tomorrow. Are you planning to use it?"

"I haven't decided."

"Very mysterious."

"I have an image to protect."

He laughed and went inside to collect the chairs. She watched him for a moment and then followed, because he was right about one thing: somebody had to put them out.


By Saturday evening the sky had cleared completely. The temperature had fallen with it, and the windows of the north cabin were beginning to frost along the edges when Thalia reached the porch.

Rhys was outside carrying a bucket of ashes toward the fire pit. He spotted her from halfway down the path.

"You're back."

"I'm allowed to walk along a public road."

"Is that what this is?"

"The north road is public to campers."

"Very legalistic."

She watched him empty the ashes, then return with the bucket. Her heel was better than it had been, and she'd stopped needing to plan each step. She still resented the wrap. It made her feel as though her own body had joined the list of people who enjoyed telling her what to do.

Rhys set the bucket beside the door. "Want to come in?"

She wanted that very much. She had spent a significant portion of the day trying not to picture this room: the narrow cot, the spare blanket, the workbench he always left cluttered no matter how often he cleared it. The picture arrived anyway, especially during the moments when her attention was supposed to be elsewhere.

"Maybe," she said.

He raised an eyebrow. "You're getting very committed to that word."

"I like a versatile answer."

"Does it mean yes?"

"Not always."

"Then I'll wait."

He leaned against the doorframe. She moved close enough to stand beside him, shoulder to shoulder, facing the black path down toward the lake. For a while neither spoke. The cold made the tips of her ears sting. Rhys had left his shirt collar open despite the temperature, which was a foolish choice that distracted her more than she'd have liked.

"I have a question," he said.

"You're full of them."

"Do you actually enjoy making me guess?"

Thalia stared into the dark. She could easily make a joke. This time she was too interested in the answer he might give if she didn't.

"Sometimes," she said. "Sometimes I'm waiting to see if you'll decide you want me here badly enough to say it."

"I've said it."

"Once."

"More than once."

"I might have missed a few."

He turned to face her. "I want you here, Thalia. Not because you need a reason to visit. Not because I need help with a bow. I like it when you come around, even if you're only stopping by to insult my coffee."

"Your coffee deserves it."

"Absolutely."

"Then why ask the question?"

"Because if you don't want to come in, I don't want you thinking you have to. And if you do, I'd rather you tell me than spend an hour making both of us guess."

She looked at him. There it was again, that irritating clarity of his. Her sister could be equally direct, but Annabeth's directness had an entirely different edge. Rhys spoke as though the answer genuinely belonged to her. It made her want to say things she usually kept to herself.

"I like being here," she said.

"Good."

"And I like knowing you want me."

"Also good."

"And I don't know what I'm doing half the time."

"That makes two of us."

She turned toward him. "You don't act like it."

"Should I start?"

"Maybe. For variety."

He stepped closer and paused. She put a hand against his chest, feeling the warmth through his shirt. His heart was beating quickly enough that she knew he'd been telling the truth.

"I can hear you thinking," he said.

"Then you're in trouble."

"Probably."

She drew him down to kiss her. They were on the porch, in a place where anybody turning off the north path might see them. For a moment she felt the old instinct to check whether anyone was watching. She ignored it, but she was not entirely comfortable with the ignoring. She didn't want a performance. She wanted him.

Rhys seemed to understand the difference. He kissed her slowly, one hand resting near her waist, and when she drew back he didn't pull her close again to prove a point.

"Inside?" he asked.

She looked at the dark path. "For a little while."

He opened the door. The cabin was warmer than the porch, and she was relieved to be able to take off her coat. He hung it beside his, then went to turn up the lamp.

"Leave it," she said.

He stopped. "Too bright?"

"Not bright enough."

He turned the wick until the room filled with a warmer light. She watched him do it and recognized that she had another answer for the question she hadn't been brave enough to ask earlier. She wanted him to look at her. She wanted to see him looking, and she wanted the choice to be hers.

He came back toward her. She caught the front of his shirt before he could say anything. Their kiss began with a smile and became serious before either of them had the chance to make another joke. When he drew back she followed, annoyed at the small distance.

"You're impatient," he said.

"You've only just noticed?"

"No. I'm starting to enjoy it."

She kissed him again, and this time she took her time. She hadn't known how much she'd like learning the expressions he made when he was trying not to laugh, or the way his confidence could give way to something unexpectedly tentative when he asked for an answer and actually waited for it.

They settled on the cot with a blanket over their knees. He placed the second pillow by her injured foot before she had to ask. For once she didn't make a comment about it. She sat facing him, their knees touching, and watched the little gold circle of lamplight on the sleeve of his shirt.

"Is this awkward?" she asked.

"Which part?"

"The part where I'm sitting here trying to think of something interesting to say."

"I'm finding it fascinating."

"You're impossible."

"Sometimes we could just sit here."

"That sounds dangerously domestic."

"Don't tell anyone."

She laughed and leaned into him. He put an arm around her, and the ease of it surprised her. She'd been so focused on the moments when they kissed that she'd forgotten what it might feel like to simply stay. To talk about nothing for a while. To lean on someone because she wanted to.

He rested his cheek against her hair. "You're quiet."

"I'm thinking."

"Should I be worried?"

"Not yet."

They sat together as the stove cooled. After a while he asked if she wanted more tea. She shook her head. He brushed a strand of hair back from her face, and she turned toward him and kissed him again. The kiss made her forget what she'd been about to say, which was either a sign of disaster or a genuine improvement in her evening.

When she pulled away, he looked as if he wanted to ask another question. She forestalled it.

"I like this," she said.

He smiled. "I was going to say the same thing."

"Don't get used to agreeing with me."

"I'll make a special effort."

She traced the line of his jaw with her thumb. He was not a prize she'd won by being difficult, and she was not a problem for him to solve by being patient. They were two people who had made a choice and were still discovering how to live with it. The fact that they wanted each other made the learning rather more enjoyable.

At nine, Thalia checked the clock and groaned.

"What's wrong?"

"I told Annabeth I'd be back before she went to sleep."

"Did you tell her where you were going?"

"No."

"Will she be angry?"

"Probably not. She'll be unbearably reasonable, which is worse."

Rhys rose to fetch her coat. She caught his hand before he could reach the hook.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"What about it?"

She thought about the forecast, the brief window of sunlight, the work she could leave for another day. There was no reason she couldn't choose an afternoon for herself.

"I might come by."

He smiled but didn't press. "I'd like that."

"I know."

"Would you like me to pretend to be surprised?"

"At least make an effort."

She stood and pulled on the coat. He opened the door, and the cold air came in around their feet. She turned and kissed him once, then again because the first kiss had been unnecessarily short.

"Good night, Calder."

"Good night, Grace."

She went down the path slowly, taking care on the frosty gravel. The lamps beyond the cedar brake were burning. She could feel the warmth of his hands long after she'd left the porch, and by the time she reached the fork she had made up her mind about Sunday.

She was going to surprise him.



She had tried to keep her mind on anything except the last time she had been alone with him, and the effort had made her disagreeable all morning.

There were details she hadn't meant to remember so clearly. Not the heroic ones people would tell around a campfire. The way he'd asked her to move a pile of bowstrings because she'd put her elbow on them without thinking. The little sound of recognition when she'd laughed at something he said instead of finding an argument. How different it had felt to stand beside him without a target between them, with neither a score nor an audience to tell her what she was supposed to do next.

The nights in between had made her miss his company in a way she did not particularly enjoy admitting. She liked the impatient, ordinary desire to see him again. She didn't like how easily it could make her read a perfectly innocent look across a shooting line as a private invitation.

Now she was acting on one deliberately. She didn't intend to spend the afternoon talking about a target shed. She didn't intend to explain away her visit as concern for her heel. She wanted to be alone with him, to kiss him until they both forgot to make jokes, and then to find out whether they were capable of sharing a quiet hour without inventing another excuse.



After Chiron's examination, she had meant to go directly to the north path. Instead she went back to Cabin One and stood in front of the open trunk at the foot of her bed, looking at her own clothes as though she'd never seen them before.

It was absurd.

She owned three perfectly good shirts. Two were too warm for the afternoon, and the third was the one she'd worn to the range that morning. She could wear the third. Rhys wouldn't care. The problem was that she cared, for reasons she was unwilling to examine too closely.

Annabeth was nowhere in sight. Her notebook lay open on the table beside a ruler and a piece of folded tracing paper. She laughed. He brushed his lips against her forehead, then her cheek, and she caught his face between both hands before he could turn the gesture into teasing. The next kiss was slower. The kind that had nowhere urgent to go. Her shoulders gradually stopped holding themselves so stiffly, and he seemed to notice without congratulating her for it.

At last the cold reached them from the glass. Rhys took her hand and drew her toward the cot, where a blanket lay folded along the foot. Thalia sat first, placing the injured heel where it wouldn't take her weight. He settled beside her. They were close enough that their knees touched.

"Are we going to talk about tomorrow?" he asked.

"Do we have to?"

"Not if you don't want to."

"Then no."

"All right."

She shifted toward him and rested her head briefly on his shoulder. He placed his arm around her without ceremony. She could tell he was resisting the temptation to ask why she had gone quiet again. He was learning that silence didn't always need to be solved.

After a moment she lifted her head. "I want this to be the same tomorrow."

He considered. "It won't be exactly the same."

"Unhelpful."

"We'll be outside. There'll be people to teach. You'll be furious at somebody for moving a target."

"I will be furious at you."

"Probably."

"But I don't want you acting as if today didn't happen."

"I won't."

She studied his face for a trace of insincerity. There wasn't one. That was inconvenient. It left her with nothing to fight.

"Good," she said.

He touched a loose strand of hair beside her temple. "Will you?"

"Will I what?"

"Act as if it didn't happen?"

She looked at him for several seconds, long enough that his expression became uncertain. He was capable of asking difficult questions without sounding demanding, but she could see the question mattered to him.

"No," she said. "Not if I can help it."

"You don't have to tell everybody."

"I know."

"And you don't have to pretend I’m a stranger."

"Nobody would believe it. You've spent six weeks ruining my archery lessons."

He laughed. She drew closer and kissed him again, lingering this time because it pleased her to see how quickly his expression changed when she did. It was flattering to want someone and discover he wanted her just as plainly, without either of them needing to describe it to an audience.

They stayed that way as the light dwindled. Sometimes they talked. Sometimes she interrupted him to kiss him. Once he reached for the blanket and she caught his wrist, only to realize he was trying to keep her bad foot warm. She let him, then offered him the dignified privilege of not mentioning it.

Later, she changed into the spare shirt he had left by the cot. He turned away while she did, although she told him he was being absurdly formal about a person who had spent the afternoon kissing him. He answered that she could call him absurd if she liked. She did, several times, before settling beneath the quilt and drawing him closer.

The room was familiar in a new way now. Not because she had memorized every object, but because she knew she could say what she wanted there without somebody treating the admission as weakness. She could reach for him without an excuse. She could ask him to wait. She could change her mind and he would change with her.

Their closeness made the night pass without ceremony. The lamp burned low. She kept his hand in hers and allowed herself the rare pleasure of staying exactly where she wished to be.

At some point, when she had stopped paying attention to the clock, Rhys shifted to put another piece of wood on the fire. She caught his sleeve.

"Wait."

"The stove needs feeding."

"In a minute."

He looked at her, then smiled. "You keep telling me that."

"And you keep listening."

"Somebody has to."

She pulled him down beside her and kissed him once more. The wood could wait. So could the morning. For this small part of the night, she wanted to be greedy with time, and she could see from the way he smiled that he was happy to let her.

She woke at four the way she always did, and this time she did get up, because it was still dark and he was warm and the cabin was warm and there was a quilt over both of them and his arm was across her waist and his breath was even and she lay there for a moment in the dark and listened to him breathe and the stove tick and the frost come down on the window, and the heel still hurt a little but it was getting better and she was going to let it get better and she was going to shoot left-footed for another week and she was going to be right at the hall Tuesday and he was going to be there and she was going to catch him looking and she was not going to care.

At half past four she slid out from under his arm with the care of a thief and dressed in the dark, buckles quiet, and stopped at the chalk slate by the door and added a line beneath the laundry instructions, and got out into the cold before he could wake up.

Outside, the north wind had the whole hill, and the path home was gravel under her boots, nine amber lanterns and the tenth, and her breath going up white into the dark, and the sun was coming.

DON'T MOVE THE TARGETS FOR ME, said the new line on the slate. Beneath it, in smaller letters, she had written: UNLESS THE NOVICES ARE TERRIBLE.

She was grinning when she reached the fork in the path. The next time Annabeth asked whether she wanted to talk, Thalia thought she might actually say yes.

Monday, November sixth, began with a message written in chalk.

Rhys discovered it at half past six, when he opened the cabin door to find the morning air clear and hard enough to sting. The slate had acquired a new line beneath the laundry instructions: DON'T MOVE THE TARGETS FOR ME. Beneath that, smaller and more daring, was the qualification about terrible novices.

He stood in the doorway for a while, looking at the handwriting.

He had seen Thalia leave a note on the slate after the first night, but this one was different. It was not a declaration, and it did not offer a promise meant to last forever. It was a joke aimed at him, written in the sort of handwriting that made every letter look as though it had been chiseled into the board by somebody who objected to small talk. It meant she had been here. It meant she had thought of him before she left.

He smiled, then went inside for the eraser.

For one uncomfortable moment he considered rubbing out the joke before the other Apollo campers saw it. Not because he was ashamed of her, but because he knew what would happen once they noticed a message in a hand that wasn't his. Questions. Speculation. The kind of friendly teasing that became a problem when people stopped remembering that the relationship belonged to the people in it.

He left the note where it was.

Later, at breakfast, a fellow instructor glanced at the chalk on his sleeve and asked why he looked so pleased with himself. Rhys told him he'd finally repaired the north bow rack. That happened to be true. He didn't mention the slate.

Thalia, meanwhile, had reached Cabin One before the first bell and fallen asleep for another hour. She woke to find the sun already making a bright line across the floor. Annabeth was gone. A cup of tea stood on the table beside a scrap of paper: *I went to the construction shed. Your heel wrap is on the chair. You left it under the bed.*

Thalia stared at the note and smiled.

Her sister knew too much. She had always known too much. The difference lately was that Thalia was beginning to understand she did not have to keep every part of her life hidden in order to keep it her own.

She put on the wrap, checked the fit, and went down to the dining pavilion. She saw Rhys before he saw her. He was carrying a tray, arguing with a friend about whether a bowl of porridge counted as a complete breakfast. His hair was still damp from washing. The sight made her stomach perform a thoroughly undignified little movement.

She went to get food and sat at the opposite end of the table.

There was a moment when he looked up and caught her eye. No one else noticed, or if they did, they didn't interrupt. He raised his cup in a tiny private salute. She raised hers in return.

That might have been the whole of the morning if he hadn't approached her after breakfast.

"I found your note."

She looked at him with practiced innocence. "What note?"

"The one that threatens my target placements."

"It was a professional warning."

"You know, someone else is going to read it."

"Good. They need to know what they're dealing with."

He considered her. "Do you want me to erase it?"

The question took her by surprise. She had left the message half expecting him to laugh and half expecting herself to regret it when she returned to the field. She hadn't expected him to offer the choice back to her.

"No," she said. "Leave it."

"All right."

"And if somebody asks?"

"I'll tell them a terrifying woman inspected my targets."

"Accurate."

They were standing near the pavilion door, where anyone might pass. He didn't reach for her. She wanted him to, then found herself annoyed that she wanted it. She could choose. She had been telling herself that for two weeks.

She took his hand.

He looked down at their fingers, then back at her face. There was a fraction of surprise before the pleasure arrived, and the sight made her bold.

"Walk with me," she said.

"Where?"

"Somewhere flat."

"Your heel?"

"It's fine. I'd like to keep it that way."

He fell into step beside her. The path around the lower meadow was almost empty at this hour. The lake was bright and still. Thalia could feel the light pressure of his fingers around hers, comfortable enough that she stopped noticing it as a separate fact and simply enjoyed walking with him.

"Last night was good," she said.

He smiled. "I thought so."

"I didn't ask for a review."

"You started one."

She laughed. "Fine. I liked being there."

"So did I."

"And I liked the part where we weren't trying to impress each other."

He looked thoughtful. "I was still trying a little."

"Naturally."

"I wanted you to have a good time."

"I did."

She stopped near the low fence. He turned toward her.

"Can I tell you something embarrassing?" she asked.

"I've been collecting your embarrassing statements."

"Very brave."

"Go on."

She looked down at their hands. "I packed a clean shirt before I came over yesterday."

He blinked, then laughed with such genuine surprise that she had to grin in spite of herself.

"You planned ahead."

"I did."

"Thalia Grace planned ahead for something pleasant."

"Keep talking and I'll take it back."

"The shirt?"

"The pleasant part."

He drew her closer and kissed her. The kiss was brief, but she felt its warmth all the way down the path. She had wanted the small acknowledgment all morning. Now she had it, and she didn't feel the need to make it grand.

"Come by after supper?" he asked.

She considered. "Not tonight."

He nodded.

"I want to spend the evening with Annabeth," she said. "We haven't had much time alone."

"Then do that."

"You aren't disappointed?"

"A little. I like seeing you. But I'm glad you want time with your sister."

She looked at him. "Those two things don't cancel each other out."

"No."

She kissed him again, partly because she liked being able to and partly because the straightforward answer deserved something better than a sarcastic remark.

At the fence he let her go. They had work in different parts of the camp. He headed toward the target shed; she went uphill toward Cabin One. For once the prospect of being apart for a day did not feel like evidence that something was missing.

The following evening she would see him again. They had not made a schedule. They had made a choice that could be made again.

Tuesday's trouble began with an invitation delivered too early.

Thalia found Rhys behind the archery shed while he was trying to repair a stuck latch. He had his coat folded over a post, his sleeves rolled, and a pencil behind one ear that he had apparently forgotten was there. She waited until he looked up.

"Tonight," she said.

He blinked. "Good morning?"

"Don't make me repeat myself."

"I'd like to know what I'm agreeing to."

She came close enough that she could lower her voice without making the conversation secretive.

"I want to see you tonight. Properly. No target inventories, no missing gloves, no audiences. I thought I'd ask rather than just appear at your door."

He smiled, but it was a tired smile.

"I'd like that."

"Excellent."

"But I have a rough day ahead. Can I tell you later whether I'll be up for company?"

Thalia stared at him. She knew the words were reasonable. The immediate little sting was not.

"You're busy."

"I'm busy until about six. After that I may be tired. I don't know yet."

"You can say no."

"I might. I'm trying not to say yes just because I want to please you."

She looked away. The remark wasn't an accusation, but it landed close to something she had been avoiding. She wanted him. She was becoming accustomed to finding him pleased when she wanted him. The possibility that he might decline without loving her less shouldn't have been difficult to understand. It was anyway.

"Fine," she said, and heard the sharpness.

He set the latch down. "Are you angry?"

"Not at you."

"Are you sure?"

"Rhys."

He waited. That was what made it worse. He wasn't defensive, and he wasn't trying to make her feel foolish. He was giving her a chance to speak honestly, which meant she had no excuse for turning this into a fight.

"I'm disappointed," she admitted. "I was looking forward to it."

"So was I. But I don't know whether I'll be good company tonight."

"I don't need you to be entertaining."

"I know. I don't always want company when I'm tired."

She considered the distinction. It had taken her most of the last week to tell him that she wanted space sometimes. She could hardly object when he asked for the same thing.

"Then let me know," she said.

"I will."

She stayed long enough to help hold the latch steady while he tightened its screws, then went about her morning. She tried to stop thinking about the conversation and failed. She was irritated with herself for feeling rejected by a man who hadn't actually rejected her. Worse, she was irritated with him for having been clear about what he needed.

At four she saw him near the dining pavilion and nearly approached. Instead she kept walking. He looked tired. She could see it in the way he moved, the small pause before he lifted a crate, the attention he had to pay to a task that normally came easily. She wanted to go to him and tell him to stop working. She wanted to take over the job and make the evening possible.

That was a particularly bad idea. She kept walking.

At half past seven, someone knocked at Cabin One.

Thalia opened the door to find Rhys standing on the porch, still wearing the coat he'd been working in. His hair had come loose over his forehead. He held a folded piece of paper.

"I said I'd let you know," he said.

She stepped outside, closing the door behind her. The air smelled of frost and chimney smoke. She could hear the low murmur of voices from the lower path.

"Well?"

"I'm exhausted," he admitted. "I don't think I have an evening in me."

She looked at him. The answer hurt less than she'd expected, perhaps because he'd come himself instead of sending a note.

"All right."

He held out the paper. "I wrote you something."

She took it, suspicious.

"What is it?"

"A terrible attempt at an apology for being tired."

"You don't need to apologize for being tired."

"I know. But I wanted to give you something."

She unfolded the note. It was a short set of musical notes, five bars written in Rhys's careful blocky hand. Beneath them he'd written: *For the woman who never believes me when I say I like seeing her.*

Thalia read the sentence twice.

"Is it a song?"

"Not yet."

"Then why give it to me?"

"Because you told me you wanted to see me. And I wanted you to know I heard you, even if I can't spend the night with you."

She looked up. He was watching her with a small, apologetic smile. She liked the gesture more than she wanted to admit. He wasn't trying to make the disappointment disappear. He was acknowledging it.

"You are an idiot," she said, quietly.

"I've been told."

"A thoughtful idiot."

"That's an improvement."

She moved close enough to kiss him. The kiss was gentle, unhurried, and she could feel the fatigue in the way he leaned into it. When she drew back, his eyes remained closed for an instant.

"Go to bed," she told him.

"You're sure?"

"I'm sure."

He opened his eyes and smiled. "I really did want to see you."

"I know."

"And I still do."

"Then you can come find me when you're rested."

He nodded. There was relief in his expression, and something like gratitude. She realized she had been given an opportunity to show him the freedom he had already offered her.

"Good night, Thalia."

"Good night."

She watched him go down the path until the light swallowed his figure. Then she went inside and looked at the musical notes again.

She could not read the whole melody from sight. She knew enough to recognize the shape: a small rising phrase, a pause, and a final line that returned to where it began. It was not an elaborate composition. It was his way of saying he had thought of her even when he couldn't give her the evening.

Annabeth looked up from her desk.

"What's that?"

"Music."

"From Rhys?"

Thalia considered lying. There was no reason to.

"Yes."

Annabeth's smile was immediate and infuriating. "Is it good?"

"I haven't heard it."

"Then play it."

"I don't have a guitar."

"You could hum it."

Thalia looked at the page. She tried the first three notes under her breath, missed the fourth, and laughed at herself.

"Don't tell him I did that," she said.

Annabeth turned back to her notebook. "Wasn't planning to."

Thalia put the paper carefully inside her book, beside the photograph from the orchard. For a while she lay awake thinking about the difference between being refused and being unwanted. She hadn't liked the lesson when it arrived. She liked the person who had taught it to her.

Wednesday, she decided, she would ask him about the rest of the melody.

On Wednesday, Rhys was waiting at the old amphitheater with a guitar.

Thalia had come because a scrap of paper had appeared in her boot at breakfast. She had no idea how he had managed that without being noticed, but the note was unmistakably his: *Seven. East steps. Bring your ears. Leave the target scores at home.*

She arrived early, partly because she was curious and partly because she objected to being late for an invitation she had not demanded. The amphitheater was nearly empty at this time of year. Rows of damp wooden seats descended toward a small stage, and the lamps at the edges had been lit against the early dark. Rain had washed the steps clean. The air smelled of wet earth and old pine.

Rhys sat on the edge of the stage with the guitar across his knees. He had a sheet of music beside him, weighted with a pebble.

"You're early," he said when he saw her.

"You were vague."

"About what?"

"Whether this was an appointment or an ambush."

"It's neither."

"Then why am I here?"

He played the first five notes of the melody he'd given her the night before. They rose, paused, and settled into a second phrase that she hadn't heard. He played it once, then again, each time with a small change in the ending.

She stood at the foot of the stage and listened. The music was not polished. He'd made no effort to turn the melody into something grand, and there was a hesitation at the transition that she remembered from Friday. But the tune had a shape now, a definite emotional movement from restlessness to a quieter resolution.

When he finished, he looked at her.

"Well?"

She considered. "You stole my idea."

"What idea?"

"The pause."

He smiled. "You taught me something."

"That's inconvenient."

"Terribly."

She climbed onto the stage and sat beside him. He moved the music so it wouldn't blow away.

"I liked it," she said.

He blinked. "You did?"

"Don't sound shocked."

"I was bracing for a detailed report on my errors."

"I could provide one."

"Maybe after supper."

She looked toward the empty seats. "Why here?"

"Better sound."

"And no witnesses."

"That too."

He set the guitar aside. She moved closer, then stopped herself. The night before she'd wanted him to spend time with her and he hadn't been able to. She had made peace with that. But now he was here, offering her a private performance, and she was aware of how much she'd missed being near him.

"Are you rested?" she asked.

"Mostly."

"Good."

"Why?"

She turned toward him and kissed him.

There was a moment of surprise, then his hand came to her cheek and the kiss deepened. She could feel the softness of his mouth, the warmth of his fingers against her skin, the way his breath changed as he leaned toward her. The amphitheater was empty, but it was not the familiar privacy of his cabin. She was aware of the space around them, the rows of seats, the open night. Somehow it made the choice feel more deliberate.

He drew back, smiling.

"I missed that," he said.

"Your own fault."

"I know."

She caught his sleeve. "Don't make me say it again."

"Say what?"

"That I wanted to see you."

He kissed her again. This time she let him take the lead for a moment, enjoying the rare luxury of not having to decide everything. When he drew back, she was smiling so openly that she didn't bother to hide it.

"Better?" he asked.

"Considerably."

He laughed, and she could hear the relief behind it. They had managed to survive one evening apart without treating it as a catastrophe. Now they were together again, and there was a pleasant ease in the reunion that she hadn't expected.

Rhys picked up the guitar and played the melody a third time. Thalia listened, then reached for his hand when he finished.

"Do you know how to dance?" she asked.

He looked at her, surprised. "A little."

"Show me."

"Here?"

"Unless you've reserved another amphitheater."

He set the guitar safely on a chair. She stood in the center of the stage, suddenly self-conscious. It was one thing to kiss someone in the dark; another to ask him to dance when neither of them had an audience to impress.

Rhys came to stand in front of her. He put one hand at her waist and held out the other. She took it, and he began to count a slow rhythm.

"One, two, three," he said.

"I know how to count."

"I'm making sure."

She stepped forward at the wrong moment and landed lightly on his shoe.

"That's not the count," he said.

"Your shoe was in the way."

"It was attached to my foot."

"Then move your foot."

He laughed. They tried again. She was better at footwork when someone was trying to hit her with a sword, apparently, than when the point was to stay close and move together. She kept anticipating the next step, shifting before the count, and Rhys kept drawing her back into the rhythm.

"Stop trying to win," he said.

"I'm trying not to fall."

"Trust me for three steps."

She looked at him. The request was small and precise. Three steps. Not a lifetime of surrender. She nodded.

He counted again. She followed, and this time their feet moved together without argument. They turned slowly in the lamplight, laughing when her heel made her take an awkward half-step. She adjusted without losing the rhythm. He kept his hand steady at her waist.

For several minutes they danced badly and then a little better. There was no music now except the tune in his head and the quiet rhythm he hummed under his breath. Thalia found herself moving closer until the space between them seemed unnecessary.

"You're not terrible at this," he said.

"High praise."

"I was going to say good."

"Don't get reckless."

He smiled and drew her nearer. She rested her head briefly against his shoulder, then looked up. The kiss that followed was slow and comfortable, interrupted by laughter when she lost her footing and caught his shirt.

"Sorry," she said.

He looked at her in mock alarm. "Was that an apology?"

"Don't get used to it."

"Never."

They stopped dancing. He kept one hand against her back, and she let her fingers rest near his collar. The night was cold, but the small space between them felt warm enough. She could hear the distant sound of water somewhere beyond the trees and the creak of the stage boards beneath their feet.

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"You're going to anyway."

"Would you like to call this a date?"

She considered. The word didn't sound as dangerous as it had a week ago. A date meant they had chosen to spend time together because they wanted to, that they might plan another one and look forward to it. It didn't have to mean they had settled every question about the future.

"Yes," she said.

His smile widened. "Good."

"But don't make a fuss."

"I wouldn't dare."

She kissed him again to emphasize the point. He kissed her back, and she felt the pleasant certainty of being wanted without needing to turn the feeling into a competition.

They remained on the stage until the lamps began to burn low. Rhys gathered the guitar and music, and she helped him carry the chairs back where they belonged. The work took five minutes. She didn't mind. They had spent the rest of the evening entirely on themselves.

At the fork he stopped.

"Come home with me?" he asked.

Thalia looked toward the north path. She wanted to. She also knew she was tired, and her heel had begun to ache after the dancing. She didn't want to turn the invitation into an obligation just because she'd enjoyed the evening.

"Not tonight," she said.

"All right."

"I wanted to," she added.

"I know."

"Tomorrow?"

"Maybe."

She narrowed her eyes. He was smiling. He had stolen her own word and was enjoying it far too much.

"You're learning bad habits," she told him.

"From a very good teacher."

She laughed and kissed him on the cheek. Then she went uphill toward Cabin One, carrying the tune in her memory and the knowledge that they had named something between them without making it more complicated than it needed to be.

Thursday's first disagreement began with a photograph.

Thalia had gone to Rhys's cabin shortly after sunset. There was no particular reason for the visit beyond the fact that they had enjoyed Wednesday's date and she wanted to see him again. She'd spent the afternoon occupied with her own work, and that had made the thought of an hour alone with him especially welcome. She was tired of being helpful. She wanted to be desired, and to have that desire meet her own without a dozen people needing something from either of them.

The door opened at her knock. Rhys smiled, then stood aside.

"You're here."

"Observant."

"Come in."

She stepped inside and immediately saw the photograph.

It was the one from the orchard, the first picture he'd taken of her, propped against a small book above the workbench. She had known he meant to keep it. She hadn't pictured it displayed where anyone entering the cabin could see.

She stopped.

Rhys followed her gaze. "What?"

"That's my picture."

"Yes."

"You said you wouldn't show anyone."

"I haven't shown anyone."

"It's sitting out."

He looked at the photo, then back at her. The delight of her arrival drained from his expression.

"I put it there because I wanted to look at it," he said.

"Other people come in here."

"Not often."

"That isn't the point."

He took a breath. She could see him considering several answers and discarding them.

"You're right," he said.

Thalia wasn't prepared for the admission. It took a moment before she could work out whether she was still angry. She was. The fact that he agreed didn't erase the small shock of finding herself on display without having chosen it.

Rhys crossed to the workbench, picked up the photograph and slipped it into the pages of a notebook.

"Better?"

"I didn't say you had to hide it."

"No. But you said I shouldn't leave it where anyone can see. That's fair."

She looked at him. "You could be annoyed."

"I am a little. Not because you asked me to move it. Because I liked having it there."

"Then maybe ask me first."

"I should have."

He sat on the edge of the workbench. She remained near the door, her coat still on. The room felt suddenly less welcoming, not because he'd made it so but because she'd come in expecting affection and found herself setting a boundary instead.

"I don't want to be a secret," he said.

She frowned. "I never said you were."

"I know. But sometimes I think you're deciding how much of me you're willing to let the rest of your life see, and I don't get a voice in the decision."

The statement landed harder than the photograph had.

"That's unfair."

"Maybe. I'm telling you what it feels like."

She took off her coat and put it on the hook, more sharply than necessary. She wanted to answer with a list of ways she'd already chosen him: the public hand-holding, the orchard date, the kiss on the range bench. She could recite the evidence like a lawyer. But the fact that she had a list suggested he was not entirely wrong.

"What do you want?" she asked.

He looked surprised. "Right now?"

"About us. About people knowing. About that picture."

He considered. "I want to be able to say I'm seeing you without feeling that I'm betraying a confidence. I want to keep a photo of you somewhere I can see it. And I want you to tell me when something is too much, rather than assuming I'll guess."

Thalia looked at the floor. Those were not extravagant demands. They were ordinary desires from someone who was becoming attached to her. She felt them as pressure because she had spent so many years making sure that no one else could set the terms of her life.

"I haven't decided how public I want to be," she said.

"I know."

"And I don't want to decide tonight just because we fought."

"Then don't."

She looked up. He was watching her steadily, without the smile he usually used to soften an argument.

"Can we agree on what I am allowed to say?" he asked.

She considered. "You can say we're spending time together. You can say you like me. You can say I'm the most talented archer you've ever met."

He nearly smiled. "That last part is disputed."

"Then don't expect mercy."

She took a breath. "But not details. Not about what happens when we're alone. And if somebody asks directly, I want us to talk before either of us answers."

"That seems fair."

"And the photo?"

He glanced toward the notebook. "I'd still like to put it up."

She thought about the image: herself making a ridiculous face, caught in a moment she had chosen. It wasn't an intimate picture in the sense of revealing anything private. What bothered her was the idea that other people could see her being happy with him and turn that into a story she hadn't approved.

"Keep it in your notebook for now," she said. "We can talk about it again later."

He nodded. "All right."

She went to sit beside him on the bench. There was enough space that neither had to touch the other. For a while they looked at the stove and listened to it ticking as the fire settled.

"I'm still angry," she said.

"I can tell."

"Are you?"

"A little."

She folded her hands in her lap. "We don't have to pretend we solved everything."

"No."

He sounded relieved by the permission. So was she. They had agreed on what to do with the photograph; they hadn't yet learned what it meant to negotiate a private life together without feeling that one of them was surrendering.

After a while Rhys asked, "Do you still want to stay?"

She looked at him. She did. She also didn't want the answer to imply that their disagreement hadn't mattered.

"Yes," she said. "But I don't want to be kissed until I'm ready."

He nodded. "All right."

She stayed on the bench while he moved the books back onto the shelf. He made no effort to charm her out of her mood. Eventually she asked him about the piece of music he'd been working on. He showed her a revised middle section and explained why the instrument he expected to accompany it couldn't reach one of the notes. She offered a suggestion. They worked through the phrase together, and the activity gave them both something to do without pretending the conversation had disappeared.

Nearly an hour passed.

Thalia watched him set the guitar aside. His expression was tired now, and more thoughtful than annoyed. She reached for his hand and drew him nearer. He came without hesitation, settling beside her. She kissed him, softly at first, then with the familiar pleasure of his warmth and the way he leaned toward her. He didn't try to make the kiss prove anything about the argument. Neither did she. They could still be annoyed with each other and want to be close. Discovering that felt like a new kind of freedom.

When they drew apart, she kept her hand in his.

"Next time," she said, "ask me before putting my face on a shelf."

"Next time, tell me if you're feeling hidden."

She considered. "Deal."

He kissed her forehead, and she leaned against his shoulder. They sat in the small lamplight until the room grew quiet around them. The photograph remained in the notebook, unseen by anyone but the person she'd given it to. She knew it was there. For tonight, that was enough.

---

By Friday, the sky had lowered over the camp until the tops of the pines vanished into cloud. A hard rain began at four and showed no sign of stopping.

Thalia had planned to stay in Cabin One with a book. She read the same page six times before realizing that the problem was not the book. It was that she wanted to go north, and she was still annoyed with herself for wanting it so badly after the argument over the photograph.

She shut the book, pulled on her boots and fetched her coat.

At the door she stopped. She could arrive at Rhys's cabin and find him busy, or tired, or simply not interested in company. She had learned that much. The thought was no longer a reason to avoid asking, but it did persuade her to take a scrap of paper and write a note before she left.

*If you're free, I'd like to see you. I'll be by the north path in twenty minutes. If you're busy, I'll go home and insult your coffee tomorrow.*

She gave it to an adult camper heading past the Apollo cabin and watched him hurry off through the rain.

Twenty minutes later she was standing beneath the shelter near the north range, listening to water drum on the canvas overhead. The rain had made the path slippery. She'd taken it slowly, watching the repaired heel on the uneven ground. She was beginning to trust the foot again, but she wasn't prepared to spend another week proving that trust by injuring it.

Rhys appeared through the rain carrying two umbrellas, one folded and one open.

"You're ridiculous," he said.

"I was thinking the same thing about you."

"You sent a messenger to tell me you wanted to see me."

"That is how invitations work."

"Then why are you standing in a shelter instead of coming inside?"

"Because you might have been busy."

He looked at her, then smiled. "I'm not."

"Good."

He opened the second umbrella and handed it to her. They walked up the path together, side by side, their shoulders occasionally bumping because neither umbrella was as wide as it claimed to be. By the time they reached his porch her boots were muddy and his sleeve was soaked.

Inside, the stove was warm. He took her wet coat and hung it beside his own. The cabin had been tidied since the night before. The photograph was nowhere in sight. She noticed that without comment.

Rhys was watching her. "Are we still arguing?"

"Not right this second."

"That's encouraging."

She crossed the room and stopped in front of him. He did not reach for her, though she could see that he wanted to. That was the point at which she decided she was tired of giving the argument more authority than it deserved.

"I wanted to come here," she said.

"I know."

"I want you."

His expression changed. The words were plain and deliberate, and she had chosen to say them in a room where no one else could hear. There was a pleasure in seeing him take them seriously.

"Thalia."

"Don't make a speech."

"I wasn't going to."

"Good."

She took his hand and brought it to her waist. He waited until she leaned toward him before kissing her. The kiss began slowly and became more insistent when she moved closer. She could feel the cool damp of his shirt beneath her fingertips, the warmth of his skin at the collar, the way he held her as though he was trying to memorize the exact shape of this moment without pinning her in it.

They moved away from the doorway. She caught the edge of his shirt and pulled him nearer, laughing when he stumbled over a boot he'd left in the middle of the floor.

"You need to clean this place," she murmured.

"I was under the impression you liked me untidy."

"That's not what I said."

He kissed her again, and she forgot what she had intended to say next. For a while they stood close together in the warm cabin, rain hammering against the window, their hands and mouths finding an ease that had become familiar without becoming ordinary. She felt him smile when she caught his face between her hands. The smile was a private one, for her alone.

Eventually he drew back and looked at her, catching the smile she had made no attempt to hide. He grinned and followed as she drew him toward the cot. She stopped to take off her wet boots and rearrange the blanket so the sore heel wouldn't be trapped under her; Rhys was already reaching to clear space beside him. He helped without comment. They settled close together, and she rested her head against his shoulder for a moment before lifting her face to kiss him again.

The rest of the evening passed privately. There was warmth and laughter, moments of quiet attention, and the pleasure of being with someone who had learned enough about her to know when she wanted to be teased and when she wanted no commentary at all. The disagreement about the photograph remained part of their history, but it did not prevent them from choosing one another tonight.

Much later, when the rain had eased, she was lying beside him beneath the quilt. The lamp cast a narrow stripe of light along the wall. Rhys had one arm around her, and his breathing was slow and even. She listened to the rainwater dripping from the eaves, feeling an unexpected reluctance to move.

"You know what I hate?" she murmured.

"Several things."

"Don't be clever."

"Then tell me."

"I hate that I can be annoyed with you and still want to be here."

He laughed softly. "That's not a terrible problem."

"It feels inconvenient."

"Most good things are."

She raised her head and looked at him. "Don't turn this into a saying."

"I was trying to sound wise."

"Terrible attempt."

He brushed a loose strand of hair from her face. She caught his hand and held it against her cheek.

"I don't want us to pretend the argument didn't matter," she said.

"We won't."

"But I also don't want to spend every evening reviewing it."

"We don't have to."

She looked at him for a long moment. He was not asking for a verdict. He was offering a way to keep living while they worked through things.

"Good," she said.

He kissed her forehead. She turned toward him and kissed him properly, a slower kiss that held no urgency. She liked the feeling of having time. She liked knowing they could disagree, apologize, make choices, and still want each other without one part of the relationship erasing the others.

When she finally closed her eyes, the rain had stopped. The cabin smelled of warm wood and the faint sweetness of cider. She would wake early, probably before him, and leave when she chose. For tonight, the question of departure could wait.

---

Saturday morning Thalia woke after seven.

At first she didn't understand the light. It was too bright, coming through the quilted window in a broad pale square. She lay still, trying to work out what she'd forgotten. Then she heard Rhys breathing beside her and remembered that she had stayed the whole night without setting herself a departure time.

That was new.

She had always been the person awake first. When she was younger, being first had felt necessary. If something was wrong in the house, she wanted to know before anybody else. If there was a decision to make, she wanted it made. Her body had learned to wake long before other people were ready to begin the day.

Here, her body had done something disobedient. It had slept.

She turned her head toward Rhys. His hair had fallen over one eye, and his hand rested loosely on the blanket between them. He looked younger asleep, his expression relaxed and unguarded. She was struck by the urge to touch his face, then by the memory that she didn't have to invent a reason. She brushed the hair away from his eye.

He stirred.

"Morning," he murmured.

"Apparently."

He opened one eye. "What time?"

"Late."

"How late?"

"Past seven."

He smiled sleepily. "That's a respectable hour."

"It's a disgrace."

"Good. I've been a bad influence."

She should have got up. Her boots were by the door. Her coat hung beside his. She could be home before anyone needed her, with enough time to claim she'd been out walking. But the thought held no appeal. She lay back against the pillow and watched the light move across the wall.

Rhys shifted closer and rested an arm around her.

"Comfortable?" he asked.

"Don't make a thing of it."

"I wasn't planning to."

"Good."

They were quiet for a while. She could hear distant voices on the path and the clang of a bucket being set down outside another cabin. Ordinary camp noises. For once they did not summon her.

"You've never stayed this late," he said.

"Clearly I've been missing an opportunity."

He smiled. "You can stay as long as you want."

"I know."

"Do you?"

She looked at him. The question wasn't an accusation. He had seen enough of her habits to know that she sometimes treated departures as a form of discipline.

"I think I'm learning," she said.

His smile changed. He drew her closer, and she rested her head against his shoulder. The warmth of him was familiar now, yet she still felt a small thrill at being able to reach for him without worrying that the gesture would ask too much.

After a while she kissed him. He kissed her back, gently, without trying to wake the rest of the world. The quiet affection of the morning was different from the anticipation of the night before, but no less intimate. She liked that. She liked having more than one way to be close.

At eight he got up to make breakfast. She watched him cross the room and felt the unusual pleasure of not having to be useful. He found bread, eggs, and the last of a jar of jam. The stove took a few minutes to warm, and he complained about it in the tone of somebody addressing a difficult relative.

"Would you like help?" she asked from the cot.

"Not yet."

"Are you sure?"

"Very."

She made herself stay where she was. He was perfectly capable of cooking eggs. She had seen evidence of it, in fact, and there was no reason to take over the job simply because she knew how.

He returned with two plates and a grin.

"Breakfast in bed," he announced.

"This is a camp cot."

"Then breakfast on the camp cot."

"Much less romantic."

"I'll have to improve the marketing."

She laughed. They ate sitting cross-legged beneath the blanket, careful not to spill crumbs onto the sheets. Rhys told her that the letter from his mother had finally been answered and that he expected to hear back within the week. He was still uncertain about the trip home. There were details to settle: train times, whether somebody could cover his lessons, how much help she actually needed.

Thalia listened.

"Does she know about me?" she asked.

He looked surprised. "Not really."

"What does 'not really' mean?"

"She knows I've been spending time with someone at camp."

"That's evasive."

"I didn't give her your name without asking you first."

She considered. "You could tell her my name."

"Are you sure?"

"Yes. I don't want you pretending I'm a secret."

He smiled slightly. "I thought you liked being private."

"I do. There's a difference."

He nodded. "I'll tell her."

She looked down at her plate. She had surprised herself again. A week ago she'd been irritated by the photograph on his shelf, and now she was giving him permission to tell his mother he was seeing her. The two choices weren't contradictory. One involved an image displayed without her agreement; the other involved a person he loved knowing about someone who mattered to him. She could draw the line where she chose.

"Can I ask you something?" she said.

"Yes."

"If you go home, will you write?"

He looked at her. "Of course."

"I don't want a letter every day."

"Good. I'd run out of things to say."

"That hasn't stopped you before."

He laughed. "Maybe once a week?"

She thought about it. "That's reasonable."

"And you can write when you want."

"I have terrible handwriting."

"I've seen the slate."

"Then you've been warned."

He reached for her hand. She let him take it.

"I'd like to know what you're doing," he said. "Not every hour. Just the things you'd want to tell me if I were here."

She considered. It was almost the same request she'd made when he was tired: tell the truth, don't invent a performance to keep the other person satisfied.

"I can try," she said.

He smiled. "So can I."

They finished breakfast and cleared the plates together. At the door she put on her boots slowly, making sure the heel wrap sat correctly. Rhys handed her coat over without telling her how to manage it. She appreciated that he had learned the difference between concern and commentary.

"See you later?" he asked.

"Maybe."

He grinned. "There it is."

She stepped forward and kissed him. The kiss was lingering and warm, a farewell that felt less like leaving something behind than carrying it with her.

When she reached the path, she looked back once. He was still on the porch, watching her with a small smile. She raised a hand in farewell and kept walking.

At Cabin One, she found the morning well underway. Nothing had collapsed in her absence. No one had needed her at four. She had slept beside someone she wanted, eaten breakfast without rushing, and made a small plan for the possibility that he might leave for a while.

It was a good morning. She didn't need to turn it into a lesson.

---

Sunday evening began with an argument about a button.

Rhys had found the shirt he'd worn when he first returned to camp, a dark blue one with a collar that never sat properly. The top button was missing, and he was holding the spare between his fingers when Thalia arrived.

"You're going to try to sew that yourself?" she asked.

"That's the general idea."

"That explains the look of despair."

He looked up. "I was getting somewhere."

"Yes. Toward ruining the collar."

He held out the needle. "Then demonstrate."

She took it from him. She had intended to stop by for half an hour. It was already nearly eight, and she had spent much of the day thinking about the fact that he might leave the camp soon. She didn't want to make his departure the subject of every conversation. She wanted to enjoy being with him while he was here.

The button gave her something simple to do. She sat beside him on the cot and drew the thread through the collar with quick, practiced movements. He watched her hands.

"Your technique is annoyingly efficient," he said.

"I've been telling you that for weeks."

"I was hoping there might be something you weren't good at."

"Dancing, apparently."

"You've improved."

"Against all odds."

She finished the stitch and bit off the thread. He took the shirt and examined the button. It was slightly crooked but secure.

"Perfect," he said.

"Don't exaggerate."

"Very nearly perfect."

"Better."

He set the shirt aside and turned toward her. She was suddenly conscious of the fact that they'd been sitting close enough to touch for several minutes without doing so. The air between them felt charged, but not with the anxious anticipation of their first night. It was a familiar kind of wanting now, something she could recognize and meet without a long internal debate.

"Come here," she said.

He moved closer. She rested her hand at his jaw and kissed him, slowly at first, then with enough certainty that he drew her nearer. His fingers settled at the back of her neck, warm and gentle. She smiled against his mouth when he paused, enjoying the way he looked at her.

"You're staring," she said.

"I am."

"Any reason?"

"I like you."

"That's a dangerously simple explanation."

"It keeps proving useful."

She laughed and kissed him again.

The cabin was warm, almost too warm, because he'd banked the stove early against the cold coming down from the ridge. A strip of lamplight fell across the quilt. She could feel the soft cotton of his shirt beneath her fingers, the slight movement of his breathing as they leaned together. There were moments when she still wanted to turn the closeness into a challenge, to find a teasing remark sharp enough to keep her from sounding pleased. Tonight she let herself be pleased.

Rhys drew back after a while, but she kept a hand on his collar.

"I cleared tomorrow morning," she said.

His answering smile came at once. "Then we have time."

She liked the way he said it. Not as a claim on the night, but as a simple observation. They could choose to spend it as they pleased. She could ask for closeness. He could ask for rest. Neither request was a test.

She reached for his hand. "I want to take my time."

"All right."

"And you can tell me if you need me to slow down."

He smiled. "I will."

The answer gave her an unexpected rush of confidence. She had spent years being the person who moved first in every situation, who took charge because hesitation felt dangerous. Here she could move deliberately without needing to be in control of everything. She could pay attention to him, let him pay attention to her, and enjoy the exchange.

She kissed him again, lingering at the corner of his mouth. He touched her cheek, then let his hand rest there as though he wanted to keep the moment from slipping away. She drew him closer by the collar she'd just repaired, laughing softly when he protested that she was going to undo her own work.

"Then don't wear such tempting shirts," she said.

"That may be the most flattering thing you've ever told me."

"Don't spread it around."

He kissed her, and she stopped talking for a while.

The hours that followed belonged to them. There was affection and anticipation, teasing that gave way to quiet, and the private ease of two adults learning how to want each other without rushing or performing for anyone else. They chose what to share, what to ask for, and when to stop talking. Outside the shutter, the wind turned colder. Inside, the lamp burned low and the unfinished work on the bench could wait.

Later Thalia lay with her head against Rhys's shoulder, listening to the familiar ticking of the stove. She was tired in a pleasant way, content enough that it took her a moment to realize she had been smiling into the blanket.

"What?" he asked.

"Nothing."

"That's an unusually happy nothing."

"Don't get used to it."

He laughed softly. She turned toward him and rested a hand against his chest. She was aware that he knew her habits now: when she was trying to hide embarrassment, when she wanted him to speak, when she was waiting for a joke she could challenge. He knew enough to be irritating. He also knew enough to be gentle without making her feel that he was handling something fragile.

"Do you think this will be different when you go home?" she asked.

He considered. "We'll be apart. That will be different."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know."

She looked up at him. "I don't want us to become a collection of promises we can't keep."

"Then let's make small ones."

"Such as?"

"Tell each other when we're busy. Write when we have something to say. Don't make the other person guess whether silence means anger."

She considered. They were reasonable promises. More importantly, they were promises each of them could fail at and repair without pretending the failure meant the end of everything.

"I can do that," she said.

He touched her hair. "So can I."

She kissed his cheek, then settled beside him again. The evening had been about closeness, desire, and the pleasure of being wanted, but the conversation afterward mattered too. She didn't want to be a person he could only love in a room with the door closed. She wanted to carry something of this ease into the ordinary days when they weren't together.

Outside, the rain began again, soft against the window. She listened to it for a while and found that she didn't need to know exactly how many more nights he would be here. Tonight was theirs. Tomorrow could begin when it arrived.

---

Monday brought the first practical sign that Rhys might actually leave.

It was a letter, opened on the workbench beside the bowl of cereal he'd forgotten to finish. Thalia arrived in the afternoon and found him staring at the page with the expression he usually reserved for a bow that refused to hold tension.

"Bad news?" she asked.

"Complicated news."

"That sounds worse."

He handed her the letter. It was from his mother, written quickly but carefully, with a few words crossed out and a line added along the bottom of the page. She had managed to arrange some help for the winter, but there were still repairs and appointments to organize. She wasn't asking him to abandon camp forever. She wanted him home for a stretch long enough to be useful, and she had listed three possible dates.

Thalia read it twice.

"You should go," she said.

Rhys looked at her. "You haven't even asked what I want."

"You've been talking about this for weeks."

"That doesn't mean I want to leave."

She put the letter on the table. "Your mother needs you."

"Yes. And I like being here."

She was aware of the temptation to solve the problem for him. She could tell him which date to choose, help him arrange the camp schedule, make herself useful enough that he would never have to think about whether she wanted him to stay. She could turn the entire issue into a practical project and avoid admitting that it hurt.

Instead she asked, "What are you worried about?"

He looked at the letter. "That I'll go home and get caught up in everything, and we'll stop knowing how to talk. That I'll come back and it will feel strange."

She felt the same fear, though she would have preferred to hear him say it first.

"That could happen," she said.

He looked up, surprised by the bluntness.

"It could," she continued. "I'm not going to tell you it won't. We don't know what it will feel like when you're gone."

"I know."

"But that doesn't mean you shouldn't go."

He nodded slowly. The fact that she had not tried to persuade him to stay seemed to make the decision both easier and harder.

"Would you come with me to the station?" he asked.

She considered. "If you want me there."

"I do."

"Then I'll come."

They sat beside each other on the cot. The letter lay between the cups on the table, a small piece of paper carrying more consequence than its size deserved. Thalia was suddenly conscious of wanting to be close to him. Not because she wanted to erase the decision, but because she wanted him to know the decision hadn't turned him into a person she resented.

She took his hand.

"Come here," she said.

He moved closer and rested his forehead against hers. She kissed him slowly, with none of the teasing she usually used to hide how much she cared. His hand rested at the back of her neck, and she felt the familiar comfort of the touch. There was no promise in it that the weeks apart would be easy. It was a promise only that they were still choosing each other now.

When they drew apart, Rhys smiled faintly.

"You're being unexpectedly kind."

"Don't get used to it."

"I wouldn't dream of it."

She rested her head against his shoulder. They sat together for a while, the way they had on the first night, except that the silence now held knowledge neither had possessed then. She knew how he sounded when he was tired, how he looked when he was worrying about his mother, and how quickly his smile returned when she made a particularly bad joke. He knew she could be unreasonable, but also that she was trying to be fair.

"Which date?" she asked eventually.

He glanced at the letter. "The nineteenth would be easiest for her."

She calculated. Six days. It seemed too soon, although she knew that was merely the feeling speaking.

"Then choose the nineteenth."

"You're sure?"

"It's your decision. I'm telling you what the letter says."

He smiled, recognizing the correction. "Right."

He chose the date and wrote the reply while she sat beside him. She didn't dictate the letter. She had opinions about the phrasing, of course, and she kept most of them to herself. When he finished, he handed it to her.

"Read it?"

She did. It was honest, practical, and not especially elegant. He told his mother he would come, that he would stay until the work was under control, and that he wanted to be back at camp before the season changed too far. He didn't promise a return date he couldn't guarantee.

"Good," she said.

"Really?"

"Yes."

He looked relieved. She folded the letter and handed it back.

After that they talked about ordinary things. Whether his winter boots would survive the trip. Whether somebody else could use his practice bow while he was away. Whether the music he'd been writing should go with him or stay in the cabin. She helped him make a short list because he asked, not because she took over, and he watched her write it with an expression of affection that was difficult to ignore.

"You're staring," she said.

"I am."

"Why?"

"Because you're here."

She looked at him. The answer was simple and deeply inconvenient.

She put down the pencil, moved closer, and kissed him.

They stayed that way until the stove needed attention. He took care of it while she watched, still a little reluctant to let go of the moment. Then he came back and settled beside her.

"Can I ask you something?" she said.

"Yes."

"When you're gone, do you want us to tell people we're together?"

He considered. "I thought we had already decided that much."

"We decided you could tell people we're seeing each other. That's not quite the same thing."

"Do you want to call it being together?"

The question made her pause. She had spent so much time resisting labels that she'd forgotten they could be useful when they described a reality she already recognized.

"I think we are," she said.

He smiled slowly. "So do I."

She felt a sudden rush of happiness, followed by the old instinct to make a joke. This time she didn't. She leaned over and kissed him, letting the moment be exactly what it was.

"We're together," she said.

"Yes."

"Don't get dramatic."

"I wouldn't dare."

He was grinning. She grinned back. The letter to his mother was still on the table, and the decision to leave had not changed. But something between them had become clearer, not because they'd conquered the future, but because they'd finally named the present.

---

Tuesday evening, Thalia found three slips of paper waiting on Rhys's table.

"What's this?" she asked.

"A very bad idea."

"At least you're honest."

He had written a question on each slip and folded them into neat squares. The table had been cleared, the lamp turned down, and two cups of cider sat near the stove. He looked pleased with himself, which made her immediately suspicious.

"Explain."

"We spend a lot of time trying not to say what we're thinking."

"You spend a lot of time making observations that are perfectly obvious."

"And you spend a lot of time insisting they aren't."

She folded her arms. "Where are you going with this?"

"Three questions. We take turns. If you don't want to answer, you don't have to. No points, no winner."

"That sounds suspiciously unlike a game."

"It's an experiment."

"I hate experiments."

"You love experiments."

"Only when I design them."

He held out one of the slips. She took it, unfolded it, and read the first question aloud.

"'What is something you want that you have not asked for?'"

She looked up. "You're trying to get me to do your work for you."

"Then ask me first."

She considered, then handed him the paper.

"What is something you want that you haven't asked for?"

He looked toward the window. "I want a picture of us where you're not shoving me."

She laughed. "That was a perfect photograph."

"It was a very accurate photograph."

"Same thing."

"But I'd like another one. Not necessarily today. Just one where we're both looking at the camera."

She thought about the photograph he'd kept in his notebook. The dispute over it had been real, and she didn't want to pretend it hadn't mattered. But the idea of choosing another image together no longer made her uncomfortable.

"We can do that," she said. "After you come back."

He smiled. "I'd like that."

"Now your turn."

He gestured toward the same slip. "You haven't answered."

She took a breath. The question was harder than it appeared. She wanted many things from him, some easy to say and others more difficult because saying them would reveal how much she cared.

"I want you to ask me to stay sometimes," she said.

He looked surprised. "I do."

"You ask if I want to stay. That's different."

He considered. "You want me to tell you that I want you here."

"Yes."

"All right."

"And I still want to be able to say no."

"Of course."

The straightforward acceptance left her oddly exposed. She had imagined having to defend the request, to explain why being wanted mattered without making it sound like an obligation. He simply understood.

He moved closer, stopping in front of her.

"I want you to stay tonight," he said.

She looked at him. There was an entirely unreasonable pleasure in hearing the words so plainly.

"That's better," she said.

He kissed her. The kiss was gentle, but she could feel the anticipation that had been gathering between them since she arrived. She slipped one hand behind his neck and held him close for a moment longer than she needed to. When they drew apart, both were smiling.

"Second question," he said.

She groaned. "You have prepared too much."

"Humor me."

She unfolded the next slip.

"'What is something you are afraid I might misunderstand?'"

She looked at him. "You are enjoying this."

"A little."

"Then you answer first again."

He thought for a while. "I'm afraid you'll think going home means I'm choosing not to be with you."

The answer was so honest that she stopped smiling. He had been worrying about the same thing she had, only from the opposite side.

"I don't think that," she said.

"I know. But I don't want it to become a story we tell ourselves when we're apart."

She reached for his hand. "Then we won't."

"Good."

She considered her own answer. "I'm afraid you'll think I'm embarrassed by us because I don't want everyone knowing the details."

He shook his head. "I don't think that."

"I know. But I still worry."

"Then we can talk about it again when it comes up."

She looked at him, satisfied. It was not a perfect solution. It was a way to keep the subject from becoming a weapon.

"Third question," she said.

Rhys handed her the last slip. She unfolded it.

"'What should we do before I leave?'"

She stared at him. "That's not a question. That's a plan disguised as one."

He looked delighted. "You've discovered my scheme."

She considered the possibilities. They could spend every night together, trying to compress the next few weeks into six days. They could arrange elaborate dates that would leave them exhausted and irritable. They could pretend the departure wasn't coming. None seemed particularly appealing.

"I want one evening with no plans," she said.

He nodded. "All right."

"And one afternoon outside the cabin. Something ordinary."

"Also possible."

"And I want to be the one who chooses when we say goodbye."

He looked at her. "What do you mean?"

"I don't want people crowding around us at the station. I don't want a performance. I want to say goodbye to you properly, in a way that belongs to us."

He considered. "Then we'll find a quiet place before I leave."

She nodded. It was exactly what she'd been trying to say without admitting how much the departure frightened her.

Rhys gathered the slips and set them aside. They had answered the questions, and the experiment was finished.

"What now?" he asked.

She looked at him. He was nineteen, standing in front of her with a slightly anxious smile, waiting to see whether she would choose to stay. She wanted him. She wanted the intimacy of his company, the pleasure of his mouth against hers, and the comfort of not having to invent a reason for either.

"I thought you asked me to stay," she said.

"I did."

"Then stop standing across the room."

He laughed and came to her. She took his face between her hands and kissed him, letting the evening move into a quieter rhythm. The questions had not solved the problem of the approaching separation. They had made it easier to stop thinking about the problem long enough to enjoy being together.

Later, when the lamp was low and the cider had gone cold, she found the slips of paper beside the table and folded them into her coat pocket.

"Are you stealing my experiment?" he asked.

"Evidence."

"Of what?"

"That you occasionally have good ideas."

He smiled. She kissed his cheek, then settled beside him again. The night was still young, and for once she had no interest in counting the hours left before morning.

---

On Wednesday, Thalia agreed to eat dinner with Rhys and two of the older Apollo instructors.

It was his idea, and she distrusted it at once.

"Why?" she asked when he invited her.

"Because I like having you around, and they asked whether I wanted to bring someone."

"Bring someone to what?"

"Dinner. We take turns cooking on Wednesday evenings. Nothing elaborate."

"That sounds like a trap."

He laughed. "It's stew and bread, not a tribunal."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

The meal was in a small common room at the north end of the camp, a place used by the older instructors when they wanted to eat after the pavilion had closed. The stove worked reasonably well. The chairs were mismatched. Someone had made bread that was slightly burned on the bottom but perfectly good if one ignored the evidence.

Thalia sat beside Rhys and tried not to behave as though she were being examined. The other instructors were friendly, though one of them had the dangerous conversational habit of asking direct questions while holding eye contact.

"So," the woman said halfway through dinner, "are you two together?"

Rhys glanced at Thalia.

It was such a small action that the woman might not have noticed. Thalia did. He wasn't asking permission to acknowledge her existence. He was checking whether she wanted to answer for herself.

"Yes," Thalia said.

The word landed neatly in the middle of the table. No one gasped. No one launched into a lecture. The woman smiled, said that she had suspected as much, and asked whether Thalia wanted another piece of bread.

"Yes," Thalia said again.

Rhys was trying not to grin. She kicked his boot lightly under the table.

"Don't," she murmured.

"I haven't done anything."

"You've got the face."

He looked down at his plate. It was a poor attempt at concealment.

The conversation moved on to a damaged set of winter shutters and a concert the Apollo cabin wanted to organize when the weather was too cold for outdoor gatherings. Thalia found that she was able to take part without the relationship becoming the only interesting fact about her. The woman who'd asked the question wanted to know whether Thalia would help judge the archery demonstration before the concert. Thalia said she would consider it if the targets were placed properly. Everyone at the table laughed, including Rhys, who had endured several weeks of commentary on the subject.

By the time they left, the cold had sharpened. The stars were clear above the ridge, and the path back to the cabin ran through a narrow stretch of pines where the wind dropped away.

Rhys took her hand.

"You were good in there," he said.

She stopped. "Good?"

"I meant comfortable."

"That isn't what you said."

He turned toward her, his expression rueful. "I said the wrong thing."

"You did."

"All right. You seemed comfortable."

She considered. "I was."

"Good."

"You're doing it again."

He laughed and pulled her gently closer. "I liked having you there."

That was better. She rested her hands against the front of his coat and looked up at him.

"I liked being there," she admitted. "Even if your friends are nosy."

"They're efficient."

"They're terrifying."

"Only when the bread burns."

She smiled and kissed him. The kiss was unhurried, but it carried the lingering pleasure of having walked into a room together and left together without having to hide either fact.

As they continued up the path, Thalia thought about the moment at dinner. She had expected public acknowledgment to feel like surrendering something private. Instead it had felt like telling the truth about a fact that already existed. The rest of their relationship remained theirs to define. Nobody at the table had demanded an account of the nights in his cabin or the letters they might write when he was gone.

It was possible, apparently, to be known without being exposed.

At the cabin Rhys put the kettle on while she took off her coat. The room was familiar now, enough that she could find the spare blanket without looking and knew which floorboard creaked near the cot. She settled there and waited for him.

He came over with two mugs.

"Tea?"

"You're very predictable."

"I've worked hard at it."

She took a sip and put the mug aside. She was still thinking about the way he'd looked at her when the instructor asked their question.

"Thank you," she said.

He blinked. "For what?"

"Letting me answer."

He sat beside her. "I thought it was your question to answer."

"It was."

He nodded, as if that settled the matter.

She turned toward him and kissed him, a slow kiss that made the room feel quiet around them. He rested his hand at her waist, and she drew nearer until their shoulders touched. There was desire in it, but also a familiarity that had grown from being seen together in daylight and choosing each other in private afterward.

When they drew apart, she was smiling.

"What?" he asked.

"Nothing."

"That's a dangerous nothing."

She caught the collar of his shirt and pulled him closer again.

"Then perhaps you'd better stop asking."

He laughed into the next kiss. They spent the rest of the evening in each other's company, talking, touching, sometimes falling quiet and letting the silence be comfortable. The public part of their relationship had expanded by a single honest word. The private part remained as intimate as ever.

Before she went to bed, she slipped the photograph of them from the orchard into the back of her book. She looked at the blurred faces and the crooked angle of his smile. Tomorrow, she decided, she would ask him to take another picture.

This time she might even agree to look at the camera.

---

Thursday afternoon, Thalia found the old camera in Rhys's hands.

He was standing in the orchard beside the low wall where they'd taken their first photographs. The trees were almost bare now, their remaining apples gone or bruised into the grass. The day was cold but dry, and the late sun threw long shadows across the path.

"You planned this," she said.

"I was hoping you'd come."

"You left a note."

"I did."

"With a time."

"Yes."

"That sounds suspiciously like a plan."

He smiled. "I've been caught."

She came closer and looked at the camera. He had cleaned the lens and checked the remaining film. One exposure was left.

"Do you want another picture?" he asked.

She considered. The first had been a surprise, and she'd agreed only after he gave her the choice. The second had caught them laughing at the wrong moment. She had kept that one because it showed something true without trying too hard to make it beautiful.

"Yes," she said. "But this time we're not going to look ridiculous."

He raised an eyebrow. "That's a very demanding condition."

"You're the one with the camera."

"I can operate it. I can't control what you do in front of it."

"An excellent excuse."

They set the camera on the wall, adjusted the angle, and tested the timer without using the film. Thalia stood beside him with her arms crossed, trying to decide how she ought to pose. Every option seemed absurd. If she smiled deliberately, she would look like somebody being instructed to smile. If she remained serious, she'd have a photograph of herself looking as though she wanted to fight the person taking it.

Rhys watched her for a moment.

"What's wrong?"

"Nothing."

"That's an impressive amount of nothing."

She gave him a look. "I don't know what to do with my face."

He laughed. "Use the one you came with."

"That's unhelpful."

"It's the only face I've ever seen you wear."

"You're going to regret that statement."

He stepped closer. "Then look at me."

She did.

He didn't ask her to smile. He simply took her hand, moved beside her, and waited. The camera's timer began to click. She was aware of his shoulder against hers, his fingers warm in the cold, and the quiet happiness of standing next to someone she had chosen.

On the last click he leaned over and kissed her cheek.

The shutter caught them in that instant.

Thalia made an indignant noise. "You cheated."

"I thought you wanted an honest picture."

"You were supposed to look at the camera."

"So were you."

She was still laughing when the print emerged. They stood close together to watch it develop. Slowly the image appeared: Rhys turned toward her, smiling against her cheek; Thalia with her eyes half closed and her mouth open in astonished laughter. The background was crooked. The light was imperfect. It was not the composed portrait she'd intended.

It was better.

She looked at the picture for a long moment.

"I like it," she said.

He leaned closer. "Really?"

"Don't make me say it twice."

He smiled. "I like it too."

She held the print carefully by its edge. It would have been easy to hand it over and let him decide where it belonged, but the argument over the first photograph was still in her memory. She wanted to be clear before it became another problem.

"You can keep this one," she said.

"Are you sure?"

"Yes. You can show it to your mother if you want."

He looked at her. "Thalia."

"Don't be dramatic."

"I'm not."

"You're making the face again."

He laughed, but his expression was soft. He had been planning to leave in three days, and the photograph would go with him. She found the idea comforting in a way she didn't want to overexplain. She didn't need him to stare at it every night. She wanted him to have a reminder of something they had chosen together.

"Can I keep the old one?" she asked.

"Of course."

"Then that's settled."

They walked back toward camp with the camera tucked safely in the bag. The path was flat enough that she didn't think about her heel at all. They passed the empty range, where the target boards had been taken down for repairs, and the sun caught the frost on the fence posts.

Rhys stopped near the cedar brake.

"Can I ask you something?"

"You just did."

He smiled. "Do you want to spend tomorrow evening together?"

She considered. Friday would be his last full evening before the weekend departure arrangements became unavoidable. She wanted it to be special, but she was also tired of treating every moment as a deadline.

"Yes," she said. "But no elaborate plans."

"That's a familiar rule."

"I mean it."

"Then we'll keep it simple."

She stepped closer, caught his collar, and kissed him. The kiss was brief, but she let it linger long enough to feel the warmth of his mouth and the way he smiled when she drew back.

"You're getting sentimental," she said.

"I have excellent company."

She looked at him. He was standing in the cold with the camera bag over one shoulder and a perfectly content expression on his face. She wanted to remember him this way: not as a man about to leave, but as a person who had chosen an afternoon with her.

"Friday," she said.

"Friday."

They continued up the path, hands linked, without bothering to decide whether the photograph had been a perfect success. It was theirs. That mattered more.

---

Friday was supposed to be an ordinary evening.

Thalia had insisted on it. She had told Rhys not to plan anything, not to cook an elaborate dinner or organize an outing, not to turn the remaining nights into a ceremony. She wanted to be with him without having to think about how many days were left before he went home.

It was a good idea in theory.

In practice, she spent the entire afternoon counting time.

At five she was at the north path, walking slowly because the ground had frozen again. At a quarter past she reached his porch and stood there for a moment, collecting herself. She had been here often enough that the door no longer felt like a threshold she needed courage to cross. That was precisely why the thought of a few weeks apart hurt.

She knocked.

Rhys opened the door with flour on his sleeve.

She stared. "What did I say about elaborate plans?"

He looked down at the flour, then back at her. "I was making bread."

"That sounds suspiciously like a plan."

"I was hungry."

"Convenient excuse."

He laughed and stepped aside. The room smelled of baking, warm wood and the faint bite of something sweet in the air. The workbench was covered with a clean cloth, and two loaves rested near the stove. He had also put a jar of cider on the table and brought the extra blanket down from the shelf.

"You prepared," she said.

"I cleaned."

"That's more alarming."

"Would you like me to put everything back?"

She shook her head and smiled despite herself. "No."

He closed the door behind her. For a moment they stood looking at each other, both aware that this evening mattered more than either wanted to admit.

Thalia moved first.

She crossed the small distance between them, put her hands against his chest, and kissed him. He responded immediately, one arm around her waist and the other hand resting at the back of her neck. The kiss lasted long enough that she forgot the remark she'd been preparing about his flour-stained shirt.

When they drew apart he was smiling.

"Hello," he said.

"Hello."

"You could have said that first."

"I considered it."

He laughed. She drew him closer again, enjoying how readily he came, how familiar his warmth had become. There was no need to negotiate whether they were allowed to want each other. They had already done that work. The question now was what they wanted to do with the time they had.

Rhys caught her hand.

"Do you want to eat?"

"Eventually."

"That's not a useful answer."

"It's the one I've got."

He looked at the loaves, then at her. "The bread can wait."

"At last, a sensible man."

She kissed him again. The small room felt warm and private, with the stove ticking and the wind moving through the pines outside. She could feel his fingers rest against her wrist, the way he paused to make sure she was still comfortable, the smile that returned each time she looked at him. It was more intense for being familiar. She knew his habits now, and he knew hers, and the knowledge made it easier to be direct rather than cautious.

They settled on the cot with the blanket over their knees. She leaned against him, and he held her close, neither speaking for a while. The evening had begun with anticipation, but the quiet afterward was just as pleasing. She could spend an hour like this and not feel that she'd failed to make the most of it.

"Do you think we're being ridiculous?" she asked.

"About what?"

"Acting as if a few weeks apart is the end of the world."

He considered. "I think we're going to miss each other."

"That's not the same thing."

"No. But it doesn't feel trivial."

She rested her head against his shoulder. "I hate how much I want you here."

He looked down at her. "I want to be here."

"I know."

"And I want to go home and help my mother."

"I know that too."

He kissed the top of her head. She turned and met his mouth, letting the kiss linger. She had wanted this evening for herself, but she didn't want to turn his departure into proof that he cared less. She wanted him to be able to leave without either of them having to apologize for the desire to stay.

After a while, they got up to eat. The bread was good, warm and slightly uneven, and Thalia told him it was terrible just to watch his expression.

"Liar," he said.

"Prove it."

He offered her another slice. She took it immediately.

"Convincing evidence," he said.

They ate sitting across from each other at the cleared table. Rhys told her that the travel arrangements were mostly settled. The camp would send the small supply van toward the station on Sunday, and he could ride along rather than arrange a separate trip. He had written to his mother with the details. He still didn't know the exact date of his return.

Thalia listened, then nodded.

"Good," she said.

"You don't have to say it's good if it isn't."

"It's good that you know how you're getting there. I don't have to enjoy the reason."

He smiled faintly. "Fair."

She reached across the table and took his hand. The gesture felt more intimate than she'd expected, perhaps because it belonged to a conversation neither of them could avoid.

"Will you take the photograph?" she asked.

"Yes."

"And the music?"

"Some of it."

"Take the rest too."

"Are you assigning me homework?"

"I'm protecting your mother from the terrible arrangement you were going to send her."

He laughed. She liked the sound more than she could say.

After dinner they cleared the dishes together. Rhys put the remaining bread away, and Thalia folded the cloth from the workbench. Their movements had become comfortable in the small space, no longer a series of polite negotiations about who should do what. He could brush past her without making the touch an event. She could catch his hand and hold it for a moment simply because she wanted to.

When the room was tidy, she stopped him by the table.

"I want to stay tonight," she said.

He looked at her. "I'm glad."

"But I don't want tomorrow to be a countdown."

"Then it won't be."

"And I don't want us making promises because we're afraid of leaving."

"We won't."

She studied his face. He looked as serious as she felt, and she was suddenly tired of seriousness.

"Good," she said. "Now come here."

He laughed, then kissed her.

The rest of the night was theirs. They had time to be affectionate, to enjoy the anticipation they had built all week, and to settle into quiet when they wanted quiet. There was no performance and no obligation to make the evening unforgettable. The desire between them was real enough without a script, and the intimacy mattered because both of them kept choosing it.

Later, beneath the quilt, Rhys traced a small circle on the back of her hand.

"You were right," he murmured.

"About what?"

"The bread."

She looked up. "It was terrible?"

"No. It was good. You were right that we didn't need anything elaborate."

She smiled. "I usually am."

"I've noticed."

She kissed him one last time before the lamp went out. She knew Sunday was coming. For tonight, she was content not to meet it ahead of time.

---

Saturday had no real plan, which turned out to be a problem only because Thalia was determined to make it perfect.

She woke beside Rhys with the first grey light on the window and lay still for several minutes, listening to him breathe. The cabin was warm. The stove had survived the night with a low bed of coals, and the floorboards had stopped their usual complaint about the cold. She could hear the faint tapping of rain against the roof. Everything seemed ordinary enough, except for the fact that tomorrow he would be leaving.

She could spend the day here, she thought. She could refuse to let him out of her sight until the moment the supply van arrived. She could make every hour a celebration of being together and then hate the celebration for reminding her that time was passing.

She turned toward him and kissed his cheek.

He opened one eye. "Morning."

"Get up."

He groaned. "You're terrible."

"Yes. We've established that."

"What time?"

"Early enough."

"That's not an answer."

"It's all you're getting."

He pulled the blanket over his head. She laughed and tugged it down again.

"Come on. We're doing something."

"What?"

"I haven't decided."

"Very reassuring."

She kissed him, and he stopped arguing long enough to return it. For a moment she was tempted to abandon the whole idea and stay exactly where she was. The familiar warmth of him was difficult to resist. But she had been thinking about what would happen after tomorrow, and she wanted one day that contained more than waiting.

"Breakfast," she said. "Then we'll see."

They ate at the little table by the stove. Rhys made eggs while she cut bread, and the two of them argued about whether the last apple belonged to the person who had bought it or the person who found it in the cupboard. Thalia won the argument by eating the apple before Rhys finished his case.

"An unjust system," he said.

"An efficient one."

After breakfast they walked to the meadow, taking the flat path that no longer made her heel ache. The sky was still grey, but the rain had paused, and the cold air smelled clean. They walked hand in hand without talking much. She was aware of how comfortable the movement had become, of the way his fingers fit between hers, and of how much she would miss the simple fact of being able to reach for him whenever she wanted.

At the edge of the meadow Rhys stopped.

"Can I ask you something?"

"You're going to anyway."

"Are you trying to spend the whole day with me because you want to, or because you think you should?"

She looked at him. It was an irritatingly precise question.

"Both," she admitted.

He nodded. "I want to spend the day with you too. But I also have a few things to pack, and I'd like to say goodbye to some people before I go."

She looked away. She had known that. She simply hadn't wanted to hear it.

"Of course."

"I don't want you feeling as if I'm counting the minutes until I can leave."

"Then don't look like you're counting."

He smiled slightly. "I'm not."

She considered. "What if we do our own things for a few hours, then meet tonight?"

"That sounds good."

She exhaled, surprised by the relief the decision brought. The day no longer had to be perfect. It could contain ordinary obligations and still belong partly to them.

At the fork he kissed her, briefly, and went toward the north cabin. She watched him go, then turned uphill. She spent the next few hours helping with a repair she had promised to finish, writing a short note to her sister, and reading a book that she actually managed to concentrate on. At no point did she stop wanting to see Rhys. She simply discovered that wanting him did not prevent her from enjoying the rest of her day.

At dusk she returned to the north path.

The cabin was warm when he opened the door. Rhys had packed most of his belongings into a single canvas bag, which stood beside the workbench. The room looked strangely bare, though the furniture hadn't moved. She glanced at the empty space where his guitar usually rested.

"Where's the music?"

"In the bag."

"Good."

"You came back."

"I said I would."

He smiled and stepped aside. She went in, hung her coat on the hook, and crossed the room to him. For a moment neither spoke. Then she put her hands at his waist and kissed him.

The kiss began slowly and grew warmer as he drew her close. She could feel the relief in the way he held her, as though he'd been carrying the knowledge of tomorrow all afternoon and was glad to put it down for a while. She had been doing the same.

When they separated, Rhys rested his forehead against hers.

"Are you all right?" he asked.

"No," she said. Then, after a moment, "But I'm glad I'm here."

He kissed her again. She moved closer, letting the familiar warmth of his hands and the quiet of the cabin settle around them.

They spent the evening without trying to make it extraordinary. There was bread left from Friday, a little cheese, and cider in the last jar. He played one song on the guitar before packing it away again. She laughed when he missed the troublesome transition he'd worked on for weeks, then kissed him before he could defend himself.

Later, sitting together beneath the blanket, she took the three slips of paper from her coat pocket and showed him that she had kept them.

"You stole the game," he said.

"I told you. Evidence."

"Of what?"

"That we managed to say something true without either of us being unbearable."

He laughed. "A historic achievement."

She rested her head against his shoulder. The room was quiet, and the desire to be close to him had softened into a steady warmth. She no longer needed to prove that every touch meant something dramatic. She wanted him here because she wanted him here. That was enough.

Before they slept, she said, "Tomorrow I want five minutes alone with you. Before the van comes."

Rhys nodded. "Five minutes."

"No speeches."

"I'll try."

"And no promises you can't keep."

"Agreed."

She kissed him gently, then settled beside him. The future was still unfinished, but they had made a small plan for how to meet it. She thought she could manage five minutes.

---

Sunday began with a knock, not an alarm.

The supply van driver wanted to leave an hour earlier than planned. Rhys returned from the door with the message and found Thalia sitting upright on the cot, furious at a person she had never met.

"Ten?" she said.

"Ten."

"We agreed on eleven."

"The driver didn't."

"He has a terrible character."

Rhys looked as if he might laugh, then thought better of it. She was still wearing his shirt, and one of the striped socks had slipped down around her ankle. The cabin was warm. His bag stood by the door with the guitar case beside it, a perfectly ordinary arrangement that seemed suddenly offensive.

Thalia climbed out of bed.

"Then we'd better finish packing."

"It's finished."

"You've got the travel papers on the table."

"I can pick them up."

"And the stove."

"I know how to close a stove."

She stopped. The room was quiet enough that she could hear rain dripping from the eaves.

"Right," she said.

Rhys came closer. "You're not angry about the stove."

"No."

"Or the driver."

"A little about the driver."

He waited.

She wanted him to kiss her. She also wanted him to stay. Those were different requests, and only one of them belonged entirely to the present moment.

"I hate this," she admitted.

"So do I."

"That's not helpful."

"No. But it's true."

She looked at him. His own composure was strained. He had been trying to make the morning easy for her, but the effort had turned into another thing she could resent, another way for both of them to behave as though being disappointed was a failure.

"Come here," she said.

He did. She put her hands against his chest and kissed him slowly, without using the kiss to interrupt an argument or avoid the truth. His arms came around her, warm and familiar. The moment lasted until she was ready to let it end.

They stayed close afterward, foreheads touching, each aware of the canvas bag by the door.

"We promised five minutes alone," she said.

"We have them."

"Then no speech."

"I wasn't preparing one."

"You had the face."

"Apparently I have several."

She smiled despite herself. Then she kissed him again, quickly, and went to get dressed.

They ate breakfast while the stove cooled. Rhys made eggs, and Thalia cut the last of Friday's bread. They had done this enough times that the routine felt familiar, which made the idea of its absence unexpectedly painful. She wanted to remember this: the knife scraping against the plate, the slight burnt edge of the bread, the way he made a face when she stole the larger piece.

At nine fifteen they took the flat path toward the cedar brake. The ground had frozen overnight. The tenth lantern remained dark on its hook near the Cabin Three porch, and the other nine marked the route down toward the lake.

Thalia stopped at the bend.

"This is where I nearly turned around."

Rhys looked at her. "The first Monday?"

She nodded. "I stood here for a long time."

"What made you keep going?"

She considered. She could have described the cold, the light in his window, the borrowed coat that had been too big for her. None of that was the answer.

"I wanted to see you more than I wanted to avoid being embarrassed."

He smiled. "I'm glad."

She reached for his hand. The path was empty. There was no one to watch them, and she was grateful for it. She had been public about the relationship enough to know she didn't need to turn this moment into a statement for anyone else.

"I'll miss you," she said.

"I'll miss you too."

"I don't want you to feel guilty about going."

"I don't."

"Good."

She looked at him. He had been patient with her, sometimes more than she deserved, but he had also disagreed with her when she was unfair. She could imagine missing the disagreements almost as much as the warmth of his hands. The thought made her laugh softly.

"What?" he asked.

"Nothing. You're going to be unbearable in your letters."

"I'll try to maintain the standard."

"Write when you can. Not because you're afraid I'll be angry."

"I will."

"And if you're busy, tell me."

"Same for you."

She nodded. Those were small, useful promises. She trusted them more than anything grand.

He kissed her, and she let the moment last. She could feel the cold against her cheeks and the warmth of him where they touched. There was no perfect way to say goodbye. She wanted to be near him, to keep the next few minutes from disappearing too quickly, and she knew he wanted the same thing.

At last she drew back.

"Go," she said.

"Not yet."

"We have a driver to terrorize."

He laughed, then took her hand and walked with her toward the service gate.

The van was waiting beside the road, dented and practical. Rhys loaded his bag and checked his ticket. Thalia stood a few paces away while he spoke to the driver. She had already said what mattered. She could let the public part be short.

Rhys turned and came back to her.

"One more?" he asked.

"You're getting greedy."

"Apparently."

She kissed him in the open, a simple farewell with no effort to conceal it. When he stepped away, she touched the pocket where he had kept the photograph from the orchard.

"Don't lose it," she said.

"I won't."

"And don't stare at it like an idiot."

"That I can't promise."

She smiled, and he climbed into the van. The driver started the engine. Rhys lifted a hand through the window, and she lifted hers in return.

She watched until the vehicle disappeared around the bend.

The path home looked exactly as it had an hour earlier. The cold remained, the lake lay quiet beyond the trees, and the pines moved in the wind. Yet the camp seemed to have acquired a space where Rhys had been. She knew it would become ordinary with time. That was part of what made it hurt.

She took the long route back, passing the place where they'd taken the photographs and the amphitheater where they'd danced. She didn't stop at either place. At Cabin One she found the book that held her picture and opened it.

There they were, captured in the middle of laughter. She looked at the blurred faces for a while, then closed the book.

She had not promised to wait perfectly. He had not promised to return unchanged. They had promised to write, to be honest, and to leave room for the rest of each other's lives.

It would have to be enough to begin with.

---

On Monday, Thalia discovered that missing someone was not an efficient activity.

She woke before four and lay awake until sunrise. The room was the same as it had been the week before: the familiar blanket, the books stacked near the table, the little draught that came through the window if the wind turned north. Annabeth was asleep in the other bunk. The camp outside was quiet.

Thalia knew what she was supposed to do with a morning. She could get dressed, inspect the winter targets, attend breakfast and make herself useful until the feeling passed. That had always been her method of handling an unwelcome thought. The problem was that missing Rhys did not behave like a problem that could be solved by carrying something heavy.

At breakfast she looked for him before remembering he wasn't there.

She was annoyed with herself for looking. Then she was annoyed that there was no one to see the look and laugh at her for it.

After the morning work she went to the north path, telling herself she wanted to return the striped socks. She had washed them, folded them neatly and put them in her pocket. There was no practical reason to keep them, except that she liked knowing they belonged to him. She had worn them almost every time she went to his cabin, and now that he was gone, the association made her feel foolish.

The door to the Apollo cabin was unlocked. One of the older campers had been assigned to check the stove while the building was mostly empty. Thalia spoke to him briefly, then went inside.

The room looked smaller without Rhys moving around it. The guitar was gone. The workbench had only two unfinished projects on it, a bow grip waiting to be sanded and a small leather case with blue thread still caught beneath the clasp. The slate by the door retained the laundry instructions and the joke she'd written after the Sunday visit.

She stood in front of it for a moment.

Someone had drawn a little arrow beneath her handwriting, pointing toward the words about terrible novices. The addition was not hers or Rhys's. It made her smile. The joke had escaped into camp life without revealing anything important about the private evening that produced it.

She put the socks on the clean shelf beside the workbench. Then, after a moment, she took them back.

She wasn't ready to return them. There was nothing shameful about that. He had told her to keep them for a while, and she had accepted. She could choose when the while ended.

She left the cabin and went down to the lake. The water was grey beneath a sky that threatened snow, and the path was empty enough that she could walk without stopping to greet anyone. She found a dry place on the low wall and sat for a while, watching the reeds move at the edge of the shore.

She remembered Rhys playing the first few notes of the tune he'd written for her. She remembered the way he'd watched her face in the orchard, waiting to see whether she'd allow the photograph. She remembered the smell of bread on Friday evening and his hand at her waist when they kissed goodbye.

These were ordinary memories. She was surprised by how much space they occupied.

At last she went back to Cabin One and took out a sheet of paper.

She had never been a good letter writer. She preferred speaking, arguing, making people look at her while she told them what she thought. A letter gave the other person too much time to interpret every word. She could imagine Rhys reading one of her sentences and smiling at the wrong part, or worse, deciding she had meant something gentle when she'd intended to be sharp.

She wrote the first line.

*Calder,*

Then stopped.

It was too formal. She crossed it out and wrote *Rhys* beneath it. That looked too intimate for the first line of a letter. She stared at the page for a long moment, then decided she was being ridiculous and continued.

*You left yesterday. The range is already worse. Somebody has moved the second target three inches to the left, and I assume you are responsible because you are not here to defend yourself.*

She smiled at the sentence. It sounded like her.

*I found your slate. Somebody added an arrow under the warning. I did not erase it. I am keeping the socks for now, because you said I could, and because there is no point in pretending I don't like them. They are still appalling.*

She stopped again. The next thing she wanted to say was more difficult.

*I miss you. I don't mean that I have nothing else to do. I have plenty to do. I just keep thinking I should tell you things and then remembering that I can't walk up the north path and find you.*

She read it back. The sentence was inelegant, but she couldn't improve it without turning it into something less true.

She continued.

*I liked Saturday morning. I liked not having to get up the minute I woke. I liked that you made breakfast and didn't ask me to fix anything. I also liked the part where you asked me to stay, because I am beginning to understand that it is possible to want somebody without making their time belong to you.*

She put down the pencil. It was the most sentimental thing she'd written in years. She considered tearing the page in half. Instead she folded it and put it in an envelope before she could change her mind.

Later she would add a postscript about his terrible cooking, so the letter would not leave him with the impression that she had become an entirely different person.

At supper she asked an adult Hermes courier when the next outgoing mail would leave camp. He told her the morning. She handed over the envelope, addressed in the careful handwriting she'd used when the words mattered.

That night she lay awake thinking about the letter traveling away from her. She could not retrieve it. She could not explain the tone or soften the sentence about missing him. Rhys would read the words as she'd written them, and she would have to trust him to know what she meant.

It was a small, frightening form of intimacy.

She found the photograph in her book, looked at it once, and put it back. The shared laughter caught in the image had not been made less real by distance. Neither had the desire that remained when she closed her eyes and remembered the feeling of his hands against hers.

Tomorrow she would wake, work, and do everything she usually did. She would also look forward to his answer. There was no reason those things could not coexist.

The first letter arrived on Friday.

Thalia found it tucked under the door of Cabin One, a narrow envelope with her name written in Rhys's slanted block capitals. Someone had added a little note in the corner to explain that the delivery had come with the morning camp mail. She picked it up and stood looking at it for an unreasonable length of time.

She had spent four days imagining what he would write. She had not expected the sight of the envelope to make her nervous.

She took it outside to the porch, sat on the top step, and opened it carefully.

*Thalia,*

*I arrived in one piece, although the train did its best to convince me otherwise. There was a delay at the second stop. A man tried to carry a chair through the narrowest doorway on the carriage and ended up trapping himself between two seats. I am telling you this because you would have had several opinions about his technique, all of them correct.*

Thalia smiled.

*My mother says hello. She has been behaving as if I am both a guest and a repairman. I have moved furniture, checked the back door, and discovered that the kitchen cupboard still refuses to close properly. She says I am not allowed to complain about any of it because I once broke the same cupboard when I was younger. I pointed out that the event was years ago. She said history is not a defense.*

*I showed her the photograph. She said you look like someone who knows exactly what she wants. I didn't argue, although I think she is going to be disappointed when she discovers how often you change your mind about whether you want tea.*

*I miss you.*

The words stood alone on the page. Thalia looked at them for a long moment. There was no joke attached, no elaborate explanation. He had written the truth and let it remain visible.

She continued.

*I miss the way you take over a room without noticing. I miss having somebody tell me when my bowstring is wrong and then accuse me of knowing it all along. I miss the way you kiss me when you're tired of arguing, although I would like to point out that sometimes I am enjoying the argument and you are interrupting excellent work.*

*I don't know how long I'll be here yet. There's more to do than my mother admitted in her letter, but less than I feared when I arrived. I can give you a better answer next week. I will not pretend it's a short visit just to make you feel better. I also don't want you thinking I've forgotten you because I'm fixing a door.*

*Write when you have something to tell me. Write when you're annoyed. Write when you've had a good day. I would rather hear the truth than get a perfectly cheerful letter that doesn't sound like you.*

*Rhys.*

At the bottom he'd drawn a small target and placed an arrow just outside the center.

*I did this on purpose. You can stop judging my technique.*

Thalia laughed aloud.

She read the letter again. The second time she slowed at the part about his mother, picturing him in a small kitchen arguing with a stubborn cupboard. She knew he was busy. She was glad. The letter made it easier to imagine his life continuing outside camp without treating that continuation as evidence that he had become less interested in her.

She folded the page and put it back in the envelope.

For a while she sat on the porch with the letter in her hands. She thought of writing back immediately, before breakfast, to prove that she'd been waiting. Then she realized he had specifically asked her to write when she had something to tell him, and she was entitled to wait until she knew what that thing was.

She took the envelope inside and placed it beside the photograph on her desk.

By afternoon she'd found several things to tell him. A target frame had been repaired badly enough that she'd had to insist it be done again. The weather had turned warmer for two hours and then colder, as if the sky had changed its mind. Someone had left a terrible new message on the Apollo slate. And she had caught herself humming his unfinished melody while carrying a bucket, which was a deeply embarrassing fact she intended to deny if challenged.

She wrote the reply after supper.

*Your mother sounds sensible. I approve of her position on the cupboard. I am glad you got there safely, although I would have paid money to watch you help the man with the chair.*

*The camp is still standing. I have not reorganized your cabin. The socks are still mine for now. The photograph is where I can find it, and I have looked at it exactly once today, which is a very dignified amount.*

She stopped, then added:

*I miss you too. I want to see you when you return, and I don't want you rushing home before you've done what you came to do. Both things are true. You can cope with the contradiction.*

She looked at the sentence. It sounded exactly like something she'd say aloud, and she was pleased with it.

Then she wrote a short final paragraph about the target he'd drawn, explaining in merciless detail why its arrow placement showed poor wrist alignment. She knew he'd laugh. She could picture his face when he reached that part of the letter, and the image pleased her more than she expected.

She sealed the envelope and put it with the outgoing mail.

That night she read his letter once more before turning down the lamp. The words hadn't answered every question about the future. They had done something more modest and more useful: they had made him present for a few minutes without requiring either of them to pretend distance didn't exist.

She could live with that.

Sunday, November twenty-sixth, was when Thalia learned how badly a person could want an ordinary conversation.

There had been no fresh letter in the morning delivery. There was no reason there should have been: the courier came through on an irregular schedule, and Rhys had already written that week. Thalia knew all of that. She checked anyway, twice, and found herself annoyed with the wooden pigeonholes for remaining empty.

By lunchtime the sky had begun to darken. The first flakes fell beyond the pavilion, small and uncertain, disappearing on the grass as soon as they landed. Thalia ate with the other instructors, carried her plate back and went uphill with no particular destination. She had a free afternoon. A month ago she would have treated that as a failure of planning. Now she wanted to enjoy it and found that her thoughts kept walking north without her.

At the fork she stopped beneath the empty lantern bracket. The trail to Rhys's cabin disappeared into the pines. She knew the stones that rocked under a boot, the section where the wind always struck from the lake, and the step that creaked beneath the porch. Knowing the path was not remotely the same thing as having somewhere to go at its end.

She turned toward the amphitheater instead.

The stage was empty, and a low line of snow had begun collecting along the back row. She sat where Rhys had once played his unfinished song and pictured him bending over the guitar, frowning because a note had arrived half a beat early. She remembered the way he'd looked up when she corrected him. They had argued about the count. Then she'd asked him to dance.

She was smiling when a voice said, "That good?"

Annabeth stood three rows above her with a rolled drawing tucked beneath one arm.

"It's cold," Thalia said.

"That wasn't an answer."

"It was an observation."

Annabeth came down the steps and sat, leaving enough room between them for Thalia to decide whether she wanted company. Thalia recognized the gesture and thought, with some irritation, that the entire camp seemed to be learning the same lesson.

"You're missing him," Annabeth said.

"Apparently everyone has decided I'm transparent."

"You've checked the mail three times."

"Twice."

"Three. You went back after lunch."

Thalia looked at her. Annabeth looked pleasantly unmoved.

"All right," Thalia said. "I miss him."

Her sister nodded as though the admission required neither congratulations nor a lecture.

"Is he all right?" she asked.

"He's at home. Helping his mother. Fighting with a cupboard that has apparently held a grudge for years."

"Sounds personal."

"It is. The cupboard has a strong case."

Annabeth smiled. The silence afterward was comfortable enough that Thalia didn't need to fill it. Snow gathered on the edges of the steps and vanished wherever a boot had left a warm mark in the wood.

"He's coming back?" Annabeth asked.

"He wants to. We don't have a date."

"That sounds difficult."

"It's not the end of the world."

"I didn't say it was."

Thalia glanced sideways. "You sound like him."

"Then he sounds sensible."

"Occasionally."

Her sister shifted the drawing into her lap. She had brought a new plan for the east wall, a design with carefully marked drainage channels and a small note that someone needed to check the soil before the first hard freeze. Thalia looked at it out of habit. Then she handed it back.

"You're allowed to go work on that," she said.

"I know."

"And I'm allowed to sit here and be unpleasant."

"I know that too."

Annabeth stood. Before she went, she reached down and squeezed Thalia's shoulder, a brief touch that reminded her of the little girl who used to fall asleep against her on cold nights.

"Write him another letter," Annabeth said.

"I already did."

"Then tell him something new."

Thalia watched her go. It was such ordinary advice that it was almost insulting. It was also right.

She returned to Cabin One and found the first letter from Rhys beneath her book. She reread the part about the man with the chair and laughed again, less because the story was funny than because she could hear how Rhys would have told it aloud, carefully setting up the ridiculous detail before leaving space for her to interrupt.

She took out a fresh sheet of paper.

*Rhys,*

*Snow began this afternoon. Not proper snow yet. The kind that thinks it can make an impression without committing to anything. I thought of you because the north path looks different when nobody's walking it beside me.*

She paused. Too solemn. She could hear herself trying to make the weather carry feelings it had not volunteered to carry. She almost crossed the sentence out. Then she left it alone. He had asked her to tell the truth, not to be clever every minute.

*I went to the amphitheater. Annabeth caught me sitting there and demanded an explanation. I told her I missed you. She took the information much better than I did.*

*Do you remember the first night you played that tune for me? I keep hearing the part you got wrong, which seems unjust because I know you have fixed it. I can hear the corrected part if I concentrate. I think I prefer it when you are there to make the mistake yourself.*

She stopped and tapped the pencil against the edge of the desk. There was something else she wanted to tell him. It was harder, though not because it was embarrassing. She had been thinking about how easily he had told her he wanted her there, how naturally his hand had found hers when they were walking, and how often she'd answered an affectionate remark with an insult because the insult gave her somewhere safe to stand.

She turned the page.

*I have been remembering Friday night. Not only because I miss kissing you, although I do, and you may feel extremely pleased with yourself about that for approximately five seconds. I keep remembering the part after supper when we stopped trying to make the evening special. I liked that you were happy for me to sit close without asking what was wrong. I liked being able to reach for you and finding you already turning toward me.*

*I want that again.*

There. No evasive joke at the end. She felt oddly exposed to a piece of paper. She could write something sharp now, make the line less earnest. Instead she allowed the sentence to stand alone.

Outside, the snowfall thickened. Someone called up the hill for the lamps to be lit early. The camp was continuing its winter work, oblivious to the fact that Thalia had written four words she might once have spent an entire week avoiding.

She finished with a note about the target frames and a warning that his mother was entitled to make him repair every cupboard in the house. Then she folded the letter and sealed it.

At the door, she hesitated. The outgoing-mail box stood down by the pavilion. If she posted the letter now, she couldn't take it back to improve the ending or disguise the part where she'd admitted what she missed. She put it inside her coat and started downhill.

She met the courier near the bend, a Hermes woman about her own age carrying a satchel and complaining cheerfully about the weather. Thalia handed over the envelope. The courier checked the address, slipped it inside and asked whether anything else needed sending.

"No," Thalia said.

The woman moved on.

Thalia stood in the path a moment, then turned toward Cabin One. It had taken a considerable amount of courage to say something unremarkable, and she was annoyed by that. She was also pleased. Rhys had written about wanting her. Now she had written about wanting him. Neither of them had been obliged to make the statement; that was what made it worth keeping.

When she reached the porch, the snow was finally beginning to settle. She paused and looked north, toward the path she knew so well.

He wasn't there. She still wanted him. Neither fact had to spoil the evening.

She went inside, shook the snow from her coat and began looking for the guitar she'd once borrowed from the music shed. She could remember enough of his melody to attempt the first phrase. The second would take some work. She suspected he'd have an objection ready when he heard what she'd done to it.

The thought made her laugh. She sat down with the instrument, set her fingers carefully on the strings and began.

## December 1: Things a Letter Cannot Do

Rhys's second letter arrived with the first serious snow.

The camp had spent the morning clearing the steps and sweeping the roofs of buildings too small to justify climbing onto. The snow packed into a dense, quiet layer beneath Thalia's boots, and the air had that peculiar stillness that made voices sound much nearer than they were. She'd spent two hours teaching a beginner's lesson indoors and was trying not to be irritated by the necessity. It was perfectly possible to teach archery without being outside. She disliked the compromise anyway.

The courier found her at the hall.

"Grace. Mail."

Thalia looked up from the bow she was checking. The courier was holding an envelope between two fingers, its corner damp from snow. She handed it over and continued toward the next building without lingering to see whether Thalia would open it.

Thalia turned the envelope over.

The handwriting was unmistakable. He had written her name a little too large for the available space, and the last letter nearly ran into the stamp. There was a faint smudge along one edge, probably made by the heel of his hand. She could remember that hand moving across a page in the cabin, pausing when he ran out of room and beginning another line beneath it.

She put the envelope inside her coat and finished the lesson.

It took an astonishing amount of discipline.

At supper she carried the letter to a quiet end of the pavilion, then decided she didn't want to read it in public. She took her food to Cabin One instead. Annabeth was sitting at the table, working through a set of measurements with a ruler and a stub of pencil.

"Mail?" she asked.

"Yes."

"From Rhys?"

"Your career in espionage is going very well."

Annabeth smiled and returned to the measurements.

Thalia sat on her bed and opened the letter.

It wasn't especially romantic at first. Rhys had spent the previous three days arguing with a plumber, had repaired a cupboard that had promptly developed a different problem, and had discovered that his mother possessed the same impossible standard for the arrangement of kitchen knives that Thalia possessed for target distances. He described an evening spent playing his unfinished song while his mother sat beside the stove and corrected his timing despite being unable to read a line of music.

Thalia laughed.

Then the letter changed.

*I keep finding things I would have told you if you were here. I could probably fill an entire notebook with them, but I suspect you would accuse me of wasting paper.*

*Yesterday I stood in the doorway with the last piece of the repair list in my hand and realized I'd finished the jobs I could do myself. The remaining appointments belong to other people. I wanted to tell you because you would have immediately ordered me to stop inventing new responsibilities.*

*You would have been right.*

She smiled despite herself.

*I think I can come back around the middle of the month. I haven't booked anything yet. My mother wants me to stay another week while she settles a few matters, and I want to be certain I can leave without making it harder for her. I'll tell you when I know.*

Thalia read those lines twice. She didn't mind that there was no exact date. Not in principle. She was less pleased by how quickly her imagination had begun counting days.

*I wish you were here tonight. Not because I've had a terrible day. It wasn't terrible. I'd just like to hear you complain about my guitar, eat something with you, and have you kiss me before we go to sleep. That last part is proving particularly difficult to replace with a letter.*

She read the sentence again.

It was direct, the way he could be when he chose to stop hiding behind a joke. There was an unexpected intimacy in seeing the desire written down, without his face or his voice there to soften it. She could not distract him by kissing him. She had to sit with the words.

She liked them.

At the bottom he had added one more line.

*Your turn. Tell me something you haven't told anyone else this week.*

Thalia set the letter down. She thought about writing immediately, but the question held her longer than she expected. There were things she had not told anyone: how often she'd looked up from supper because a familiar laugh sounded almost like his; how she'd held the photograph beneath the lamp and imagined his hands warm against her face; how the first morning without him had made her feel foolishly bereft even while the rest of her life continued exactly as it should.

She couldn't fit all of that into an ordinary account of her week. She didn't need to.

Annabeth stood from the table and stretched.

"Is he all right?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"He's dealing with repairs."

"That sounds like him."

Thalia looked up. "You sound fond of him."

"I like people who don't treat you as if you're difficult just because you disagree with them."

Thalia opened her mouth and closed it again.

"You're saying I disagree too much."

"I'm saying I know you."

Annabeth gathered her papers. At the door she paused.

"Tell him the truth," she said.

"You have no idea what I'm writing."

"No. But you looked like you were deciding whether to."

The door closed behind her. Thalia sat with the letter in her lap and considered the infuriating fact that her sister was often right.

She found a clean sheet.

*Rhys,*

*Your mother sounds as though she has the right idea about the kitchen knives. You should probably listen to her.*

*The snow has finally arrived. I am trying not to interfere with the people whose job it is to clear the paths. The word "trying" matters in that sentence.*

She paused, then continued.

*You asked me to tell you something nobody else knows this week.*

*I kept the shirt you lent me on the first Sunday. I know you know that, because it was in my bag when I left and you watched me take it. What you don't know is that sometimes I put it on when I read before bed. It smells less like your cabin now. I am annoyed by that. I don't especially want a new shirt. I want the old one to smell like you again.*

She stared at the words.

It was not an elegant confession. It was not even a flattering one. She'd never expected to become a person who admitted such things. But the thought had been hers for several days, and writing it down felt better than constructing a joke to hide it.

She wrote another line.

*I also miss kissing you. I won't pretend I don't. The exact things I miss are private, and I intend to remind you of them when you come back.*

She considered crossing that out. Instead she smiled, a little embarrassed, and left the sentence alone. He would know what she meant. More importantly, he would know how pleased she was to mean it.

Outside the cabin, the snow had stopped. Thalia could hear someone knocking it from a roof with the flat side of a shovel. She moved to the small window and looked at the path to the north. It was white now, interrupted only by the dark posts that carried the lamps. The cabin where she had spent so many evenings stood hidden beyond the trees.

She took the letter with her when she went to bed. It had been a long day. Tomorrow would bring another lesson, another stack of bows that needed attention and perhaps a new problem Annabeth would insist they solve before supper. She wanted those things to matter as they always had. She also wanted Rhys to come back, to open the north cabin door and look pleased to find her there.

She set the letter on the table beside her photograph. Then she took the borrowed shirt from beneath her pillow and pulled it over her head before turning out the lamp.

She was not trying to live in a memory. She was simply allowing herself to miss someone she intended to see again.
