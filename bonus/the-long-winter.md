## Bonus Interlude: The Long Winter

Monday, the twenty-third of October. The wind had come around to the north with the cold riding it, and the whole hill had the locked-down feel of a place that had decided to be winter and was not going to be argued with about it.

Thalia Grace walked the west path alone with her boots loud on the gravel. She passed the ninth amber lantern, and the tenth on the hook by the Cabin Three porch, with her hands in the pockets of a coat that was not hers and her breath going up white into the dark. The coat was Percy's. She had taken it off the hook by the door on her way out of Cabin Three without asking, and Clarisse had seen her do it and said nothing, because Clarisse understood that there are nights when a person needs to leave a warm room and go and stand in the cold outside a door that is not theirs.

Earlier that morning, she had made the mistake of believing that a problem became less real if she gave it a sufficiently crowded schedule.

At seven, she had helped carry the last crates of winter arrowheads out of the dry store. At half past seven she had argued with a son of Hermes about where the straw targets should go when the north field iced over. At eight, with the sun barely clear of the trees, she had taken her place on the shooting line and discovered that the heel was worse in the cold.

She put her weight on it anyway.

The first arrow struck two inches outside the circle. She pulled another from the quiver and tried to compensate by tightening the shoulder on her drawing side. The second arrow landed even farther left.

"You're moving before you release," Annabeth said.

"I can see where the arrows went."

"Then stop acting surprised by it."

Her sister was sitting on the fence with a field notebook balanced on one knee, supposedly checking the dimensions of the winter target shed. She had a pencil tucked into her braid and had drawn a very good plan of the shed. She had also watched every arrow Thalia had shot.

Thalia set a third arrow against the string. "Don't you have a building to annoy?"

"I do. Unfortunately, somebody put the targets in front of it."

"I can move."

"That's what I'm concerned about."

Thalia turned to give her the look that had stopped Annabeth talking when she was seven. It had not worked for two years, and there was no reason to expect it would work now. Annabeth kept her pencil on the page and met the look without blinking.

Across the field, Rhys had finished examining a younger camper's grip. He started toward them, saw Thalia's expression, and took the longer route around the equipment bench.

"Coward," Thalia called.

"Professional judgment," he called back.

He carried three practice bows across to the novices rather than come between the Grace sisters. That ought to have been irritating. Instead, Thalia found herself watching the way he gave each bow to its new owner: one instruction, one chance to try, no fuss when somebody got it wrong.

Annabeth followed her gaze. "He isn't going to argue you into having a working foot."

"Neither are you."

"No. Chiron might, though. He's fairly good at feet."

Thalia loosed the third arrow. She knew before it landed that she had twisted the heel again. Pain went up the back of her leg, sudden and hot enough to make her grip the bow too hard.

Annabeth was off the fence before the arrow hit the target.

"Don't," Thalia said.

"I haven't done anything."

"You're about to."

"I was going to pick up the arrows you dropped."

Thalia looked down. Two arrows had slipped from the loose side of her quiver. Annabeth collected them without touching her, then set them carefully on the bench instead of returning them to Thalia's hand.

"You used to tell me the difference between being brave and being stupid," Annabeth said. "It was usually right after I'd done something you didn't like."

"I remember."

"I remember too."

That last sentence struck differently from the others. Annabeth had memories of a sister who had raised her, a sisterhood written into this version of their childhood that the younger girl could recognize through stories and habits but not always through her own recollection. Thalia had spent years being the person who remembered for both of them. Sometimes Annabeth's quiet insistence sounded like a challenge to that job. Sometimes it sounded like a girl trying to do it herself.

Thalia lowered the bow.

"You're allowed to be annoyed with me," Annabeth said. "I just don't want to watch you make it permanent."

"I don't need an audience for every stupid decision."

"Then stop making them on the public range."

Annabeth picked up her notebook, checked the pencil was still in her braid, and headed down the hill toward the construction shed. She did not look back. Thalia wanted to call her back and also wanted fifteen minutes without being somebody's older sister. She was old enough to know that both could be true. It did not help her decide which one to act on.

She put the bow away after the fourth arrow. It was a small concession, and she hated how much effort it took.

---

By lunch the argument had become public without either sister saying another word about it. Thalia went into Cabin Three for a length of cord Clarisse had promised her and found the winter work table covered in measurements, cloth, and a formidable pile of things that had been washed but not mended.

Silena sat cross-legged on the bench, replacing buttons on a child's coat. Percy was holding the free end of a strip of canvas and looking personally wronged by its tendency to curl.

"You're late," Clarisse said, not looking up from the list.

"For what?"

"For being useful. Hold that end."

Thalia took the canvas from Percy. He flexed his fingers in relief.

"I've been holding that for eighteen minutes."

"You've been complaining about holding it for eighteen minutes," Silena corrected.

"That's how I measure time."

They were making a removable cover for the west-end awning, or trying to. The existing awning had been repaired twice and still sagged toward the corner every time it rained. Silena wanted the cover to come down before a hard snow, so nobody would have to climb a ladder on frozen boards to rescue the entire structure.

Thalia gave the fabric a closer look. "That seam won't take the strain."

"It will if the tension goes to the cord, not the stitching," Silena said. "Charlie checked it."

"Of course Charlie checked it."

Silena didn't blush. She threaded the needle with a sharp motion. "Yes. He knows how to fix things. It's a very attractive quality in somebody helping with the winter work."

Percy nearly let go of his corner laughing.

Clarisse rapped the table with a knuckle. "Less commentary. More canvas."

Thalia held the line steady while Silena pinned the edge. The work had a useful rhythm to it, even if the ache in Thalia's heel made standing still unpleasant. Percy told an elaborate story about a Hermes camper who had mistaken roofing tar for stove polish. Clarisse corrected the exaggerations only when they interfered with the important point, which was that the tar had to be scraped off a door. Nobody asked Thalia about her foot.

Then she stepped down to reach the end of the table, shifted her weight, and caught herself on its corner.

Clarisse's pencil stopped moving.

"Sit," she said.

"I'm fine."

"That wasn't a question."

"Are you going to start issuing medical orders now?"

"No. I'm going to stop you tearing my table in half because you won't sit on a bench."

Thalia sat. Silena passed the pinned canvas across the table so the work could reach her instead of making her reach it. The three of them kept arguing about the seams and whether the temporary covering would survive the first heavy wind. It was almost worse than a lecture. Nobody made her admit why they had rearranged the room.

When Percy went to put the canvas away, he noticed her looking at his coat on the hook.

"Take it if you need it," he said.

"I wasn't asking."

"That sounds like a yes."

"It's a better coat than mine."

"It's also three sizes too big."

"Then nobody will recognize me."

He grinned. "Try not to get accused of stealing from yourself. There's paperwork."

She stayed another hour and helped Silena finish two of the long seams while Clarisse dealt with the supply list. By the time they folded the cover and put it aside for the next dry day, Thalia had missed her own self-imposed appointment to inspect the fence by the north arena. It hadn't fallen down without her. This seemed an unreasonable development.

At the door, Silena looked up from her sewing. "Annabeth went to the library."

"I didn't ask."

"I know. She took the shed drawings."

"Good."

"She was angry, by the way."

"I gathered that."

Silena pulled the thread through the last button and bit it off. "She's allowed."

"So am I."

"Also true."

Nobody softened the answer for her. That was one of the things Thalia liked about Cabin Three, and on this particular afternoon it was one of the things she resented.

---

The argument with Annabeth found her again before supper.

Thalia was returning the borrowed cord to the shed when she saw her sister kneeling beside the short retaining wall, holding a ruler against a loose stone. The notebook was open, one corner weighted with a pebble. She was testing where snowmelt would go once the ground froze and thawed again. Her glove was damp clear through.

"You're going to lose a finger," Thalia said.

Annabeth didn't look up. "Not from a wet glove."

"It will freeze."

"In the next ten minutes? Impressive weather forecasting."

Thalia set the cord beside the wall. She wanted to say she hadn't meant to sound like that. Instead, she crouched to inspect the stone, promptly discovered that crouching pulled on her heel, and swore under her breath.

Annabeth put down the ruler.

"No," Thalia said. "Don't give me the face."

"I haven't moved my face."

"It's your whole face."

Annabeth got up and brushed the dirt off her knees. "You don't have to tell me what happened to your foot. I can see it. I also don't have to spend every day pretending I can't."

"I know you can see it."

"Then why does it make you so angry when I say so?"

Thalia had a dozen answers, most of them unhelpful. Because she could remember Annabeth small enough to carry. Because a skinned knee used to be something Thalia could fix with water and an old shirt. Because there were years when refusing to fall down had seemed like the only useful thing she could do. Because now her sister noticed everything and did not wait for permission to notice.

"Because you sound like me," Thalia said at last.

Annabeth paused. "Is that supposed to be insulting?"

"Sometimes."

That got the smallest possible smile.

Thalia sat on the low wall. Annabeth stayed standing, but she didn't leave.

"I hate not being able to trust my own foot," Thalia said. "And I hate somebody watching to see if I trust it wrong."

"I can't promise not to notice."

"I wasn't asking you to."

"Good. Because that would be a stupid promise."

Thalia looked down at the cord, at the dirt ground into the woven strands, and made herself say the thing she had come down the hill avoiding. "I'll go to Chiron tomorrow."

Annabeth took a moment before answering. She was learning, slowly and with great difficulty, that not every victory required a closing argument.

"All right," she said. "I'll give him the shed measurements then. Two reasons to be in one place."

"You're not coming to supervise."

"I have a drawing to show him."

"You've always got a drawing."

"I've always got a building that someone insisted didn't need fixing."

Thalia let her have that one. Annabeth took back the ruler and started down the path with the notebook under her arm. They were still angry at each other, just not in quite the same way.

By six, when Thalia borrowed Percy's coat and stepped out of Cabin Three, the injury had become something she was actually going to do something about. It remained a poor explanation for why she was walking toward Rhys Calder's light. She had no better explanation ready.

Someone had known about the heel since Sunday. Percy had said it aloud at the fire ring, with no ceremony or accusation, and Thalia had spent the next day discovering how much she disliked being observed when she couldn't do anything about the observation. Now the north path ended at Rhys's cabin, with lamplight caught in the quilted window. She could still turn around. She had promised Annabeth an examination tomorrow; she had promised Rhys nothing. That, she was beginning to suspect, was why coming here mattered.

There was a particular humiliation in realizing she had been flirting with him for almost six weeks and had somehow managed to convince herself she was conducting a professional rivalry.

She'd first noticed the problem when he corrected her elbow.

It had been a Thursday with rain threatening, one of the afternoons that made the archery field smell of turned earth. She was halfway through a round, shooting well enough to be angry that she wasn't shooting better, when Rhys walked behind the line with a clipboard tucked beneath his arm and paused at her station.

"You're compensating," he'd said.

"I know."

"Then why are you doing it?"

"Because if I stop, the arrow lands in the wrong county."

He hadn't laughed. That was the first thing that caught her attention. Most of the boys who tried to advise Thalia laughed before she was finished talking, whether she was funny or not, as if anything other than agreement might result in a spear through the chest.

Rhys had stepped back behind the safety line. "Do you want me to show you?"

Thalia had looked him over, from his muddy boots to the ridiculous clipboard, and decided he was probably an irritating person.

"Go ahead."

He'd picked up a spare bow, set his feet, and deliberately reproduced her mistake. The arrow had landed left. Then he'd adjusted his stance and shot again, correcting without a speech. He passed the bow back to her.

"That's the difference. Your shoulder's doing the work your feet should be doing."

"My feet are doing plenty."

"That's what worries me."

She had expected him to stay and watch. Instead he went to help a camper whose string had slipped. The next time Thalia shot, her arrow struck where she'd intended. She hated that. More precisely, she hated how much she wanted to call him back and show him.

By the end of the afternoon she had invented a reason to walk past his bench.

"Your feet are too narrow," she said.

Rhys looked down at his boots. "Excuse me?"

"When you shoot. You're making yourself less stable than you need to be."

He glanced at the empty practice line. "I didn't shoot while you were watching."

"Yes, you did."

"When?"

"Before lunch. Near the second target."

He paused, and there was something briefly, annoyingly delighted in his face. "You've been keeping track of me."

"I have eyes."

"Apparently very thorough ones."

"Are you going to fix your stance?"

"Probably. Show me what you mean."

She'd demonstrated, expecting an argument. He'd stepped into the corrected position, considered the change, and nodded. There had been none of the wounded pride she might have enjoyed sparring with. He had asked a second question, an intelligent one about the angle of his back foot, and for the next ten minutes they had talked about shooting without either trying to prove the other incompetent.

It was the kind of conversation Thalia had been missing without realizing it. Competence didn't have to be a struggle for rank. A person could take her seriously without making her responsible for their feelings about it.

That should have been enough to make him interesting.

It wasn't the whole problem.

The next week he caught a falling target frame with one hand while she braced the other end. Someone had installed the hinge backward, and the frame had started folding down toward the youngest campers. Thalia had been first to see it. Rhys was first to move. They managed to stop it between them, then spent an hour taking the whole thing apart while the instructor explained to Chiron that the hinge had been perfectly sound that morning.

Rhys worked close beside her without hovering. His shoulders strained beneath an old grey shirt when they lifted the heavy crosspiece back into position. She noticed that, and then noticed herself noticing it, which was irritating.

"Hold this," she'd said, passing him a bolt.

"Yes, captain."

"Don't call me captain."

"Would you prefer 'your majesty'?"

"I would prefer you stop talking."

He'd grinned. "I'll put that on the list."

She had spent the following hour being considerably sharper with him than the crooked hinge deserved. At the end he wiped the grease from his hands and offered her his clean rag, which was a small courtesy she disliked for the unreasonable reason that she wanted him to do it again.

The first time they'd been alone without an excuse had happened by accident. The field lights had gone out while they were sorting equipment, and a late shower had trapped them beneath the narrow shelter by the tool shed. The rain made a sheet between them and the path.

"Well," he said. "We're stuck."

"It's rain. Not a siege."

"You're welcome to test it."

She stayed where she was.

He leaned against the post with his arms folded. Outside the shelter, drops bounced off the top of the water barrel. The rain made it possible to be silent without inviting interrogation.

"How old were you when you started?" he asked.

"Started what?"

"Archery."

"Young."

"Useful level of detail."

"I was a child. There was a bow. Eventually somebody decided I shouldn't be allowed to shoot toward the cabins."

"Seems reasonable."

"I disagreed at the time."

He laughed. It was different from the laugh he used on the field, softer and a little surprised. Thalia turned toward him before she could help herself.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You laughed."

"You were describing yourself at six. I was imagining the camp safety report."

"Seven," she said.

"Even worse."

"And it was one incident."

"An important distinction."

She could have told him about the night she had taught Annabeth to brace a toy bow, about how the smaller girl had insisted on holding the string all by herself and nearly fallen backward. She could have told him about the things she had learned too young, before anyone had made them into organized camp lessons. Instead she stood beside him and watched the rain.

He didn't pry.

Another minute passed.

"You left for a while," she said.

Rhys nodded. "I did."

"Why come back?"

"Because there are people here I like."

"Very diplomatic."

"And because the winters are short on competent instructors."

"Also diplomatic."

"And because I missed it."

"What part?"

He glanced toward the dark range. "Getting to be good at something without pretending it solves everything else."

She had looked at him then, really looked, and found no performance to argue with. He was nineteen. He had a life outside the valley, a mother he wrote to, responsibilities he had gone home to shoulder and would shoulder again. He had chosen to come back and wasn't pretending the choice had made his life simple.

There were things she might have wanted to ask. None of them felt suitable for the shelter or the rain.

When the shower eased he pushed away from the post.

"I'd better get the targets covered."

"Before the next siege?"

"Exactly."

She followed him out and helped, and for several days afterward was profoundly irritating to everyone who asked why she had stayed late at the range.

The humiliating part was that nobody had asked.

After that came the little things. A cup of cider left on the bench when the evening sessions ran long. A joke about her insistence on counting every arrow in a quiver even when the number was written on the case. A walk back from the field in which they argued for twenty minutes about which of them had made a worse first impression.

"You told me my feet were wrong," he said.

"They were wrong."

"Your entire opening statement."

"I like to be efficient."

"And here I thought you were desperate to talk to me."

She had stopped in the middle of the path.

Rhys had stopped too. The smile disappeared when he saw her face.

"That was a joke," he said. "Too much?"

She might have punished him for asking. She might have made the rest of the walk unpleasant because she was embarrassed, which was sometimes her preferred method of managing embarrassment.

Instead she said, "Not entirely."

"Not entirely what?"

"Wrong."

He looked at her for a second. She could hear the loose lantern hook tapping against its bracket above them. Then he smiled, slowly enough that she had time to wish she hadn't spoken and to be glad she had.

"Good," he said.

That was all. He didn't make a move, didn't claim an invitation, and didn't turn her confession into a victory. He walked the rest of the way with her, close enough that their coats sometimes brushed, and said good night at the fork.

The night after that, she dreamed of kissing him.

She woke furious with herself, which did not improve the situation. Neither did seeing him that morning at the range, lifting a hand in greeting as if the world hadn't rearranged itself overnight.

She began taking the longer path to breakfast. It wasn't remotely convenient. Twice she encountered him on it anyway.

One afternoon he'd said, "You know you can just come over if you want something."

"What makes you think I want something?"

"Because you've been standing at the far end of the range glaring at the back of my head for ten minutes."

"I've been checking your form."

"With your bow unstrung?"

She'd looked at the bow in her hand, discovered he was right, and turned to leave.

He hadn't called after her.

It was the restraint she couldn't stop thinking about. When she wanted to be difficult, he was perfectly willing to let her be difficult. But he wasn't interested in pulling the answer out of her. He would take what she chose to say and leave the rest alone.

And tonight, finally, she'd made a choice.


At the last bend in the path, Thalia realized she had been arranging reasons to see him for weeks.

She could tell herself that the bow needed restringing or that somebody had to settle the argument about the north range. Once, she had spent half an hour in his company discussing a warped target stand neither of them was responsible for fixing. He must have known. The stand had been perfectly straight.

Tonight she had no excuse left. The heel was hurting, but Chiron could deal with the heel. What brought her up this hill was the memory of Rhys leaning against the equipment shed with his sleeves pushed up, waiting until she'd finished speaking before answering. She had been thinking about his hands, his mouth, and the fact that he never tried to seize a moment that hadn't been offered. At the range they were both perfectly capable of being difficult. Alone, she suspected he might be dangerous in an altogether different way.

That last thought made her stop hard enough to jar her bad foot.

"Idiot," she muttered, though it wasn't clear which of them she meant.

She had wanted people before. Wanting Rhys felt less familiar because she didn't have to turn it into an argument. If she went to him, it would be because she chose to. He could still say no. They could both change their minds. That ought to have made walking up the steps simple. Instead she spent an unreasonable amount of time examining the porch railing as though it might contain instructions.

She wasn't frightened of him. That was worth being exact about. She was frightened of the possibility that she might enjoy being wanted without first proving she'd earned it.

Then she heard him laugh at something through the door, a brief private laugh, followed by the scrape of a chair. She knew that laugh. She wanted to hear it closer.

She climbed the last step.

She stood outside the door for a long moment. The cold came up the hill off the lake and moved the pines. She could see the light through the quilted window, and the shape of him moving around inside, and she thought about turning around. She thought about it seriously, the way a person thinks about a thing they are afraid of, and she stood in the cold and weighed it — the going-back, the going-on — and the going-on won, the way it always did, because she was Thalia Grace and she had never once in her life walked away from a door she had decided to walk through.

She did not knock. She had decided, somewhere on the path, that she was not going to knock. She opened the door and went in and shut it behind her and stood on the mat with the cold still in her coat and her breath going up and her heart going like a thing that had been let out of a room it had been kept in too long.

Rhys Calder's cabin had a stove with a real chimney and eight cots and one window with a quilt hung over it, and it smelled of varnish and bowstring wax and the particular green smell of Apollo money — laurel cuttings drying from the rafters, somebody's incense gone cold in a dish, autumn apples somebody's mother had mailed. He was sitting on the floor with his back against a cot, working a new wrapping onto a bow limb with both hands, and he did not look surprised to see her in the doorway. He set the bow down, and he looked at her, and he did not say anything for a moment, which was the first thing she noticed about him that was different.

"You've got a bad heel," he said.

"You've got a door I'm standing in."

"I asked you to come by so I could say it to you instead of writing it on the slate." He got up. In the lamplight he was exactly what he was — nineteen, broad through the shoulders in the way of the boys who worked with their hands, still a little surprised about it, a camp shirt gone soft as breath with washing. "It's been three weeks. You favor it going downhill."

"I favor nothing. I'm a professional."

"You're eighteen, and you do it on the downhill from the north ridge, and I watch you do it because I'm the one who made you shoot on it." He came and stood in front of her and put one hand carefully at her waist and the other up to her jaw, and he looked at her the way he looked at things he had been waiting for. "Come in. Sit down. I've got a heater and I've got the good cider."

She came in. She sat. It was easier than deciding to.

He had the small stove going and a mug of something that steamed, and he gave her the chair with the cushion on it and sat himself on the edge of the cot, and they talked about nothing for twenty minutes the way people do when they are circling. The winter cabin assignments. The forge's new bellows. Whether the Tempest Hall counted as swimming season — rule five, they agreed, meant it counted. The letter his mother had sent with four dollars in it and a note that said *do not lose the four dollars.* The slate by the door with the laundry instructions and the camper gossip written over it in three different hands, and the top line, in a square careful hand she did not recognize, said *ETERNITY. THAT'S ALL.* He caught her looking at it.

"I wrote that," he said. "I was seventeen and stupid and I meant it."

"It's still there."

"It's still there and I still mean it." He grinned, but only a little. "I've been back six weeks, Thalia. I'd like to stop being careful with you, if that's allowed."

The stove ticked. Laurel rustled in the rafters. Outside, the wind came down off the ridge and went through the pines like water through reeds, and she sat with her hands around the mug and thought about being somebody's somebody and how long it had been since anyone had asked her a plain question and waited.

"How careful have you been?" she said.

"Extremely. It's been annoying."

"Show me."

He set his own mug down. "Stand up," he said, and she stood, and he stepped in close, close enough that the front of his shirt had the stove's heat in it, and put one hand carefully at her waist and the other up to her jaw, and kissed her — unhurried, warm, the kind of kiss that waits to be invited into anything else. She kissed him back with her mouth open and her hand at the button of his collar, and the kiss went on, and the world narrowed to the heat of him and the rasp of the cold at the window and her own pulse in her throat.

He broke it by degrees, the way the good ones do — half an inch, foreheads touching, breath shared. "Well?" he said.

"Your elbow correction was better than that," she said, and heard her own voice come out shaky, and was furious at it for exactly as long as it took him to laugh — low, quick, delighted.

"Before I get any better at it," he said, "I want to ask you something properly, and then I want you to answer it without a joke."

"Ask."

"I'd like tonight to be not careful. The two of us. You're eighteen. I'm nineteen. We're both adults and it's October and the heater's going." His thumb moved once against her waistband, warm through the shirt. "I want you in my bed tonight. I want to know what you feel like when you're not being polite. If you want that too, say yes. If you want to sit and drink cider and talk about the winter list, say that instead, and I'll never make it weird."

Thalia looked at him. This boy who had gone away and come back broad. Who corrected her stance on a cold field until her arms remembered it. Who had been careful for six weeks like a man, and who was now standing in front of her with his heart going as plainly as hers was.

"Yes," she said, with more certainty than she'd expected. She took his hand off her waist and put it flat on the front of her shirt, over her heart, and held it there, and looked at him, and stepped in until there was no space left between them, and kissed him again — hard this time, open-mouthed, her hand fisted in the front of his shirt — and he got it, the way he always got it, and his arms came around her and he kissed her back, and he understood that she was choosing him, not surrendering the choice.

---

She learned that he was warm everywhere, all over, the way fire is warm. That his hands shook when they went to the buttons of her shirt, and that she noticed, and that noticing made something in her chest let go of a thing it had been holding for years. They undressed each other slowly and not smoothly, with laughter for the sleeve that got stuck, with seriousness when the shirt came off and he looked at her like she was the whole north field in March. He touched her like someone who had memorized her by watching — the place under her collarbone, the line of her spine, the small scar on her hip from when she was nine and Beryl's garden had a fence with nails in it. She gave him back the same honesty, his shoulders under her hands, the new planes of him, the sound he made when she found the hinge of his jaw, his name in her mouth like a promise she was testing.

"Bed," he said finally, against her temple. "Now."

His cot had a camp mattress and a real blanket that had been washed that week, and they got under it and fit together with the plainness of two people who knew what they were doing and did it anyway, slowly, on purpose, the heater ticking and the quilt making a cave against the dark. He kissed her mouth, her throat, the place over her heart, and stopped there, breathing hard, and she pulled him up and rolled him onto his back and straddled him, and he looked up at her with his hands at her hips and his breath going ragged, and she reached between them and took him in her hand and guided him to her and sank down onto him, slow, until he was seated to the root, and she gasped, and he gripped her hips, and she said, "Don't stop," and he said, "I won't," and she didn't.

She rode him slow at first, then faster, and he met her, and the cot creaked against the floorboards, and there was nothing gentle in it at all. It was wet and blunt and desperate and perfect, her hands flat on his chest, his hands on her hips, the slap of skin and their voices in the warm dark of the cabin. She felt the orgasm building from the soles of her feet, the current running up through her, and when it hit she came with his name in her mouth, her back arching off him, her nails in his chest, and he followed a moment later with his hands tight on her hips, three hard jolts and a groan so deep she felt it in her chest.

Afterward she collapsed onto him, both of them shaking, and he held her with his arms around her back and his face in her hair, and they lay like that for a long time, not talking, just breathing, and then he said, "Your heel," and she said, "I know," and he said, "I've got a splint," and she said, "I don't need a splint," and he said, "I'm going to make you see somebody about it," and she said, "Fine," and they lay in the dark and listened to the stove tick and the frost come down on the window, and it was warm, and it was enough, and it was the first time.

She had expected that wanting someone would be the difficult part. It turned out that there was a different difficulty in being there afterward with no immediate reason to leave.

Rhys had pushed the blanket up around her shoulders, and she was examining the small cut on his knuckle because looking directly at him made her feel as though he'd notice every thought she hadn't finished having. The cut was nearly healed. She studied it anyway.

"You keep doing that," he said.

"Doing what?"

"Finding something on me that needs fixing."

"It's a cut. You're an Apollo kid. You could heal it."

"It's a scratch. I could also let it heal like everybody else."

"Shocking disregard for your gifts."

He shifted closer, and she did not pretend she hadn't noticed. Their elbows met beneath the blanket. She liked the simple physical fact of him beside her more than she'd been prepared to admit. He could be irritating without effort; so could she. There was relief in discovering that neither quality vanished when the door was closed.

"Can I say something without you making fun of me?" he asked.

"Unlikely, but you can try."

"I liked tonight."

That was so plain that it caught her off guard. She had been expecting an invitation, perhaps a joke she could meet with a sharper one. Instead he had made a statement that required nothing except an answer.

"Good," she said.

"That's all?"

"Are you going to demand a written review?"

"No. I'd just like to know whether you liked it too."

She lowered her eyes to their hands. It would have been easy to kiss him and avoid the question. She did want to kiss him. That was part of the trouble.

"Yes," she said. "I liked tonight. Quite a lot, actually."

"All right."

"Don't get smug."

"You're a little late."

She nudged him with her shoulder. He smiled and caught her hand. After a moment he released it again, not because he had to but because it was still hers to offer. She thought about that more than she expected. With most people, closeness seemed to come attached to a list of duties. She had to protect Annabeth, keep the younger campers in line, prove to Clarisse that she wasn't taking anybody's place. Rhys had wanted her there, and his wanting hadn't come with an assignment.

"What does this make us?" she asked.

He considered the question. "Two people who had a good night?"

"Calder."

"I know what you mean. I don't want to invent a grand answer just because we did something important to us."

"It was important?"

"To me. You can tell me if I'm misreading that."

"You aren't."

"Then let's start there."

She could work with that. It was better than having an entire relationship declared on her behalf because they had spent one night together. She had tried to be very certain about what she wanted when she arrived. She was less certain about what she wanted next month, and for once uncertainty did not feel like an order to act.

"Tomorrow," she said, "I'm seeing Chiron."

"I know."

"Annabeth knows. Which is unbearable."

"She'll be pleased."

"She'll pretend not to be, and it will somehow be worse."

He laughed again. She reached up and touched his cheek, stopping him before the laugh was finished. This time she kissed him slowly, because she could, because there was no audience to surprise and no reason to win.

When they drew apart, he said, "Are you staying until morning?"

"I usually wake at four."

"That wasn't the question."

"I know."

She thought about the walk down the hill, the empty loft, Annabeth's notebook by her pillow, the explanations she did not owe anybody and the ones she might eventually choose to give. Then she looked around the small cabin. The stove had settled to a red line beneath its grate. His bow lay in pieces by the workbench. A single sock had fallen behind the cot. It was not an enchanted palace or a promise that he would never leave. It was a room where she wanted to remain.

"I'm staying," she said.

He lay back against the pillow, satisfied with that, and she settled beside him. After a while she found his hand again. He didn't comment. They argued quietly about which of them had been worse at the north range that morning, then stopped talking altogether. The silence belonged to them, which felt stranger, and rather better, than she had imagined.

---

She woke at four the way she always did, and for once in her life she did not get up.

He was warm. The cabin was warm. Somewhere out past the walled window the tenth lantern was still burning on the Cabin Three porch for anybody who came around the cedar brake late. Annabeth would be asleep in the loft. Clarisse would be on her porch with her tea gone cold, count-of-five arithmetic done for the night. The whole world was warm tonight and the sky had more stars in it than anybody had names for, and she had until five to stay exactly where she was.

The heel still hurt. She would let somebody look at it. She would stop running downhill and start running flat and uphill, and she would let him correct her form on the field at eight in the morning with the whole camp watching and say yes to it in front of everybody.

But not until four fifty.

At half past four she slid out from under his arm with the care of a thief and dressed in the dark, buckles quiet, and stopped at the chalk slate by the door and wrote three words over the laundry instructions, and got out into the cold before he could wake up and be careful about anything.

Outside, the north wind had the whole hill, and the path home was gravel under her boots, nine amber lanterns and the tenth, and her breath going up white into the dark.

ETERNITY. THAT'S ALL, said the slate, over the laundry instructions.

She was grinning all the way up the hill, and nobody saw her do it, which made it worse.

On Tuesday morning, Chiron discovered that Thalia Grace could be persuaded into an examination but could not be persuaded into enjoying it.

He set a stool beside the infirmary window and told her to take off her boot. She did so with the theatrical care of someone submitting evidence under protest. The bruise behind the heel had settled into a yellow-green color, and the swelling was small enough to pretend it wasn't there until he pressed one finger against the tendon.

Thalia caught the edge of the stool.

"There," Chiron said.

"Yes, I know where my heel is."

"You know where it hurts. That isn't quite the same thing."

He checked her range of motion, had her put weight on the foot without the boot, and watched her walk six measured steps across the floor. He did not pronounce anything miraculous. He said the joint needed another period of protection, a change to her training, and a return visit if the pain failed to improve. Running downhill was out. Standing through a long archery session was unwise. A supportive wrap would help her remember the limit even if she was determined to ignore it.

"There's a hall tonight," she said.

"I know. You helped put it on the schedule."

"I promised I'd be there."

"You can be there sitting down. This is an event, Thalia, not a test of whether you can make yourself miserable."

"Everyone is enjoying that distinction lately."

"Perhaps because you've been refusing to make it."

He gave her the wrap and made her demonstrate putting it on correctly before he let her take it away. By the time she reached the archery field, the frost had lifted off the grass and several of the younger campers were waiting for their first lesson.

Rhys had the targets closer than usual.

"Did you move those because of me?" she asked.

"I moved them because three new kids can't hit anything at sixty yards."

"Convenient."

"Frequently."

He looked down at the wrap and back up at her face. She could have told him to stop checking. Instead, she held out the bow he had lent her, still in its canvas case.

"I can teach from the line," she said. "I can't stand here for an hour. You need somebody at the targets."

"I can put Milo at the targets. He needs the practice collecting things."

Milo, who was passing behind them, objected loudly. Rhys sent him to lay out the quivers anyway.

Thalia unwrapped the bow and examined the string. "You didn't ask how it went."

"You've been through enough people asking how it went. I can tell you want to shoot."

"I do."

"Two arrows, then. If Chiron said none, we do none."

"He said no long sessions."

"Two, then."

She shot one arrow left of center, adjusted without changing her footing, and landed the second exactly where she meant to. It was not a victory over the injury. Her heel still hurt when she walked back to the bench. But she had taken the shot she could take, and the whole field had continued without waiting for her to prove she was invincible.

Rhys picked up the target book. "Hall at seven?"

"I said I'd be there."

"I wasn't checking whether you'd keep a promise. I was asking whether you wanted me to save you a seat."

Thalia watched Milo knock over a quiver and start collecting arrows with a very solemn expression. Then she looked back at Rhys.

"Save me a seat," she said. "Near the exit. I reserve the right to leave if Percy starts explaining the rules."

"He wrote some of them."

"Exactly."

Rhys wrote her name on the volunteer line of the target book, not under shooter but under instructor. It was a small change, done in pencil, and she suspected she would resent it less after a week.

From farther up the hill, Annabeth came out of the construction shed carrying a rolled drawing taller than her shoulder. She saw the wrap, noticed the relocated targets, and kept walking without giving Thalia so much as a triumphant glance.

Thalia appreciated that. Later she would tell her so, badly and over supper, while Annabeth pretended she wasn't pleased. For now she took the book from Rhys, corrected Milo's score with a firm pencil stroke, and started instructing the next group.

The Tempest Hall was full of noise when Thalia arrived on Tuesday evening.

A row of lanterns lit the passage outside. From inside came the slap of water against tiled steps, the screech of a bench being dragged across the floor, and Percy's voice attempting to make itself heard over several campers who were determined to interpret the posted rules creatively. Thalia could tell he was enjoying himself from the volume of his complaints.

Rhys was waiting at the second entrance, with two folded towels under his arm.

"You're late," he said.

"I was considering skipping."

"Then I'd be standing here with two towels and no one to annoy."

"You'd manage. You have other talents."

"I do, but this is my favorite tonight."

He offered her a towel. She took it because the air down here had the damp cold of a cellar. There was a bench free at the near end, close to the dry route out. He hadn't put her in a corner where she couldn't see the pool, or somewhere conspicuous where everyone would know the seat had been reserved. The thoughtfulness annoyed her in the way kindness sometimes did when she'd spent too much of the day expecting to fight for something.

She sat, stretched the injured leg until it felt comfortable, and looked across the room. Annabeth was instructing Percy to move a marker back six inches. Percy was arguing that the marker was symbolic. Annabeth told him that water didn't understand symbolism and neither, apparently, did he.

Rhys followed her gaze. "They always like that?"

"They're worse when they agree."

"They seem happy."

"They are."

He glanced at her. She wasn't sure whether he had heard the change in her voice. She could have explained that Percy and Annabeth had an ease with each other that felt older than either of them was supposed to be. She could have said that sometimes watching her sister be wholly herself with somebody else made Thalia proud and uneasy in equal measure. But it was not her secret to explain, and even if it had been, she wasn't ready to explain the parts of her own childhood that made letting go so difficult.

Instead she nudged his boot with hers.

"You're staring."

"You're doing the same thing."

"I'm allowed. She's my sister."

"I was staring at you."

She went still, then looked away before her expression gave her up. Rhys was watching the water again when she glanced back. He did that sometimes: said the thing that left her without an easy answer, then allowed her the dignity of not having to find one immediately.

The session began. A daughter of Demeter lost her footing on the first submerged step and recovered before anyone could reach her. Percy called out a congratulation as though she'd completed a quest. Clarisse, supervising from the far rail, gave him a look that should have extinguished him. It didn't.

Thalia stayed for forty minutes. She helped a younger camper work out why the room seemed louder on the wet side of the divider, then caught herself enjoying the child's bright-eyed account of the echo. She hadn't expected much from sitting still. She certainly hadn't expected to be useful while doing it.

Rhys spent most of the time on the far side of the pool with the novices. Each time he looked toward her, she recognized that he was trying to ask whether she was comfortable without doing it in front of an audience. She answered once with a raised eyebrow and once with an obscene gesture too low for the children to see. The second answer made him laugh so hard that the camper beside him demanded to know what he'd missed.

At half past eight, the last of the beginners left and the room began to empty. Percy was winding up a long explanation about the proper way to dry a rope. Annabeth listened with the exhausted affection of someone who had already corrected the same mistake three times. Thalia rose carefully from the bench.

"Chiron told you not to stand too long," Rhys said when he returned.

"I just stood up."

"I meant earlier."

"I didn't."

"I know. You listened."

"If you compliment me for sitting down, I'm leaving."

"Noted."

He shouldered the towels. She could see that the right one had been folded into a remarkably precise square. The left looked as though he'd wrestled it into submission.

"Which did you fold?"

He inspected them. "I don't want to answer that."

"Excellent. You can still surprise me."

They left by the dry corridor, where the sound of the pool dropped away behind the door. It was colder outside than it had been at sunset. The ground had developed a crust at the edges of the path, and Thalia had to watch where she placed her boots. Rhys offered an arm at the steep section. She considered being irritated, then took it and let him steady her over the two uneven steps.

"Not going to argue?" he asked.

"I might save it for something worthwhile."

"I'll keep a list."

"Don't. Clarisse has enough lists for the entire state."

They walked a little farther. At the fork, one path led up toward Cabin One and the other ran past the north arena to the Apollo cabin. Thalia stopped where the gravel had been packed smooth by the carts.

"Come with me?" Rhys asked.

She heard the invitation for what it was. No invented errand, no need to demonstrate that she was the sort of person who could walk alone after dark.

"For how long?"

"As long as you want. You're tired. I'm not pretending otherwise."

"I'm tired of everybody measuring my heel."

"Then we won't discuss your heel."

"What will we discuss?"

He considered. "Your terrifying opinions about the novice targets."

"They're not terrifying. They're correct."

"I knew we'd find something."

She went with him.

---

The cabin was unoccupied when they reached it. Rhys checked the slate by the door, put the two towels away, and stirred the coals until a small flame caught. Thalia stood by the bench watching him move around the room. Yesterday she had crossed its threshold on purpose for the first time. Tonight the room still felt particular, still held the memory of what they'd chosen, but it no longer made her feel as though she were entering a contest that required a brave face.

Rhys put the poker down. "Tea?"

"You're turning into an old man."

"You're welcome to make it yourself."

"I'd rather watch you ruin it."

He filled the kettle. She moved toward the workbench and picked up a length of blue thread that had been left beside a small leather case. A half-mended glove lay under it.

"Is this yours?"

"My mother's."

"Your mother sent you a glove?"

"She sent me two. I managed to tear one during the trip. I promised to fix it."

"You're bad at sewing."

"She'll know that, too."

He looked over her shoulder while she examined the seam. The cut ran across the palm, shallow but awkward to mend. The thread would be visible even if he matched the stitches properly.

"Let me," she said.

"You don't have to."

"I know. If I were required to, I would refuse."

He handed her the needle. She sat on the edge of the cot, where the lamp was stronger, and repaired the glove with firm little stitches. It was not complicated. Her hands knew the work from years of making too little cloth serve too many purposes.

Rhys sat beside her, close enough to watch but careful not to jostle her arm. She liked his attention on her fingers, though she would rather have swallowed a coin than admit it.

"My mother used to tell me not to waste time on people who could fix something themselves," he said.

"Good advice."

"She wasn't always right."

Thalia glanced at him. "Because she thought people shouldn't help?"

"Because sometimes fixing a thing together is the point."

"That's dangerously sentimental, Calder."

"Could be. Don't tell anyone."

She finished the last stitch, bit off the thread, and held the glove out. He examined it, his thumb brushing the seam, and looked genuinely impressed.

"I did know you were good at that," he said.

"Clearly."

"No. I mean I knew you could do it. I didn't know you'd do it for me."

That was worse than a compliment. It was accurate.

She set the glove aside. "There's a difference between helping someone and being put in charge of them."

"Yes."

"Don't make me your glove person."

He laughed. "I solemnly swear I won't."

She turned to face him properly. The lamplight left a pale line along his cheek. She had been aware of wanting to kiss him through the entire conversation, and she was tired of pretending that the feeling had to be disguised as something else.

"Can I?" she asked.

He looked surprised only for a moment. "Please."

The kiss was easy at first, then less so, because neither of them had stopped wanting the other since yesterday. He moved his hand to her cheek. She caught his wrist and held it there, not to prevent him from touching her but to keep him close while she took her time. He smiled against her mouth when she paused.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. You asked."

"Should I not?"

"No. I liked it."

"Don't get used to good manners."

"I wouldn't dream of it."

She kissed him again. When the kettle began to rattle, neither of them reached for it immediately. Rhys was the first to laugh, which was unfair because she'd just decided she was going to be the one who broke the silence.

"That will boil dry," he said.

"Then go save your kettle."

"You're very demanding."

"Only when I'm here."

"That's not what I've heard."

She shoved his shoulder lightly. He caught her hand, kissed her knuckles, and went to pour the tea.

The rest of the evening was quieter. They drank from mismatched mugs and talked about nothing of consequence until the conversation found its way, as it inevitably would, to the question Thalia had been avoiding: whether she wanted this to happen again in a way that could be spoken aloud.

"I don't want an appointment," she said.

"Neither do I."

"I don't want you waiting for me every night, wondering if I'm coming."

"I wouldn't."

"You're going to say that now."

"I'm saying it because it's true."

She set down her mug. "And I don't want to be one more job you have to organize around the winter list."

Rhys leaned back against the bedframe and rubbed at a thumbprint of blue thread stuck to his sleeve. "You're not a job. But I do like knowing when I might see you. That isn't the same thing as putting you on a roster."

She knew he was right. That did not make the distinction comfortable.

"I could come by Thursday," she said. "If you're free."

"I should be. I have work until six."

"Then I won't interrupt it."

"Thalia, you can interrupt me sometimes."

"How generous."

"I'll try not to put that on a plaque."

She smiled despite herself. "Thursday, then."

"Thursday."

He came to sit beside her, not as a reward for reaching an agreement but because they were alone and both wanted him there. She rested her head briefly against his shoulder, an intimacy that seemed to surprise her more than any of the kissing had.

When she stood to leave, he didn't ask her to stay. She had said she was going home, and he took her at her word. He walked her as far as the porch. Outside, the cold made her eyes water. She drew the borrowed coat closer.

"You're still wearing Percy's coat," he said.

"He's not getting it back until spring."

"Does he know that?"

"He suspects."

Rhys kissed her once on the threshold. There was nothing dramatic about it, and that was its own kind of danger. She could imagine doing it a hundred times, each one different enough to matter.

"Thursday," he said.

"Don't be late."

"It's my cabin."

"Then you have no excuse."

She stepped down into the dark. Behind her he closed the door against the cold. She was halfway past the first lantern before she realized that for nearly an hour she hadn't thought about whether she was walking correctly. The heel began to ache again as soon as she noticed. She swore at it, then continued up the path with the kind of private smile she hoped nobody would see.
