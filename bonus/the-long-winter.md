## Bonus Interlude: The Long Winter

Monday, the twenty-third of October. The wind had come around to the north with the cold riding it, and the whole hill had the locked-down feel of a place that had decided to be winter and was not going to be argued with about it.

Thalia Grace walked the west path alone with her boots loud on the gravel. She passed the ninth amber lantern, and the tenth on the hook by the Cabin Three porch, with her hands in the pockets of a coat that was not hers and her breath going up white into the dark. The coat was Percy's. She had taken it off the hook by the door on her way out of Cabin Three without asking, and Clarisse had seen her do it and said nothing, because Clarisse understood that there are nights when a person needs to leave a warm room and go and stand in the cold outside a door that is not theirs.

Earlier that morning, she had made the mistake of believing that a problem became less real if she gave it a sufficiently crowded schedule.

At seven, she had helped carry the last crates of winter arrowheads out of the dry store. At half past seven she had argued with a son of Hermes about where the straw targets should go when the north field iced over. At eight, with the sun barely clear of the trees, she had taken her place on the shooting line and discovered that the heel was worse in the cold.

She put her weight on it anyway.

The first arrow struck two inches outside the circle. She pulled another from the quiver and tried to compensate by tightening the shoulder on her drawing side. The second arrow landed even farther left.

"You're moving before you release," Annabeth said.

"I can see where the arrows went."

"Then stop acting surprised by it."

Her sister was sitting on the fence with a field notebook balanced on one knee, supposedly checking the dimensions of the winter target shed. She had a pencil tucked into her braid and had drawn a very good plan of the shed. She had also watched every arrow Thalia had shot.

Thalia set a third arrow against the string. "Don't you have a building to annoy?"

"I do. Unfortunately, somebody put the targets in front of it."

"I can move."

"That's what I'm concerned about."

Thalia turned to give her the look that had stopped Annabeth talking when she was seven. It had not worked for two years, and there was no reason to expect it would work now. Annabeth kept her pencil on the page and met the look without blinking.

Across the field, Rhys had finished examining a younger camper's grip. He started toward them, saw Thalia's expression, and took the longer route around the equipment bench.

"Coward," Thalia called.

"Professional judgment," he called back.

He carried three practice bows across to the novices rather than come between the Grace sisters. That ought to have been irritating. Instead, Thalia found herself watching the way he gave each bow to its new owner: one instruction, one chance to try, no fuss when somebody got it wrong.

Annabeth followed her gaze. "He isn't going to argue you into having a working foot."

"Neither are you."

"No. Chiron might, though. He's fairly good at feet."

Thalia loosed the third arrow. She knew before it landed that she had twisted the heel again. Pain went up the back of her leg, sudden and hot enough to make her grip the bow too hard.

Annabeth was off the fence before the arrow hit the target.

"Don't," Thalia said.

"I haven't done anything."

"You're about to."

"I was going to pick up the arrows you dropped."

Thalia looked down. Two arrows had slipped from the loose side of her quiver. Annabeth collected them without touching her, then set them carefully on the bench instead of returning them to Thalia's hand.

"You used to tell me the difference between being brave and being stupid," Annabeth said. "It was usually right after I'd done something you didn't like."

"I remember."

"I remember too."

That last sentence struck differently from the others. Annabeth had memories of a sister who had raised her, a sisterhood written into this version of their childhood that the younger girl could recognize through stories and habits but not always through her own recollection. Thalia had spent years being the person who remembered for both of them. Sometimes Annabeth's quiet insistence sounded like a challenge to that job. Sometimes it sounded like a girl trying to do it herself.

Thalia lowered the bow.

"You're allowed to be annoyed with me," Annabeth said. "I just don't want to watch you make it permanent."

"I don't need an audience for every stupid decision."

"Then stop making them on the public range."

Annabeth picked up her notebook, checked the pencil was still in her braid, and headed down the hill toward the construction shed. She did not look back. Thalia wanted to call her back and also wanted fifteen minutes without being somebody's older sister. She was old enough to know that both could be true. It did not help her decide which one to act on.

She put the bow away after the fourth arrow. It was a small concession, and she hated how much effort it took.

---

By lunch the argument had become public without either sister saying another word about it. Thalia went into Cabin Three for a length of cord Clarisse had promised her and found the winter work table covered in measurements, cloth, and a formidable pile of things that had been washed but not mended.

Silena sat cross-legged on the bench, replacing buttons on a child's coat. Percy was holding the free end of a strip of canvas and looking personally wronged by its tendency to curl.

"You're late," Clarisse said, not looking up from the list.

"For what?"

"For being useful. Hold that end."

Thalia took the canvas from Percy. He flexed his fingers in relief.

"I've been holding that for eighteen minutes."

"You've been complaining about holding it for eighteen minutes," Silena corrected.

"That's how I measure time."

They were making a removable cover for the west-end awning, or trying to. The existing awning had been repaired twice and still sagged toward the corner every time it rained. Silena wanted the cover to come down before a hard snow, so nobody would have to climb a ladder on frozen boards to rescue the entire structure.

Thalia gave the fabric a closer look. "That seam won't take the strain."

"It will if the tension goes to the cord, not the stitching," Silena said. "Charlie checked it."

"Of course Charlie checked it."

Silena didn't blush. She threaded the needle with a sharp motion. "Yes. He knows how to fix things. It's a very attractive quality in somebody helping with the winter work."

Percy nearly let go of his corner laughing.

Clarisse rapped the table with a knuckle. "Less commentary. More canvas."

Thalia held the line steady while Silena pinned the edge. The work had a useful rhythm to it, even if the ache in Thalia's heel made standing still unpleasant. Percy told an elaborate story about a Hermes camper who had mistaken roofing tar for stove polish. Clarisse corrected the exaggerations only when they interfered with the important point, which was that the tar had to be scraped off a door. Nobody asked Thalia about her foot.

Then she stepped down to reach the end of the table, shifted her weight, and caught herself on its corner.

Clarisse's pencil stopped moving.

"Sit," she said.

"I'm fine."

"That wasn't a question."

"Are you going to start issuing medical orders now?"

"No. I'm going to stop you tearing my table in half because you won't sit on a bench."

Thalia sat. Silena passed the pinned canvas across the table so the work could reach her instead of making her reach it. The three of them kept arguing about the seams and whether the temporary covering would survive the first heavy wind. It was almost worse than a lecture. Nobody made her admit why they had rearranged the room.

When Percy went to put the canvas away, he noticed her looking at his coat on the hook.

"Take it if you need it," he said.

"I wasn't asking."

"That sounds like a yes."

"It's a better coat than mine."

"It's also three sizes too big."

"Then nobody will recognize me."

He grinned. "Try not to get accused of stealing from yourself. There's paperwork."

She stayed another hour and helped Silena finish two of the long seams while Clarisse dealt with the supply list. By the time they folded the cover and put it aside for the next dry day, Thalia had missed her own self-imposed appointment to inspect the fence by the north arena. It hadn't fallen down without her. This seemed an unreasonable development.

At the door, Silena looked up from her sewing. "Annabeth went to the library."

"I didn't ask."

"I know. She took the shed drawings."

"Good."

"She was angry, by the way."

"I gathered that."

Silena pulled the thread through the last button and bit it off. "She's allowed."

"So am I."

"Also true."

Nobody softened the answer for her. That was one of the things Thalia liked about Cabin Three, and on this particular afternoon it was one of the things she resented.

---

The argument with Annabeth found her again before supper.

Thalia was returning the borrowed cord to the shed when she saw her sister kneeling beside the short retaining wall, holding a ruler against a loose stone. The notebook was open, one corner weighted with a pebble. She was testing where snowmelt would go once the ground froze and thawed again. Her glove was damp clear through.

"You're going to lose a finger," Thalia said.

Annabeth didn't look up. "Not from a wet glove."

"It will freeze."

"In the next ten minutes? Impressive weather forecasting."

Thalia set the cord beside the wall. She wanted to say she hadn't meant to sound like that. Instead, she crouched to inspect the stone, promptly discovered that crouching pulled on her heel, and swore under her breath.

Annabeth put down the ruler.

"No," Thalia said. "Don't give me the face."

"I haven't moved my face."

"It's your whole face."

Annabeth got up and brushed the dirt off her knees. "You don't have to tell me what happened to your foot. I can see it. I also don't have to spend every day pretending I can't."

"I know you can see it."

"Then why does it make you so angry when I say so?"

Thalia had a dozen answers, most of them unhelpful. Because she could remember Annabeth small enough to carry. Because a skinned knee used to be something Thalia could fix with water and an old shirt. Because there were years when refusing to fall down had seemed like the only useful thing she could do. Because now her sister noticed everything and did not wait for permission to notice.

"Because you sound like me," Thalia said at last.

Annabeth paused. "Is that supposed to be insulting?"

"Sometimes."

That got the smallest possible smile.

Thalia sat on the low wall. Annabeth stayed standing, but she didn't leave.

"I hate not being able to trust my own foot," Thalia said. "And I hate somebody watching to see if I trust it wrong."

"I can't promise not to notice."

"I wasn't asking you to."

"Good. Because that would be a stupid promise."

Thalia looked down at the cord, at the dirt ground into the woven strands, and made herself say the thing she had come down the hill avoiding. "I'll go to Chiron tomorrow."

Annabeth took a moment before answering. She was learning, slowly and with great difficulty, that not every victory required a closing argument.

"All right," she said. "I'll give him the shed measurements then. Two reasons to be in one place."

"You're not coming to supervise."

"I have a drawing to show him."

"You've always got a drawing."

"I've always got a building that someone insisted didn't need fixing."

Thalia let her have that one. Annabeth took back the ruler and started down the path with the notebook under her arm. They were still angry at each other, just not in quite the same way.

By six, when Thalia borrowed Percy's coat and stepped out of Cabin Three, the injury had become something she was actually going to do something about. It remained a poor explanation for why she was walking toward Rhys Calder's light. She had no better explanation ready.

Someone had known about the heel since Sunday. Percy had said it aloud at the fire ring, with no ceremony or accusation, and Thalia had spent the next day discovering how much she disliked being observed when she couldn't do anything about the observation. Now the north path ended at Rhys's cabin, with lamplight caught in the quilted window. She could still turn around. She had promised Annabeth an examination tomorrow; she had promised Rhys nothing. That, she was beginning to suspect, was why coming here mattered.

There was a particular humiliation in realizing she had been flirting with him for almost six weeks and had somehow managed to convince herself she was conducting a professional rivalry.

She'd first noticed the problem when he corrected her elbow.

It had been a Thursday with rain threatening, one of the afternoons that made the archery field smell of turned earth. She was halfway through a round, shooting well enough to be angry that she wasn't shooting better, when Rhys walked behind the line with a clipboard tucked beneath his arm and paused at her station.

"You're compensating," he'd said.

"I know."

"Then why are you doing it?"

"Because if I stop, the arrow lands in the wrong county."

He hadn't laughed. That was the first thing that caught her attention. Most of the boys who tried to advise Thalia laughed before she was finished talking, whether she was funny or not, as if anything other than agreement might result in a spear through the chest.

Rhys had stepped back behind the safety line. "Do you want me to show you?"

Thalia had looked him over, from his muddy boots to the ridiculous clipboard, and decided he was probably an irritating person.

"Go ahead."

He'd picked up a spare bow, set his feet, and deliberately reproduced her mistake. The arrow had landed left. Then he'd adjusted his stance and shot again, correcting without a speech. He passed the bow back to her.

"That's the difference. Your shoulder's doing the work your feet should be doing."

"My feet are doing plenty."

"That's what worries me."

She had expected him to stay and watch. Instead he went to help a camper whose string had slipped. The next time Thalia shot, her arrow struck where she'd intended. She hated that. More precisely, she hated how much she wanted to call him back and show him.

By the end of the afternoon she had invented a reason to walk past his bench.

"Your feet are too narrow," she said.

Rhys looked down at his boots. "Excuse me?"

"When you shoot. You're making yourself less stable than you need to be."

He glanced at the empty practice line. "I didn't shoot while you were watching."

"Yes, you did."

"When?"

"Before lunch. Near the second target."

He paused, and there was something briefly, annoyingly delighted in his face. "You've been keeping track of me."

"I have eyes."

"Apparently very thorough ones."

"Are you going to fix your stance?"

"Probably. Show me what you mean."

She'd demonstrated, expecting an argument. He'd stepped into the corrected position, considered the change, and nodded. There had been none of the wounded pride she might have enjoyed sparring with. He had asked a second question, an intelligent one about the angle of his back foot, and for the next ten minutes they had talked about shooting without either trying to prove the other incompetent.

It was the kind of conversation Thalia had been missing without realizing it. Competence didn't have to be a struggle for rank. A person could take her seriously without making her responsible for their feelings about it.

That should have been enough to make him interesting.

It wasn't the whole problem.

The next week he caught a falling target frame with one hand while she braced the other end. Someone had installed the hinge backward, and the frame had started folding down toward the youngest campers. Thalia had been first to see it. Rhys was first to move. They managed to stop it between them, then spent an hour taking the whole thing apart while the instructor explained to Chiron that the hinge had been perfectly sound that morning.

Rhys worked close beside her without hovering. His shoulders strained beneath an old grey shirt when they lifted the heavy crosspiece back into position. She noticed that, and then noticed herself noticing it, which was irritating.

"Hold this," she'd said, passing him a bolt.

"Yes, captain."

"Don't call me captain."

"Would you prefer 'your majesty'?"

"I would prefer you stop talking."

He'd grinned. "I'll put that on the list."

She had spent the following hour being considerably sharper with him than the crooked hinge deserved. At the end he wiped the grease from his hands and offered her his clean rag, which was a small courtesy she disliked for the unreasonable reason that she wanted him to do it again.

The first time they'd been alone without an excuse had happened by accident. The field lights had gone out while they were sorting equipment, and a late shower had trapped them beneath the narrow shelter by the tool shed. The rain made a sheet between them and the path.

"Well," he said. "We're stuck."

"It's rain. Not a siege."

"You're welcome to test it."

She stayed where she was.

He leaned against the post with his arms folded. Outside the shelter, drops bounced off the top of the water barrel. The rain made it possible to be silent without inviting interrogation.

"How old were you when you started?" he asked.

"Started what?"

"Archery."

"Young."

"Useful level of detail."

"I was a child. There was a bow. Eventually somebody decided I shouldn't be allowed to shoot toward the cabins."

"Seems reasonable."

"I disagreed at the time."

He laughed. It was different from the laugh he used on the field, softer and a little surprised. Thalia turned toward him before she could help herself.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You laughed."

"You were describing yourself at six. I was imagining the camp safety report."

"Seven," she said.

"Even worse."

"And it was one incident."

"An important distinction."

She could have told him about the night she had taught Annabeth to brace a toy bow, about how the smaller girl had insisted on holding the string all by herself and nearly fallen backward. She could have told him about the things she had learned too young, before anyone had made them into organized camp lessons. Instead she stood beside him and watched the rain.

He didn't pry.

Another minute passed.

"You left for a while," she said.

Rhys nodded. "I did."

"Why come back?"

"Because there are people here I like."

"Very diplomatic."

"And because the winters are short on competent instructors."

"Also diplomatic."

"And because I missed it."

"What part?"

He glanced toward the dark range. "Getting to be good at something without pretending it solves everything else."

She had looked at him then, really looked, and found no performance to argue with. He was nineteen. He had a life outside the valley, a mother he wrote to, responsibilities he had gone home to shoulder and would shoulder again. He had chosen to come back and wasn't pretending the choice had made his life simple.

There were things she might have wanted to ask. None of them felt suitable for the shelter or the rain.

When the shower eased he pushed away from the post.

"I'd better get the targets covered."

"Before the next siege?"

"Exactly."

She followed him out and helped, and for several days afterward was profoundly irritating to everyone who asked why she had stayed late at the range.

The humiliating part was that nobody had asked.

After that came the little things. A cup of cider left on the bench when the evening sessions ran long. A joke about her insistence on counting every arrow in a quiver even when the number was written on the case. A walk back from the field in which they argued for twenty minutes about which of them had made a worse first impression.

"You told me my feet were wrong," he said.

"They were wrong."

"Your entire opening statement."

"I like to be efficient."

"And here I thought you were desperate to talk to me."

She had stopped in the middle of the path.

Rhys had stopped too. The smile disappeared when he saw her face.

"That was a joke," he said. "Too much?"

She might have punished him for asking. She might have made the rest of the walk unpleasant because she was embarrassed, which was sometimes her preferred method of managing embarrassment.

Instead she said, "Not entirely."

"Not entirely what?"

"Wrong."

He looked at her for a second. She could hear the loose lantern hook tapping against its bracket above them. Then he smiled, slowly enough that she had time to wish she hadn't spoken and to be glad she had.

"Good," he said.

That was all. He didn't make a move, didn't claim an invitation, and didn't turn her confession into a victory. He walked the rest of the way with her, close enough that their coats sometimes brushed, and said good night at the fork.

The night after that, she dreamed of kissing him.

She woke furious with herself, which did not improve the situation. Neither did seeing him that morning at the range, lifting a hand in greeting as if the world hadn't rearranged itself overnight.

She began taking the longer path to breakfast. It wasn't remotely convenient. Twice she encountered him on it anyway.

One afternoon he'd said, "You know you can just come over if you want something."

"What makes you think I want something?"

"Because you've been standing at the far end of the range glaring at the back of my head for ten minutes."

"I've been checking your form."

"With your bow unstrung?"

She'd looked at the bow in her hand, discovered he was right, and turned to leave.

He hadn't called after her.

It was the restraint she couldn't stop thinking about. When she wanted to be difficult, he was perfectly willing to let her be difficult. But he wasn't interested in pulling the answer out of her. He would take what she chose to say and leave the rest alone.

And tonight, finally, she'd made a choice.


Two Saturdays before she finally knocked on Rhys's door, Thalia had nearly kissed him and then spent the following week trying to forget it.

The problem began with a window.

It was the one at the north end of the practice shed, an old narrow pane set into a frame that swelled in damp weather and refused to close completely. The first frost had left a white line along its lower edge. Rhys was working on it with a chisel and a small bag of replacement screws, and Thalia happened to arrive in the middle of his second attempt.

She had not been looking for him, she told herself.

She had wanted a length of cord. There was no reason to distinguish that errand from the fact that she had taken the long path to the shed instead of the short one, or that she had slowed when she saw his coat hanging outside the door. Coincidence was a useful explanation for a surprising number of choices.

"You're doing that wrong," she said.

Rhys didn't look up. "Good afternoon, Grace."

"The frame's warped."

"That's what I'm trying to fix."

"You're shaving the wrong edge."

He set down the chisel and turned toward her. He wore a grey work shirt with the sleeves pushed above his elbows, and there was a streak of dust across one cheek. Thalia noticed it. She then became annoyed with herself for noticing something that had absolutely no bearing on the structure of a window.

"By all means," he said. "Show me."

She took the chisel. He stepped aside without complaint, which removed the pleasure she'd expected from proving him wrong. She examined the frame and found that he had indeed been working the wrong edge, though only by a little. She scraped away the narrow ridge and tested the window. It moved halfway down, then stuck again.

Rhys held out his hand.

"You were saying?"

"That it's worse than I thought."

"Convenient."

She gave the tool back.

They worked on the frame together, testing the sash after every small adjustment. It was slow, irritating work. At one point Rhys took the entire thing out, and Thalia held the glass steady while he repaired the guide. She was close enough to see the fine pale marks on his fingers from old bowstrings and the crease beside his mouth when he was concentrating.

He had good hands. That was an unhelpful observation.

"You're staring," he said.

"I'm checking whether you know what you're doing."

"How's the verdict?"

"Uncertain."

He looked up. Their faces were unexpectedly close.

For a moment neither moved.

Thalia could hear rain beginning on the roof, soft at first, then louder when the wind shifted. She was aware of his breath, his hand resting against the lower edge of the frame and the absurd possibility that she might lean another inch toward him simply because she wanted to know what it would feel like.

She didn't.

Rhys looked at her for one heartbeat longer, then returned his attention to the window.

"Hold that corner," he said.

She did, annoyed by the relief and disappointed by it at the same time.

When the repair was finished, the window slid down without resistance and closed against the sill with a satisfying click. Rhys tested it twice.

"There," he said.

"Excellent. You have achieved the basic purpose of a window."

"Only with expert supervision."

"Naturally."

He put away the tools while she gathered the loose screws. The rain was heavier now. They would have to wait before leaving, unless one of them wanted to walk through it and pretend not to notice.

Rhys sat on the workbench. Thalia leaned against the opposite wall, close enough to see him clearly but far enough that the space between them had become a subject of its own.

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"You've been asking questions for weeks."

"About Annabeth."

Thalia's expression changed.

"What about her?"

"She asked me whether I'd let her take one of the beginner groups after the winter rotation. I told her she should talk to you."

"Good."

"She didn't look pleased."

"That's because she doesn't like asking permission."

"She's fourteen."

"Yes. I've noticed."

Rhys rested his elbows on his knees. "She said you'd decide she'd overcommitted before she even finished explaining the plan."

Thalia folded her arms.

"She probably has."

"Maybe."

"You don't know her the way I do."

"No," he said. "I don't."

She looked at him, irritated by how little he had tried to defend himself.

"Then why bring it up?"

"Because she was asking me about teaching. I think she'd be good at it. But she's worried you'll hear her request as an attempt to get away from you."

Thalia considered the remark. It was too direct, and it struck too close to something she didn't like acknowledging: that she had spent years being Annabeth's protection, and that she had begun to treat any movement away from that protection as a judgment on the job she'd done.

"She's a child," she said.

"She is."

"She can make foolish decisions."

"Definitely."

"And if something happens?"

"Then you help her deal with it. You don't have to prevent every mistake she might make."

Thalia looked toward the repaired window. The rain ran down the pane in small crooked lines. She wanted to object, and she knew most of her objections would be correct in a narrow practical sense. Annabeth could make mistakes. The world was full of risks. But the larger point remained: her sister was not a fragile object Thalia could keep safe by holding still.

"You sound like Chiron," she said.

Rhys smiled faintly. "Terrible compliment."

"Don't be pleased with yourself."

"I'm not."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she asked, "Would you give her a group?"

"With supervision. One afternoon a week. I'd start with the children she already knows."

That was a reasonable plan, specific enough that she couldn't dismiss it as optimism. She considered the younger campers, Annabeth's careful instruction and her habit of noticing mistakes before anyone else had identified a problem. Her sister might be good at teaching.

"I'll talk to her," Thalia said.

"Good."

She turned toward him sharply. "That doesn't mean you've won."

"I hadn't realized we were competing."

"You always know."

He smiled. She tried not to smile back.

They remained in the shed while the rain passed. Rhys told her about the first time he'd tried to teach a younger camper and been so impatient that the child abandoned the bow entirely. Thalia countered with a story about trying to explain knots to Annabeth when she was little, only for the younger girl to point out that Thalia herself had tied the wrong one. They were both laughing when the rain eased enough to reveal the path again.

At the door Rhys stopped.

"You've got dust on your face," he said.

"From the window."

"Of course."

She reached up to brush it away. He caught her wrist gently, not to stop her but to show her where the dust was. His thumb touched the side of her cheek.

The moment was ordinary. It was also not ordinary at all.

Thalia looked up.

He was watching her with a seriousness that made the little shed seem suddenly very quiet. She could see the choice he was making, the way he held still instead of turning the touch into an invitation she hadn't given. She could have moved closer. She knew he would not have been indifferent.

She was not ready.

She turned her face toward the repaired window, and he released her hand.

"Missed a spot," she said.

"Probably."

Neither sounded convinced.

They left the shed together. The ground was wet beneath their boots, and the clouds were beginning to break above the lake. At the fork he paused.

"Annabeth," he said. "I meant what I said."

"So did I."

"Good."

She looked at him. There was something she wanted to ask, something entirely unrelated to her sister or the range. She wanted to know whether he had nearly kissed her. She wanted to know whether he had wanted her to lean closer, and how long he might be willing to wait before deciding she never would.

Instead she said, "Your window's going to stick again after the next heavy rain."

He laughed.

"Then you'll have to come back."

She met his eyes.

"Maybe," she said.

She walked uphill with the rain easing around her, convinced she'd handled the situation sensibly. She lasted until the next morning before she began finding reasons to pass the practice shed again.


Five days before the first Monday night, Thalia learned that Rhys had been keeping one of her arrows.

He showed it to her when she came to the range early, before the beginners had arrived. It was a practice shaft with a small nick near the fletching, the kind of damage most instructors would have ignored until the feather came loose entirely. Someone had sanded the nick smooth and tied the feather back with a thread so fine she had to hold it up to the light to see it.

"That's mine," she said.

"Yes."

"Why do you have it?"

"You left it after the rain last week."

"I left half a dozen."

"This was the one you kept trying to fix."

She looked at him. He was standing behind the equipment bench in an old green shirt, perfectly calm about the fact that he had noticed something nobody else would have remembered. She had worked on the arrow during a lesson, then put it aside when a younger camper needed help. She hadn't thought about it since.

"You could have thrown it out," she said.

"I could have."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because it was still good."

That was a terrible answer. It wasn't the answer she wanted, which was to know why he had noticed what she cared about and taken time to repair it when she wasn't there.

He handed the shaft over. She turned it between her fingers. The fletching sat straight, the binding neat enough that the arrow would fly reliably. He had done good work.

"Thank you," she said.

He looked startled. Then he smiled, slowly, as though the words were worth keeping.

"You're welcome."

She put the arrow in her quiver and began checking the others, grateful for something ordinary to occupy her hands. The lesson began a few minutes later. There were students with poor grips, an Apollo boy who kept shooting before the signal and a daughter of Demeter who insisted that wind was responsible for every miss. By the end of the session Thalia had nearly forgotten the arrow, until she reached for it by accident and discovered that the repaired feather lay perfectly against the shaft.

She used it for the final demonstration.

The arrow struck the center of the target.

She looked toward Rhys. He had seen. He raised one eyebrow, the familiar expression of a man resisting the temptation to say something insufferable. She returned the look without smiling.

After the lesson he came over.

"Good arrow?"

"Acceptable."

"High praise."

"The archer did most of the work."

"Naturally."

She slipped the arrow back into its place. She could have made another joke. Instead she asked, "Do you do that for everyone?"

"Repair arrows?"

"Notice the ones people want to keep."

He thought a moment. "I try."

"I've never met a committee with such a hostile chair."

"Give me time."

He moved the mug she'd nearly knocked over and settled beside her. The moment might have been spoiled if either of them had insisted on pretending she wasn't in pain. Instead the interruption changed their pace. He traced the back of her hand with his thumb. She slid her fingers between his.

"There's something else," she said.

He waited.

"If I come back here, I don't want it to be because I've been assigned to make you feel wanted. And I don't want you turning every evening into a test of whether I can admit I need you."

"I don't think you need me."

"Oh, thank you."

"I think you like me."

"At the moment."

"I like you too. Quite inconveniently."

She smiled despite herself. "You're making a mistake, Calder."

"Several."

"What's the biggest?"

He touched her cheek. "Thinking about tomorrow when you're still here."

She didn't answer. It was one of the few things anyone had said to her lately that required no rebuttal.

He asked whether she wanted tea. She said no. They shared a little water instead and sat with the blanket over their knees while the stove burned lower. When she began to get cold, she told him. When he shifted to make room, she pulled him back and told him not so far. The requests were clumsy, almost comically ordinary, and they pleased her more than any performance of perfect understanding could have done.

They kissed again, slower this time. She liked the difference. She liked being able to change the pace without explaining it, and she liked that he checked her face afterward rather than assuming one good moment meant permission for everything that followed. The night remained theirs, full of things they could ask for and things they might still decline. When she finally settled against him, the whole room smelled of warm wood and extinguished lamplight.

"Another secret," she murmured.

"I thought we'd exhausted my supply."

"One more."

He considered. "I kept looking at the north path tonight."

She raised her head. "You knew I'd come?"

"No. That's why I kept looking."

Something in her chest tightened, but not unpleasantly. She couldn't remember the last time somebody had admitted to hoping she'd arrive without making her feel guilty for the hours she hadn't been there.

"I nearly went home," she said.

"I know."

"No, you don't."

"I know you could have. I'm glad you didn't."

She looked at the darkened window. The decision was still hers, made and remade in every small thing she chose to say. She wanted to keep the strange freedom of that. She wanted him, too, even after the first fierce excitement of the evening had changed into something quieter.

"So am I," she said.

Rhys drew the blanket over her shoulder. There was a long pause, filled only by the occasional click of the stove and the wind coming through the pines. Then he said, "For the record, you were the first person I looked for at the range today."

"You were the first person I criticized."

"Romantic."

"I'm giving you everything I've got."

He caught her smiling before she could hide it. She kissed him to stop the commentary, and this time the kiss felt less like crossing a threshold than finding out how comfortable she might become on the other side of it.

She woke at four the way she always did, and for once in her life she did not get up.

He was warm. The cabin was warm. Somewhere out past the walled window the tenth lantern was still burning on the Cabin Three porch for anybody who came around the cedar brake late. Annabeth would be asleep in the loft. Clarisse would be on her porch with her tea gone cold, count-of-five arithmetic done for the night. The whole world was warm tonight and the sky had more stars in it than anybody had names for, and she had until five to stay exactly where she was.

The heel still hurt. She would let somebody look at it. She would stop running downhill and start running flat and uphill, and she would let him correct her form on the field at eight in the morning with the whole camp watching and say yes to it in front of everybody.

But not until four fifty.

She had always been suspicious of mornings.

A person who woke at four could get quite a lot done before everybody else noticed they were awake. At home, when Annabeth was younger, the hour had belonged to small tasks: checking a latch, making sure there was water, finding the socks that had disappeared overnight. She had liked having something to do while the rest of the house slept. It gave her the comforting illusion that she could be ready for anything.

Here, for once, there was nothing she needed to fix.

Rhys's arm was across her waist. She lay still, listening to him breathe, and considered the tremendous absurdity of having spent so much of her life arguing against people treating her as fragile while finding herself reluctant to move because she liked somebody holding her. His hand was open against the blanket, loose in sleep. She could have slipped out from beneath it. She had done more difficult things blindfolded. Instead she covered it with her own.

He stirred but didn't wake.

Thalia smiled into the dark. He would be insufferable if he knew how long she'd spent thinking about a hand.

She was nearly asleep again when he murmured, "You're thinking."

Her eyes opened. "No."

"That was remarkably quick."

"Go back to sleep."

He shifted against the pillow. The cabin was dark enough that she could see only the pale line of his cheek. "Is it morning?"

"Four."

"That's not morning."

"It is for some of us."

"Terrible people."

She leaned up on her elbow. "You're one to talk. You get to the range before eight."

"Eight is a humane hour."

"Eight is practically noon."

He laughed softly. Then, a little more awake, he looked at her.

"How's the heel?"

She groaned. "I've been awake for three minutes and you've already ruined everything."

"I thought I should get it out of the way."

"It's fine."

"All right."

She waited for the rest. It didn't come. He simply moved the pillow a little closer to her foot without demanding that she use it, then settled back down.

"You could be more irritating," she said.

"I'll try later."

"Good."

She lay down beside him again. The darkness made it easy to forget the size of the room, the eight empty cots and the workbench littered with his unfinished projects. She knew where his hand was and how to find his face if she wanted to kiss him. That was enough geography for the hour.

After a moment she did kiss him. The first touch was brief, intended as a farewell of sorts. It became something else when he kissed her back.

"Careful," he murmured.

"What now?"

"You're going to make me forget how tired I am."

She smiled. "Sounds like a personal problem."

He drew her nearer. She liked the warmth of him, the soft rumble of his laugh when she caught his shirt and refused to let the kiss end as quickly as he'd intended. The privacy of the hour made every small touch feel deliberate. They could be affectionate without worrying what anyone would say, and they could laugh when their noses knocked together without ruining anything.

She had expected to feel different after the previous night, but she'd been wrong about the sort of difference. She was still herself. Still impatient, still stubborn, still capable of making a terrible decision simply because somebody had told her not to. What had changed was how easy it suddenly seemed to stay.

She rested her forehead against his. "I have to go."

"I know."

"Annabeth will wake up."

"Then you should go."

She didn't move.

Rhys waited. Then, with an amusement he made no effort to hide, he said, "You're very bad at leaving."

"I'm excellent at leaving."

"Demonstrate."

She kissed him again.

"That was the opposite," he said when she drew back.

"Your standards are arbitrary."

"My standards are clear."

He touched her cheek, and she turned into his hand. The familiar teasing fell quiet. She could feel how much she'd miss his company even when she was back in her own bed, surrounded by the ordinary sounds of Cabin One. She didn't want to make a speech about it. She wanted to stay another minute.

"Can I come back?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Don't answer so quickly."

"Would you prefer hesitation?"

"Possibly."

He pretended to think. "Well, the winter schedule is very demanding. I'd need to consult my records."

She shoved him gently. He caught her hand and pressed it against his chest.

"Come back when you want to," he said. "Tell me when you can't. That's all I need."

Thalia looked at him. It was such a manageable request that she had no objection ready. She wanted, suddenly, to promise more than that. She didn't. She had learned enough about promises to know that their usefulness came from keeping them.

"All right," she said.

He smiled sleepily, pleased by the unadorned answer. She kissed him one last time, then lay beside him until the clock made the small click that marked the half hour.

The room had not transformed overnight. The window still leaked cold at the corner. The stove still made noises at unpredictable intervals. Rhys's shirt still lay crumpled beside the cot, and her borrowed coat still hung on the same hook. She was beginning to suspect that she'd like coming back to a room that remained stubbornly ordinary.

She looked at him. He was falling asleep again, one hand still resting against hers.

For once she was not leaving because somebody needed her. She was leaving because she had chosen a night, enjoyed it, and wanted to be home when the day began. The distinction made her feel lighter than any promise could have.


At half past four she slid out from under his arm with the care of a thief and dressed in the dark, buckles quiet, and stopped at the chalk slate by the door and wrote three words over the laundry instructions, and got out into the cold before he could wake up and be careful about anything.

Outside, the north wind had the whole hill, and the path home was gravel under her boots, nine amber lanterns and the tenth, and her breath going up white into the dark.

ETERNITY. THAT'S ALL, said the slate, over the laundry instructions.

She was grinning all the way up the hill, and nobody saw her do it, which made it worse.

On Tuesday morning, Chiron discovered that Thalia Grace could be persuaded into an examination but could not be persuaded into enjoying it.

He set a stool beside the infirmary window and told her to take off her boot. She did so with the theatrical care of someone submitting evidence under protest. The bruise behind the heel had settled into a yellow-green color, and the swelling was small enough to pretend it wasn't there until he pressed one finger against the tendon.

Thalia caught the edge of the stool.

"There," Chiron said.

"Yes, I know where my heel is."

"You know where it hurts. That isn't quite the same thing."

He checked her range of motion, had her put weight on the foot without the boot, and watched her walk six measured steps across the floor. He did not pronounce anything miraculous. He said the joint needed another period of protection, a change to her training, and a return visit if the pain failed to improve. Running downhill was out. Standing through a long archery session was unwise. A supportive wrap would help her remember the limit even if she was determined to ignore it.

"There's a hall tonight," she said.

"I know. You helped put it on the schedule."

"I promised I'd be there."

"You can be there sitting down. This is an event, Thalia, not a test of whether you can make yourself miserable."

"Everyone is enjoying that distinction lately."

"Perhaps because you've been refusing to make it."

He gave her the wrap and made her demonstrate putting it on correctly before he let her take it away. By the time she reached the archery field, the frost had lifted off the grass and several of the younger campers were waiting for their first lesson.

Rhys had the targets closer than usual.

"Did you move those because of me?" she asked.

"I moved them because three new kids can't hit anything at sixty yards."

"Convenient."

"Frequently."

He looked down at the wrap and back up at her face. She could have told him to stop checking. Instead, she held out the bow he had lent her, still in its canvas case.

"I can teach from the line," she said. "I can't stand here for an hour. You need somebody at the targets."

"I can put Milo at the targets. He needs the practice collecting things."

Milo, who was passing behind them, objected loudly. Rhys sent him to lay out the quivers anyway.

Thalia unwrapped the bow and examined the string. "You didn't ask how it went."

"You've been through enough people asking how it went. I can tell you want to shoot."

"I do."

"Two arrows, then. If Chiron said none, we do none."

"He said no long sessions."

"Two, then."

She shot one arrow left of center, adjusted without changing her footing, and landed the second exactly where she meant to. It was not a victory over the injury. Her heel still hurt when she walked back to the bench. But she had taken the shot she could take, and the whole field had continued without waiting for her to prove she was invincible.

Rhys picked up the target book. "Hall at seven?"

"I said I'd be there."

"I wasn't checking whether you'd keep a promise. I was asking whether you wanted me to save you a seat."

Thalia watched Milo knock over a quiver and start collecting arrows with a very solemn expression. Then she looked back at Rhys.

"Save me a seat," she said. "Near the exit. I reserve the right to leave if Percy starts explaining the rules."

"He wrote some of them."

"Exactly."

Rhys wrote her name on the volunteer line of the target book, not under shooter but under instructor. It was a small change, done in pencil, and she suspected she would resent it less after a week.

From farther up the hill, Annabeth came out of the construction shed carrying a rolled drawing taller than her shoulder. She saw the wrap, noticed the relocated targets, and kept walking without giving Thalia so much as a triumphant glance.

Thalia appreciated that. Later she would tell her so, badly and over supper, while Annabeth pretended she wasn't pleased. For now she took the book from Rhys, corrected Milo's score with a firm pencil stroke, and started instructing the next group.

The Tempest Hall was full of noise when Thalia arrived on Tuesday evening.

A row of lanterns lit the passage outside. From inside came the slap of water against tiled steps, the screech of a bench being dragged across the floor, and Percy's voice attempting to make itself heard over several campers who were determined to interpret the posted rules creatively. Thalia could tell he was enjoying himself from the volume of his complaints.

Rhys was waiting at the second entrance, with two folded towels under his arm.

"You're late," he said.

"I was considering skipping."

"Then I'd be standing here with two towels and no one to annoy."

"You'd manage. You have other talents."

"I do, but this is my favorite tonight."

He offered her a towel. She took it because the air down here had the damp cold of a cellar. There was a bench free at the near end, close to the dry route out. He hadn't put her in a corner where she couldn't see the pool, or somewhere conspicuous where everyone would know the seat had been reserved. The thoughtfulness annoyed her in the way kindness sometimes did when she'd spent too much of the day expecting to fight for something.

She sat, stretched the injured leg until it felt comfortable, and looked across the room. Annabeth was instructing Percy to move a marker back six inches. Percy was arguing that the marker was symbolic. Annabeth told him that water didn't understand symbolism and neither, apparently, did he.

Rhys followed her gaze. "They always like that?"

"They're worse when they agree."

"They seem happy."

"They are."

He glanced at her. She wasn't sure whether he had heard the change in her voice. She could have explained that Percy and Annabeth had an ease with each other that felt older than either of them was supposed to be. She could have said that sometimes watching her sister be wholly herself with somebody else made Thalia proud and uneasy in equal measure. But it was not her secret to explain, and even if it had been, she wasn't ready to explain the parts of her own childhood that made letting go so difficult.

Instead she nudged his boot with hers.

"You're staring."

"You're doing the same thing."

"I'm allowed. She's my sister."

"I was staring at you."

She went still, then looked away before her expression gave her up. Rhys was watching the water again when she glanced back. He did that sometimes: said the thing that left her without an easy answer, then allowed her the dignity of not having to find one immediately.

The session began. A daughter of Demeter lost her footing on the first submerged step and recovered before anyone could reach her. Percy called out a congratulation as though she'd completed a quest. Clarisse, supervising from the far rail, gave him a look that should have extinguished him. It didn't.

Thalia stayed for forty minutes. She helped a younger camper work out why the room seemed louder on the wet side of the divider, then caught herself enjoying the child's bright-eyed account of the echo. She hadn't expected much from sitting still. She certainly hadn't expected to be useful while doing it.

Rhys spent most of the time on the far side of the pool with the novices. Each time he looked toward her, she recognized that he was trying to ask whether she was comfortable without doing it in front of an audience. She answered once with a raised eyebrow and once with an obscene gesture too low for the children to see. The second answer made him laugh so hard that the camper beside him demanded to know what he'd missed.

At half past eight, the last of the beginners left and the room began to empty. Percy was winding up a long explanation about the proper way to dry a rope. Annabeth listened with the exhausted affection of someone who had already corrected the same mistake three times. Thalia rose carefully from the bench.

"Chiron told you not to stand too long," Rhys said when he returned.

"I just stood up."

"I meant earlier."

"I didn't."

"I know. You listened."

"If you compliment me for sitting down, I'm leaving."

"Noted."

He shouldered the towels. She could see that the right one had been folded into a remarkably precise square. The left looked as though he'd wrestled it into submission.

"Which did you fold?"

He inspected them. "I don't want to answer that."

"Excellent. You can still surprise me."

They left by the dry corridor, where the sound of the pool dropped away behind the door. It was colder outside than it had been at sunset. The ground had developed a crust at the edges of the path, and Thalia had to watch where she placed her boots. Rhys offered an arm at the steep section. She considered being irritated, then took it and let him steady her over the two uneven steps.

"Not going to argue?" he asked.

"I might save it for something worthwhile."

"I'll keep a list."

"Don't. Clarisse has enough lists for the entire state."

They walked a little farther. At the fork, one path led up toward Cabin One and the other ran past the north arena to the Apollo cabin. Thalia stopped where the gravel had been packed smooth by the carts.

"Come with me?" Rhys asked.

She heard the invitation for what it was. No invented errand, no need to demonstrate that she was the sort of person who could walk alone after dark.

"For how long?"

"As long as you want. You're tired. I'm not pretending otherwise."

"I'm tired of everybody measuring my heel."

"Then we won't discuss your heel."

"What will we discuss?"

He considered. "Your terrifying opinions about the novice targets."

"They're not terrifying. They're correct."

"I knew we'd find something."

She went with him.

---

The cabin was unoccupied when they reached it. Rhys checked the slate by the door, put the two towels away, and stirred the coals until a small flame caught. Thalia stood by the bench watching him move around the room. Yesterday she had crossed its threshold on purpose for the first time. Tonight the room still felt particular, still held the memory of what they'd chosen, but it no longer made her feel as though she were entering a contest that required a brave face.

Rhys put the poker down. "Tea?"

"You're turning into an old man."

"You're welcome to make it yourself."

"I'd rather watch you ruin it."

He filled the kettle. She moved toward the workbench and picked up a length of blue thread that had been left beside a small leather case. A half-mended glove lay under it.

"Is this yours?"

"My mother's."

"Your mother sent you a glove?"

"She sent me two. I managed to tear one during the trip. I promised to fix it."

"You're bad at sewing."

"She'll know that, too."

He looked over her shoulder while she examined the seam. The cut ran across the palm, shallow but awkward to mend. The thread would be visible even if he matched the stitches properly.

"Let me," she said.

"You don't have to."

"I know. If I were required to, I would refuse."

He handed her the needle. She sat on the edge of the cot, where the lamp was stronger, and repaired the glove with firm little stitches. It was not complicated. Her hands knew the work from years of making too little cloth serve too many purposes.

Rhys sat beside her, close enough to watch but careful not to jostle her arm. She liked his attention on her fingers, though she would rather have swallowed a coin than admit it.

"My mother used to tell me not to waste time on people who could fix something themselves," he said.

"Good advice."

"She wasn't always right."

Thalia glanced at him. "Because she thought people shouldn't help?"

"Because sometimes fixing a thing together is the point."

"That's dangerously sentimental, Calder."

"Could be. Don't tell anyone."

She finished the last stitch, bit off the thread, and held the glove out. He examined it, his thumb brushing the seam, and looked genuinely impressed.

"I did know you were good at that," he said.

"Clearly."

"No. I mean I knew you could do it. I didn't know you'd do it for me."

That was worse than a compliment. It was accurate.

She set the glove aside. "There's a difference between helping someone and being put in charge of them."

"Yes."

"Don't make me your glove person."

He laughed. "I solemnly swear I won't."

She turned to face him properly. The lamplight left a pale line along his cheek. She had been aware of wanting to kiss him through the entire conversation, and she was tired of pretending that the feeling had to be disguised as something else.

She leaned in, watching him catch her intention a moment before their mouths met. His answering smile made her bolder.

The kiss was easy at first, then less so, because neither of them had stopped wanting the other since yesterday. He moved his hand to her cheek. She caught his wrist and held it there, not to prevent him from touching her but to keep him close while she took her time. He smiled against her mouth when she paused.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. You asked."

"Should I not?"

"No. I liked it."

"Don't get used to good manners."

"I wouldn't dream of it."

She kissed him again. When the kettle began to rattle, neither of them reached for it immediately. Rhys was the first to laugh, which was unfair because she'd just decided she was going to be the one who broke the silence.

"That will boil dry," he said.

"Then go save your kettle."

"You're very demanding."

"Only when I'm here."

"That's not what I've heard."

She shoved his shoulder lightly. He caught her hand, kissed her knuckles, and went to pour the tea.

There was a particular satisfaction in making him forget an argument halfway through.

She'd discovered it when they were discussing the glove. She was discovering it again now, when Rhys returned with the mugs and found her sitting on the cot looking so deliberately unimpressed that he put them down before asking what he'd done wrong.

"Nothing," she said.

"That's your nothing face?"

"You've been watching me."

"Hard not to."

She tilted her head toward the empty space beside her. He took the invitation, then immediately spoiled his advantage by sitting an unnecessarily respectful distance away.

"What's that?" she asked.

"A seat."

"Very funny."

"I thought I was in trouble."

"You might be."

"For what?"

She could have given him a dozen reasons. None would have explained what she actually wanted, and she was tired of making him solve a puzzle she already knew the answer to. So she reached for his hand, pulled him closer, and kissed him until the kettle and the rain and the folded towels were distant things she had no interest in discussing.

Rhys kissed her back with the kind of attention that could make her lose track of the room. She caught his collar between two fingers. He had changed out of his damp shirt after the hall, and the clean cotton was still warm from the stove. His hair brushed her cheek when he leaned toward her. When she smiled against his mouth he smiled too, and the resulting kiss was an untidy one.

"What?" he asked.

"You still smell like the pool."

"I washed."

"Not well."

"That is a devastating accusation."

She drew him nearer. "I'll survive."

He was laughing when he kissed her again, but the laughter faded. There were moments with him when she felt the full force of wanting something without having to disguise it as a challenge. She'd come here because she wanted to see him, because she wanted his hands in hers and his mouth against hers, because a day spent doing what Chiron told her had been dull and painful and she was tired of being watched for signs that she was failing.

Rhys stopped and looked at her. "You went quiet."

"I'm capable of it."

"I know."

"Don't sound so pleased."

"I'm trying to work out whether the silence is good."

She breathed out and rested her forehead against his. "It's good."

"All right."

"You could sound more convinced."

"You just told me."

"Fine."

She kissed the corner of his mouth. He turned his head and caught the next kiss properly. For the first time since she'd left the hall, her shoulders relaxed.

The lamp threw a small gold circle across the quilt. Outside the cabin, a gust shook the shutter and made the door creak against its latch. The ordinary sounds seemed far away while she sat with him, her hand at the back of his neck and his arm around her. It was strange to be able to ask for closeness and receive it without anybody trying to turn the exchange into a bargain.

Then her ankle slipped off the cushion.

She hissed and pulled away.

Rhys's hand left her waist at once. "You all right?"

"Yes. I forgot how feet work."

"Do you want to move?"

"Eventually. I'm considering a long-term grudge against gravity."

He reached for the pillow. She let him put it beneath her heel, then settled into the corner where the cot met the wall. The adjustment took all of thirty seconds, but she was ashamed of how quickly the pleasure of the moment had become irritation with herself.

"Don't," she said when she caught him looking at her.

"Don't what?"

"Decide I can't handle being here."

His expression sharpened. "I wasn't deciding anything."

"You saw me wince."

"Yes. It hurt. I understand the concept."

"Then stop looking as if you're going to send me to bed."

"I was about to ask whether you wanted to lean against me again."

Thalia stared at him.

"That's an option," he added.

She wanted to be furious. It would have been less embarrassing if he'd actually been patronizing her. Instead he had seen her stumble and offered the exact thing she wanted.

"You're impossible," she said.

"I'm getting that impression."

She moved closer. He slipped his arm around her, and she rested against him. Their joined hands lay on the quilt near her knee. She studied the marks on his fingers from the string and the faint stain of blue thread beneath one nail.

"I'm doing it again," she said.

"What?"

"Treating you like somebody who's about to tell me what I'm allowed to do."

"Sometimes I will tell you you're doing something foolish."

"I know."

"I'd prefer you didn't get angry before I have the chance."

That made her laugh, reluctantly. "No promises."

"I thought not."

She looked up at him. There was a seriousness in his face that had been obscured during the kisses, and she found she wanted to understand it.

"What happens if I say I don't want to leave yet?" she asked.

"Then you don't leave yet."

"You've got work tomorrow."

"At seven."

"You need sleep."

"Eventually."

"Those are terrible answers."

"They're the answers I've got."

She traced the seam of his cuff. "I don't want you assuming I'm coming over every time I have a bad day."

"I don't."

"Or that I'm going to be easy to live with."

"I have noticed the evidence against that."

She gave him a pointed look.

"Thalia," he said, more quietly, "I like being here with you. I don't need a guarantee that every evening will go well."

"What if I turn up and I'm in a terrible mood?"

"I might be in one too."

"What a charming pair."

"We can be miserable at opposite ends of the room."

She laughed, and he leaned in to kiss her. She met him halfway. Their mouths parted; she caught him again. The second kiss was slow enough that she felt herself making a choice rather than reacting to his. She could end it. She could ask for more. She could stay where she was with no plan beyond wanting to remain.

"Better?" he asked.

"Considerably."

"Glad to be of service."

"Don't ruin it."

He rested his face beside hers. She liked the warmth of his cheek, the slight scrape when he turned his head, the way he never seemed embarrassed by the fact that he liked to touch her. She had never been embarrassed by wanting. She was embarrassed by being seen wanting, which was a different thing, and she was learning that he had no particular interest in exposing her for it.

"Can I tell you a terrible secret?" he asked.

"You've got an alarming number of those."

"I kept thinking about last night."

She looked at him sideways. "During training?"

"Not during the part with the arrows."

"How conscientious."

"During the time between."

"Calder, there was hardly any time between."

"I know."

She grinned. "You are a disgrace to your cabin."

"I've been told."

She caught his face between her hands and kissed him again. He went still for a fraction of a second, then leaned into her with an eagerness he had stopped trying to hide. She liked that, too. The ability to surprise him remained a small, delightful advantage. This time she used it simply because she could.

Eventually they drew apart and sat breathing quietly, foreheads resting together.

"You're going to be very annoying tomorrow," she said.

"I don't see why I'd wait."

"Exactly."

She could hear voices crossing the path beyond the shutter. Someone knocked on the neighboring cabin and called for a misplaced quiver. Rhys made no move to see who it was. He was entirely occupied with the girl beside him, and for once she didn't have the impulse to make a joke about the attention.

"I should go soon," she said.

"All right."

She waited for him to bargain. He didn't.

"But not yet."

His smile returned. "All right."

She took his hand, settled the aching foot comfortably, and stayed. They didn't have to pretend that stopping one kind of intimacy meant withdrawing from the other. That distinction turned out to be worth learning.


The rest of the evening was quieter. They drank from mismatched mugs and talked about nothing of consequence until the conversation found its way, as it inevitably would, to the question Thalia had been avoiding: whether she wanted this to happen again in a way that could be spoken aloud.

"I don't want an appointment," she said.

"Neither do I."

"I don't want you waiting for me every night, wondering if I'm coming."

"I wouldn't."

"You're going to say that now."

"I'm saying it because it's true."

She set down her mug. "And I don't want to be one more job you have to organize around the winter list."

Rhys leaned back against the bedframe and rubbed at a thumbprint of blue thread stuck to his sleeve. "You're not a job. But I do like knowing when I might see you. That isn't the same thing as putting you on a roster."

She knew he was right. That did not make the distinction comfortable.

"I could come by Thursday," she said. "If you're free."

"I should be. I have work until six."

"Then I won't interrupt it."

"Thalia, you can interrupt me sometimes."

"How generous."

"I'll try not to put that on a plaque."

She smiled despite herself. "Thursday, then."

"Thursday."

He came to sit beside her, not as a reward for reaching an agreement but because they were alone and both wanted him there. She rested her head briefly against his shoulder, an intimacy that seemed to surprise her more than any of the kissing had.

It would have been sensible to leave as soon as the tea was finished.

Thalia did not feel especially sensible.

Rhys had put the cups away and returned to the cot, where she was sitting with the blanket caught beneath her knee. He bent to retrieve a fallen book from the floor, and she caught his sleeve before he straightened.

"Come back."

"I was going to put this away."

"It can survive on the floor."

He looked at the book, then at her hand around his wrist. His expression changed with a slowness she found almost unbearable.

"All right," he said.

She drew him down beside her. He was smiling before she kissed him, and he wasn't smiling when she finally drew back. She liked the difference. She liked knowing that she could unsettle him without being cruel and that he could do the same to her. Their mouths met again. She could feel the pleasant shiver of anticipation before he leaned closer, the brief moment when both of them could still choose to stop. Then there was the warmth of him and the quiet relief of not having to pretend she wanted anything else.

He rested a hand against her cheek. She covered it with hers, keeping him close while the kiss slowed.

"You're going to ruin my schedule," he murmured.

"Good."

"I have to be awake at six."

"So do I."

"You wake at four."

"That's why I'm better than you."

He laughed against her mouth. "Naturally."

She had meant to make another joke, but he kissed her again and the thought went away. There was no contest in this, not really. She had been trying to turn the evening into one because she knew how to win a contest. She was only beginning to learn how to stay in a pleasant moment without demanding that it prove something.

She drew back, touched his face, and let herself look.

"What?" he asked.

"I was wondering what you were like before I started annoying you."

"Quieter."

"Less interesting."

"Possibly."

"Did you expect this?"

He shook his head. "I hoped for it."

There was something in the simple answer that made her want to move closer again. She did. She kissed him slowly, then rested her forehead against his and listened to him breathe.

"You could have told me," she said.

"Would you have believed me?"

"Probably not."

"Exactly."

She laughed. Her heel was comfortable against the cushion, and the cabin felt very far away from the noisy hall and the winter lists. For a moment she wondered whether she could spend the entire evening here without having to explain herself to anyone.

Then she remembered she had told Annabeth she'd be back, and she sat up.

"I should go."

Rhys looked at her. "All right."

She frowned. "You could sound a little disappointed."

"I am disappointed. I also know you said you wanted to go."

"You're alarmingly good at that distinction."

"I'm getting practice."

She kissed him again, briefly. Then another time, less briefly, because the first had been a poor farewell. He was laughing when she finally let him stand.

"You're terrible at leaving," he said.

"I've been told."

"By whom?"

"Very rude people."

He fetched her coat. When she put it on, the borrowed sleeves nearly covered her hands. Rhys folded one back, then the other, with an expression of concentration as though it were an important piece of work. Thalia watched him, finding the tenderness of the gesture almost harder to bear than the kissing.

"Don't start," she said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You've got the face."

"Which one?"

"The one that makes me want to stay."

He smiled. "That's not an expression I'm planning to stop making."

She reached for him one last time. The kiss was unhurried, and when they separated she let her hands fall from his shoulders. The evening wasn't being cut short. It was ending because she had chosen an ending, and she liked knowing there would be another time to choose differently.

She picked up the coat's loose cuff, tugged it down over her fingers, and went toward the door before she could reconsider for a seventh time.


At the door, he didn't ask her to stay. She had said she was going home, and he took her at her word. He walked her as far as the porch. Outside, the cold made her eyes water. She drew the borrowed coat closer.

"You're still wearing Percy's coat," he said.

"He's not getting it back until spring."

"Does he know that?"

"He suspects."

Rhys kissed her once on the threshold. There was nothing dramatic about it, and that was its own kind of danger. She could imagine doing it a hundred times, each one different enough to matter.

"Thursday," he said.

"Don't be late."

"It's my cabin."

"Then you have no excuse."

She stepped down into the dark. Behind her he closed the door against the cold. She was halfway past the first lantern before she realized that for nearly an hour she hadn't thought about whether she was walking correctly. The heel began to ache again as soon as she noticed. She swore at it, then continued up the path with the kind of private smile she hoped nobody would see.

---

Wednesday, the twenty-fifth of October, arrived wet and unusually quiet.

Thalia knew it was raining before she opened her eyes because the roof of Cabin One announced every drop. It was a narrow wooden roof, and the sound came down through the rafters with surprising intimacy: taps against the boards at first, then a prolonged rush when the wind turned and sent the rain across the shingles. She lay awake and listened. Annabeth was still asleep. The youngest camper in the next room was snoring with the dedicated enthusiasm of someone who had never known responsibility for other people's rest.

She ought to have been thinking about the training schedule. Chiron wanted the archery instructors to agree on the winter rotation, and someone had to inspect the target room before another cold front came through. But Thalia had already read the schedule twice. She could have recited every name on it.

What she kept remembering was Rhys at the door the night before, his hand at the small of her back when she'd kissed him goodbye, the way he'd stood in the doorway after she stepped away as if he wasn't quite ready to go inside. She had looked back once. He'd been watching her, not dramatically, simply with the expression of someone who liked the sight of her leaving and would have preferred she stay.

It was not a dignified memory for an eighteen-year-old woman to replay four times before breakfast.

She got dressed without turning on the lamp. Her heel ached when she pulled the sock over it, and she adjusted the wrap Chiron had given her. That was the one part of her life that had been explained in exhaustive detail lately, with the additional benefit that everyone seemed interested in offering an opinion.

Annabeth opened one eye.

"You're early."

"I live here."

"That wasn't my observation."

"Then improve your observations."

Annabeth turned onto her back and watched Thalia finish fastening her boot. "You got back later than you said."

"Not much."

"Forty minutes."

"Has someone been keeping records?"

"Yes."

Thalia looked over. Her sister was smiling. Not broadly, and not in the manner of a child who had discovered a secret and intended to torment its owner. It was worse. Annabeth looked pleased, and a little uncertain about whether she was allowed to be.

"Did you need me?" Thalia asked.

"No."

"Then don't count my minutes."

"I wasn't worried."

"That's new."

"I was curious."

Thalia stood, testing the support wrap. The boot fit better today. "Curiosity killed something."

"A monster, usually. You taught me that."

It was such a direct little reminder of their history that Thalia almost laughed. She crossed to her sister's bunk and straightened the blanket that had twisted at the edge.

"Stop doing that," Annabeth said.

"Doing what?"

"Fixing things when you don't want to answer."

Thalia left the blanket alone.

There was no practical reason to lie. Annabeth was fourteen, her younger sister, and perfectly entitled to dislike being treated like an interrogation device. But Thalia was also eighteen and had begun to discover that there were parts of her life she wanted to keep entirely her own.

"I spent time with Rhys," she said.

"I know."

"We were talking."

"That part I didn't know."

Thalia narrowed her eyes. Annabeth's expression became carefully innocent.

"Go back to sleep," Thalia said.

"Can't. I'm awake now."

"Then do something useful. Your ruler's about to fall off the table."

Annabeth reached out and caught the ruler without looking. Her smile widened.

Thalia went outside before it could turn into a full discussion. She was halfway down the porch steps when she realized she was smiling too.

The rain had made the path black and shining. She crossed the camp toward the dining pavilion and found that the morning crew had already put out porridge, toast, and a jar of honey that looked suspiciously as though someone had scraped it clean with a spoon and then tried to put the evidence back. She took a bowl and sat at the end of the bench nearest the open side.

Rhys came in carrying two wrapped bows.

He saw her immediately. That was the first problem. The second was how much it pleased her. His expression changed just enough for her to recognize it, a quick private smile before he had even said good morning.

He set the bows beside the supply table and brought his breakfast over.

"Mind if I sit?"

"I was enjoying the solitude."

"Then I'll try not to interfere."

He sat down.

She looked at his porridge. He had put honey on top, an outrageous quantity of it, and was making no attempt to disguise the fact.

"You're going to regret that."

"Good morning to you too."

"That's enough honey for four people."

"How many people are watching?"

"At least two. I'm sure somebody else will notice."

"I'll live with the scandal."

They ate for a minute. Thalia knew exactly where his hand was on the bench, close to hers but not touching. She was aware of other people moving around the pavilion and of the entirely ordinary fact that they had the right to eat breakfast without announcing their private lives to the camp. That ought to have been comfortable. Instead she was conscious of the effort both of them were making to behave as if nothing had changed.

He put his spoon down.

"You're staring at my hand."

"I was assessing the structural integrity of the bench."

"Verdict?"

"Poor."

"Terrible news."

She leaned closer, enough to keep her voice out of range of the campers passing behind them.

"I didn't sleep much."

His expression changed. "Bad heel?"

"Bad thoughts."

"About?"

She gave him a look.

He caught the meaning at once. For a second the composure vanished, replaced by a smile so open that she had to look away before she did something stupid, like reach across the table and kiss him.

"That makes two of us," he said.

"You're not supposed to say that at breakfast."

"Where should I say it?"

"Somewhere without an audience."

"Very strict rules."

"Reasonable ones."

He leaned back and picked up his spoon. A child from Demeter's cabin squeezed between them to reach the honey, and the conversation had to wait while Rhys moved his elbow out of the way. Thalia watched the kid depart with a piece of toast dripping down both sides.

"See?" she said. "Contagious."

Rhys laughed. "You're blaming me for the honey now?"

"I blame you for several things."

"Will I get a written list?"

"Not where anyone can find it."

The remark sounded more suggestive than she'd intended. His expression told her he had noticed. She hadn't realized how much she would enjoy being the one to make him lose his train of thought.

Then he lowered his voice.

"I wanted to see you this morning."

The words were ordinary, and they went straight through her defenses.

"You are seeing me."

"I know. I'm glad."

There was no request attached. He didn't ask where she'd been or whether she intended to come to his cabin again. She wished, suddenly, that she could take his hand under the table without thinking about who might see. She could have done it. It was only a hand. But she knew it would mean something visible, and she wasn't ready to decide the matter between one bite of porridge and the next.

So she said, "I might come by after the range."

"All right."

"Not a promise."

"I didn't ask for one."

She nodded, satisfied and faintly annoyed by his reasonableness. Then she stole a piece of his toast.

"Thalia."

"What?"

"You have your own."

"Yours looks better."

"It's the same bread."

"Then you won't miss it."

He watched her eat it, smiling. For a while they said nothing. There was a particular comfort in sitting together in public while each of them held a private memory of the night before. She had never considered that secrecy might be pleasant when it didn't come from shame. It could simply be space between two people, something they hadn't yet invited anyone else to occupy.

When breakfast ended, Rhys stood and collected the empty bowls.

"Field in twenty minutes," he said.

"I know the schedule."

"I suspected you might."

She glanced around. Nobody was looking at them. Before she could talk herself out of it, she touched his wrist as he reached past her.

He looked down.

She could have said something sharp, made the gesture mean nothing. Instead she let her fingers rest against his skin for one heartbeat, then withdrew.

"See you there," she said.

His smile was quiet. "See you there."

She watched him walk toward the dish return with the bowls stacked in his hands. She had spent the better part of her life believing that affection was something you protected other people with, a set of actions aimed away from yourself. The realization that she could receive it without first offering an explanation left her oddly unsteady.

A moment later Annabeth appeared in the pavilion carrying a notebook, a ruler, and a piece of toast.

"You look happy," she said.

"That's an accusation."

"Only if you make it one."

Thalia stole half her toast as well.

"Hey."

"Structural inspection," Thalia said.

Annabeth rolled her eyes and sat down beside her. Thalia kept the rest of the morning's private happiness to herself, but she stopped trying so hard to hide that it existed.

---

The range was empty by late afternoon. A rain shower had sent most of the instructors into the storage shed, leaving the field smelling of wet straw and trampled grass. Thalia found Rhys at the equipment bench taking the string off a practice bow.

"You didn't eat," she said.

He looked up. "I did."

"An apple isn't lunch."

"You've been watching my lunch?"

"Someone has to maintain standards."

He looked behind her at the deserted path. "Are you here on official business?"

"I have a complaint."

"That sounds official."

"Your bow rack is crooked."

"It's always been crooked."

"Then it's a long-standing complaint."

He set the bow down and gave her his attention. She knew that expression: the quiet, almost amused patience he used when he could tell she'd come with a subject she didn't really want to name.

She stepped closer. The field was almost deserted, and she was suddenly tired of pretending that every visit to him required some useful excuse.

"I wanted to see you," she said.

His face changed. The grin he had been holding back became more open.

"Then I'm glad you're here."

"You're supposed to make this difficult."

"I've tried that. You don't enjoy it as much as you think."

She glanced toward the path. "I don't want the others involved."

"Then we'll keep our business our own."

"And I don't want you acting strange at work."

"What does strange look like?"

"Looking at me every five minutes."

"I'm doing that already."

"Exactly. Be less obvious."

He laughed. She moved close enough to take his hand, and he threaded his fingers between hers. Neither of them made the gesture into a promise. It was a simple choice, made in a quiet corner of the range after a long day.

"Are you coming by tonight?" he asked.

"I don't know."

"Fair answer."

"Don't sound disappointed."

"I'm a little disappointed. That's allowed."

She looked up at him, surprised by the simple honesty. He hadn't tried to make the disappointment her problem. He hadn't hidden it either.

"I might be tired," she said.

"Then get some rest."

"I might want to see you."

"Then come see me."

"You're making a terrible habit of being reasonable."

"You'll survive."

She kissed him. It was a quick, private kiss, the kind she had spent the entire afternoon wanting to give him. He was smiling when she drew back.

"What?" she asked.

"I like when you tell me what you want."

"Don't expect speeches."

"I didn't ask for one."

He took her other hand and brought it to his chest. They stood together for a moment while the wind moved through the pines and the last light caught on the wet target frames.

"I won't always be easy to be around," she said.

"Neither will I."

"That's not a persuasive answer."

"It's an accurate one."

She found herself smiling despite the irritation. He was not trying to convince her that this would be effortless. That was one of the reasons she'd come back.

She squeezed his fingers and let go.

"Good night," she said.

"Good night. Maybe."

She turned at the corner of the shed. "Don't get clever."

"Too late."

He was still smiling as she left. She kept the private warmth of the conversation all the way up the hill, and she did not pretend she had gone to the range to inspect a bow rack.

---

The decision not to go to Rhys's cabin survived precisely until supper.

Thalia sat through half a bowl of lentils, listened to Clarisse explain that somebody had put the new lantern wicks in the wrong drawer, and tried not to look toward the north path every time a person crossed the pavilion. She told herself she was entitled to a night alone. She had had plenty of them before this week; she had always managed to fill the hours. She could read, check the boot wrap, go to bed and wake tomorrow without making a production of liking someone.

Then Annabeth said, "Would you please stop moving your spoon?"

Thalia looked down. She'd been pushing the lentils in a slow circle for several minutes.

"Eat," Annabeth added. "Or go do whatever you're thinking about."

"I wasn't thinking about anything."

"Then you've been conducting a fascinating experiment on soup."

Thalia ate three decisive spoonfuls. It proved nothing. By the time the plates were cleared, she had made up her mind that she wanted to see Rhys, and once she'd admitted it, there was no pleasure in sitting around pretending otherwise.

She walked north without the borrowed coat this time. The cold was sharper than she had expected, and she was halfway up the path when she considered going back for it. That would have been sensible. She kept walking.

There was light in the cabin window. She stepped onto the porch and knocked.

The sound surprised her. On Monday she'd made a point of entering without asking, as though making a dramatic entrance might resolve all the questions she'd been carrying. Now she had an ordinary reason to knock: he might not want company. He might be working, or tired, or in the middle of a letter to his mother. She didn't want to assume the right to interrupt merely because they'd kissed.

She knocked a second time.

Nobody answered.

The door wasn't locked. She could have opened it and waited by the stove. She had done that before, and it wouldn't have been dangerous or particularly scandalous. But the thought stopped her: she was about to turn a possibility into a claim. She had decided she wanted him; that did not oblige him to be waiting.

She sat on the porch step instead.

After ten minutes the cold had worked into her hands. She regretted not bringing the coat with a force out of proportion to the problem. She could go home. Rhys might be another hour. The idea of admitting she'd come all this way for nothing was irritating enough that she stayed another five minutes.

A figure came up the path carrying a paper bag and a rolled cloth.

"Thalia?"

Rhys stopped beneath the porch lamp. He looked genuinely surprised.

"I didn't know you were coming."

"I didn't either until about twenty minutes ago."

He took in her bare hands and the thin shirt under her jacket. "You look frozen."

"I'm entirely capable of being frozen."

"That's very impressive."

He came up the steps and put the paper bag down. His expression was warm, but she could see him thinking.

"Did you try the door?" he asked.

"I knocked."

"Twice?"

"Yes."

"Why didn't you go in?"

She considered the question. "Because you weren't here."

"That's the usual reason to go inside and wait."

"Not when you're not expecting me."

He looked at her for a moment, then smiled.

"I like that you thought about it."

"Don't praise me for basic manners."

"I'm not. I'm saying it matters."

He opened the door and stood aside. She went in first, glad of the heat, and found the cabin unchanged since the last time she'd left it. A bow lay on the bench. The blue thread had been wound neatly onto a spool. The slate by the door carried the laundry instructions under two lines of camp gossip, both written in different hands.

Rhys put the paper bag on the table.

"What is that?" she asked.

"Dinner."

"You already ate?"

"No. I was at the kitchen. They had extra bread and some stew that won't survive another day."

"I ate."

"That's tragic. You'll have to watch."

He found a bowl and spoon. She remained by the stove until the feeling returned to her fingers, then took the chair opposite him. He ate with the unembarrassed appetite of someone who had spent the day carrying equipment. It was unexpectedly nice to watch. She was used to his careful concentration over a bow or his attention when she spoke. Here he was simply hungry, spilling a little stew onto the table and cursing his own carelessness.

"Is that how you're going to eat when I'm here?" she asked.

"It's how I usually eat when someone isn't inspecting me."

"An appalling standard."

"You can file a complaint."

She reached across and wiped the spill with the edge of a cloth. He caught her hand gently.

"Leave it," he said. "I'll clean up when I'm finished."

"I was helping."

"I know."

She looked at him. There was no criticism in his voice, but she had come into the room and immediately started fixing something that wasn't her responsibility.

"You do that a lot," he said.

"Stop me?"

"Turn into an instructor when nobody asked you."

She withdrew her hand. "I can stop."

"I wasn't asking you to stop being useful. I was asking you to be here."

That was an answer she couldn't immediately fight. She settled back in the chair. He finished his stew, washed the bowl himself, and came to sit beside her.

"Better?" he asked.

"Marginally."

He smiled. "Good."

They sat in silence. She was aware that she had arrived without a plan and that he hadn't asked for one. It was a relief. She'd thought she needed a reason to come, and she'd spent fifteen minutes in the cold making sure she wasn't intruding. Now she was here, and nobody was treating the visit as an obligation.

Rhys touched her hand. "Can I ask you something?"

"You're going to anyway."

"Do you want a night like Monday, or do you just want to sit with me?"

She stared at him. The question was plain enough, yet she felt embarrassed by how much she appreciated being asked.

"I don't know," she said.

"That's allowed."

"I'm not usually undecided."

"You've been undecided about almost everything this week."

"That's a vicious accusation."

"It's a factual one."

She gave him a sharp look, but he was smiling. She took his hand and moved closer, enough that their knees touched.

"I wanted to see you," she said. "I didn't think much beyond that."

"Then we can start there."

She kissed him. Not to end the conversation, but because the conversation had reached a place where there was nothing left to negotiate before she could do it. He kissed her back, one hand warm against her shoulder, and she felt the frustration of the day dissolve without needing to explain each piece of it.

When she drew away, she stayed close.

She stayed near him, letting the smile on his face answer the question she had almost asked. It bothered her in the nicest possible way.

"You know what?" she said.

"What?"

"I don't think I have to win every conversation with you."

"That will be a relief to the entire camp."

"Don't push it."

He laughed, and she leaned in to kiss him again.

For a while they were quiet. The room had its own rhythm: the stove settling, the occasional creak of the boards, the thin sound of rain beginning against the window. She liked that they could be together without a declared purpose. She could rest against him and still feel like herself. She could speak, or not, without having the silence interpreted as trouble.

Then Rhys said, "I have to tell you something."

Thalia stiffened.

"It's not terrible," he added.

"Then don't introduce it that way."

He glanced toward the workbench. "My mother wrote this morning. She needs me to look over a few things at home before winter is over."

The words took a moment to settle. She knew he had a life outside camp. She knew he had left before and would leave again. Knowing didn't prevent the first small, unreasonable sting.

"When?"

"I don't know yet. Nothing immediate."

"Then why tell me now?"

"Because you were sitting on my porch waiting for me, and I thought you deserved to know there's a part of my life that might take me away for a while."

She looked at him. It would have been easy to turn the conversation into an argument about whether he had a right to leave. She didn't want to do that. He did have a right. So did she.

"I don't want to make you choose between this and your family," she said.

"I wouldn't ask you to."

"And I don't want you making decisions for me because you think I'll react badly."

He nodded. "I won't."

"You have to tell me when you know."

"I will."

She studied his face for a moment and found that she believed him. Not because he'd said it beautifully, but because he had been willing to say the awkward thing before it became urgent.

"Fine," she said.

"Fine?"

"Fine. Now kiss me again."

Relief crossed his face. He came to her willingly, and the kiss was not an answer to the question of whether he would leave. It was only the answer to what she wanted from him right now. They had both said what needed saying. The future could remain unfinished without spoiling the present.

She stayed until the lamp burned low. At the door he found the spare scarf and wrapped it around her neck, ignoring her claim that she wasn't cold. She accepted it after a brief argument, mostly because she'd arrived without a scarf and knew perfectly well that she was cold.

"Bring it back," he said.

"Maybe."

"You're going to run out of reasons to visit if you keep stealing my clothes."

She smiled. "I'll invent new ones."

He looked pleased by that. She kissed him once more and went down the path, the scarf warm against her face, already thinking of how she would tell him that his stew had looked terrible and that she'd liked his company anyway.

---

Thursday began with the kind of bright cold that made every metal surface painful to touch.

Thalia had Rhys's scarf tucked under her coat. She hadn't decided whether to return it. Wearing it on the way to breakfast felt faintly ridiculous; leaving it behind had felt worse. In the end she wound it once around her throat and let the loose end disappear beneath the collar. It was plain dark wool, and anyone looking at her would have seen nothing worth remarking on.

She knew exactly where it had come from.

The field was already busy when she arrived. She had no plan to spend long there. Chiron's instructions remained clear, the heel had improved just enough to tempt her into mistakes, and the Thursday beginners were supposed to be an easy session. Rhys was walking the north side of the range, checking the ropes at the far targets.

He looked up as she approached.

"That's mine."

She followed his gaze to the scarf. "Really? I've had it for years."

"Remarkable. I must have been losing it for a very long time."

"You should take better care of your things."

"I was beginning to think you were volunteering."

She stopped beside him. The sun had only just cleared the ridge, and the backs of the targets still carried white beads of frost. Nobody was close enough to hear them.

"Do you want it back?" she asked.

He considered. "Not particularly."

"Then stop complaining."

"I'm not complaining. I'm appreciating how well it suits you."

She felt her face threaten to betray her. She looked toward the targets instead. There was an enormous advantage to having a professional rivalry with someone, and that was the ability to turn away and examine equipment without appearing shy.

"That knot's coming loose," she said.

"Which knot?"

"The one at the far post."

Rhys looked. "That's not loose."

"It will be after the thaw."

He took a moment, then started walking toward the far post. She followed, amused by how easily she'd redirected the conversation. But halfway down the range he slowed to match her careful pace.

"Did you sleep?" he asked.

"Some."

"Good."

"I did not say I slept well."

"Did you?"

She considered. "Better than Monday."

"That's an encouraging trend."

"You're going to take credit for it."

"I was thinking about it."

She glanced sideways. He was smiling with the easy certainty of someone who had finally stopped pretending there was no connection between them. She liked it. She also resented how obvious her liking might become if she let herself keep looking.

At the far post, the knot was sound. Rhys pulled on it once, then looked at her.

"You lied."

"I offered a prediction."

"You predicted a knot would come loose?"

"Eventually, all knots do."

"Very philosophical."

"Don't tell anyone."

He leaned against the post and folded his arms. "You could have just asked me to walk with you."

"Could I?"

"Yes."

She looked at him. "Then I wanted you to walk with me."

His face changed. That was becoming the reward she didn't want to admit she sought. Not obedience. Recognition. He looked at her as though she'd handed him something he'd been waiting to receive, and he was careful not to squeeze the life out of it by asking for more.

"I'm glad you did," he said.

She gave his arm a small shove. "Come on. We're going to be late."

The lesson itself passed without incident. Thalia found she was improving at leaving her bad foot out of the work. She could demonstrate a stance while seated. She could offer a correction without rushing forward to take someone's bow out of their hands. When the students finished, she sat on the equipment bench and discovered that her leg was tired but not aching.

Rhys came over and sat beside her.

"Better?"

She rolled her eyes. "You sound like Chiron."

"One of the great tragedies of my life."

"It's improving."

"Good."

She reached for his hand, cautiously, because three instructors were still gathering arrows at the far end of the range. He let her take it. They sat together in full view, not making a show of anything, and it was the most daring thing she'd done all morning.

"Is this going to make you uncomfortable?" she asked.

He turned toward her. "Holding hands?"

"Being seen."

"Not with you."

She looked away. The answer was too direct to deflect.

"I don't want every person here having an opinion," she said.

"They will anyway."

"Helpful."

"They already have opinions about your target scores. About my cooking. About whether the hall needs another safety line. We don't get to control every opinion."

"We can control what we tell them."

"Yes."

She traced one of the worn seams of the scarf with her free hand. "That's what I want. For now."

He nodded. "Then that's what we'll do."

"What does that mean?"

"That I won't talk about anything private without asking you first. And you don't have to pretend not to know me when we're together in public."

She considered. It was a sensible arrangement, which made her suspicious.

"You've thought about this," she said.

"Since breakfast yesterday."

"How long?"

"Enough."

Thalia watched the last of the students leave the field. A bowstring twanged once from the storage shed, where someone was testing tension before putting equipment away. The sound faded. She and Rhys remained on the bench, their hands together between them.

"All right," she said.

He smiled. "All right."

She moved closer and kissed him once, brief but unmistakable. The sky didn't split. Nobody ran screaming from a cabin. They were two adults sitting on an old bench after a morning's work, and she was kissing the man she'd wanted to kiss since the day he'd corrected her elbow.

When she drew back, Rhys was looking at her with such obvious affection that she felt a surge of pleasure followed immediately by the need to be sarcastic.

"You're going to get insufferable."

"I had excellent teachers."

She laughed. The kiss had been public in the small sense that anyone nearby could have seen it. She hadn't died of embarrassment, and Rhys hadn't transformed into a spectacle. She was relieved by that, although she would rather have swallowed a nail than admit it.

"Tonight?" he asked.

She knew exactly what he meant. The question brought back the cabin, the quiet, the privacy of their last few evenings together.

"After six?"

"After six."

"I might come."

"I'll be there."

She squeezed his fingers one last time before letting go. They stood together and carried the practice bows into storage, arguing about whether the damaged leather cases should be replaced or repaired. It was a normal argument. They could have had it before anything changed between them. Now it took place with a private promise underneath, and she found she liked that almost as much as she liked the promise itself.

---

Friday evening brought the first honest frost to the north side of the cabin.

Thalia had spent the afternoon in the library pretending to read a book about old military formations. She'd understood perhaps two pages. The rest of the time she had watched the clock, irritated by its persistence in advancing at the same rate whether she wanted it to or not. She had promised herself she wouldn't go to Rhys every night. Nobody had required the promise. It had grown in her head as a defense against finding herself attached.

At a quarter past seven, she shut the book and went to find him.

This time the cabin door was open. The stove was lit, and a soft, unfamiliar sound floated into the hallway. It took her a moment to recognize a guitar. Not one of the bright strummed songs Apollo's children sometimes played around the campfire, but a low, careful progression with spaces left between the notes.

Rhys sat on the edge of the cot with an old acoustic guitar across his lap. His sleeves were rolled, his head bent, and he was concentrating so intently that he didn't hear her arrive. The sight stopped her. She had grown accustomed to seeing him competent on the range. This was something else: a private skill practiced without an audience, with the awkward repetition of somebody trying to make a small piece of music sound exactly right.

He missed a note, swore softly, and started the measure again.

"You're better with a bow," she said.

He jumped. The wrong string buzzed beneath his fingers.

"Gods, Thalia."

"An impressive performance."

"You could knock."

"The door was open."

"That doesn't mean you should announce your arrival by criticizing my playing."

"Would you prefer applause?"

"No."

"Then I made the correct choice."

He set the guitar aside, but she reached for the neck before he could put it away.

"Keep playing."

He blinked. "You just told me I was terrible."

"I said you were better with a bow. That's different."

"That might be the most generous distinction you've ever made."

"Don't waste it."

He studied her for a second, then settled back with the guitar. She sat on the opposite cot and listened. The melody had a simple shape, but he kept stopping at the same transition, playing it twice and shaking his head. Eventually she understood what he was trying to do. He wanted the last note of one phrase to linger beneath the first note of the next. His fingers weren't quite cooperating.

"Slow down," she said.

"I'm already going slowly."

"No. You're playing the first part slowly and rushing through the part you don't trust."

He looked up. "You play?"

"A little."

"Since when?"

"Since I was thirteen and found an abandoned instrument in a closet. I wasn't very good."

"You didn't mention that."

"You've never asked."

He considered that, then handed her the guitar. It was a simple gesture, so unguarded that she felt unexpectedly pleased. He wasn't showing her off or challenging her. He wanted to know what she could do.

She settled the instrument across her knees, adjusted the position so the sore heel didn't pull, and played the troublesome transition at half speed. Her fingers were rusty. She missed one note herself, caught the mistake, and kept going.

Rhys listened without correcting her.

"That," she said. "The pause is the point."

He took the guitar back and tried again. This time the note held.

"There it is," she said.

He looked absurdly delighted.

"You're pleased with yourself," she added.

"You're an excellent teacher."

"I'm going to regret letting you know that."

"Probably."

He set the guitar down properly, resting it in the narrow space beside the workbench. Then he turned toward her, close enough that she could see the pale mark where the strap had rubbed the side of his neck.

"What brought you here?" he asked.

"The music."

"You didn't know I was playing."

"Then your terrible playing summoned me from the library. It's a divine gift."

"Very plausible."

She smiled. "I wanted to see you."

His expression softened, and the room seemed to change with it. She was becoming familiar with that transformation: the moment he stopped treating her arrival as an interruption and began treating it as a choice she'd made for herself.

He reached for her hand. "Come here."

This time the request pleased her. She crossed the space between the cots and sat beside him. His shoulder was warm against hers. She could still feel the music in the quiet, the unfinished progression hanging between them.

"Play something else," she said.

"Are you going to grade it?"

"Mercilessly."

He laughed and picked up the guitar again. He played an old folk song she half recognized, then another with words he refused to sing because he said he couldn't remember all of them. She caught him lying when he hummed the missing verse under his breath.

"You know the words."

"I know some words."

"Sing."

"No."

"You're afraid of embarrassing yourself."

"With you? Constantly."

She leaned closer until her cheek touched his shoulder. "Good."

He sang a line, quietly, then another. His voice was not polished. It had a pleasing roughness when he dropped into the low notes. Thalia closed her eyes for a moment, aware of the almost embarrassing tenderness of sitting beside someone who was singing because she'd asked him to.

When the song ended, he put the guitar away.

"Was that acceptable?" he asked.

"Marginally."

"High praise."

She turned to him and kissed him. It had been waiting in her from the moment she saw him at the instrument, and she let it happen without announcing it. He caught her face between his hands and kissed her back, gentle at first, then with an eagerness that made her smile. She could feel him smiling too, which made the kiss less elegant and considerably more satisfying.

He drew back. "You're going to be impossible if you find out I can cook."

"I have already seen the evidence against that claim."

"One bad stew."

"An unforgettable bad stew."

"You're vicious."

She kissed him again before he could continue defending his cooking. They stayed close afterward, knees touching, with the guitar resting safely against the bench. The lamp burned beside the window, and the first flakes of frost were settling along the glass.

"What are we doing?" she asked suddenly.

He looked at her. "Sitting?"

"Rhys."

"I know what you mean."

"Then answer."

He thought for a moment. "I think we're spending time together because we want to. I think we'd both like more of it. Beyond that I don't know."

She wanted to dislike the incompleteness of the answer. Instead she found it relieved her. She had been half expecting a word that would require them to decide what they were to each other before they'd had time to discover it.

"Do you want a title?" she asked.

"Do you?"

"Not especially."

"Then we don't need one tonight."

She looked toward the guitar. "You know, most people would have used that as an excuse for a terribly romantic speech."

"I can try if you want."

"No."

"An immediate rejection."

"An act of mercy."

He laughed, and she pressed her face into the side of his neck. She liked the sound of him laughing when he wasn't protecting himself from an audience. She liked knowing that he could be nervous about something as ordinary as music, and she liked being invited to see it.

He put an arm around her shoulders. They sat quietly until the frost hid the view beyond the window.

When she eventually stood to leave, the room seemed too warm compared with the dark beyond the door. Rhys helped her find her coat, which she had dropped somewhere between the threshold and the chair. He held it open, and she slipped her arms into the sleeves.

"Are you going to play tomorrow?" she asked.

"Probably."

"Don't stop at that transition."

"You're giving me homework."

"I give excellent homework."

He touched her cheek. "Come by if you want to hear it again."

She looked at him. "I might."

"I was hoping you'd say that."

She kissed him once more. It was a private, deliberate farewell, not a promise of anything she couldn't guarantee. But as she walked down the path she carried the tune in her head, and later, lying awake in Cabin One, she found herself humming it under her breath.

She wasn't thinking about his bow or his ability to fix a broken hinge. She was thinking about the fact that he'd been willing to play a half-finished song with her in the room. It was a different sort of intimacy, and she wasn't ready to call it less important than the others.

---

Saturday was the first time Rhys proposed a plan that did not involve either his cabin or her injured foot.

"Half an hour," he said at breakfast. "After lunch. Flat ground."

"That sounds suspiciously like an appointment."

"It's a suggestion."

"Where?"

"Come with me and find out."

Thalia put down her mug. "Do you expect me to enjoy surprises?"

"Not necessarily."

"Excellent pitch."

He smiled. "You can say no."

She considered saying no just to see what he would do. The idea was childish and not particularly interesting. She had spent a week testing the limits of his patience. It was beginning to occur to her that she could use the same energy on something more enjoyable.

"Half an hour," she said.

"Half an hour."

"Flat ground."

"Flat ground."

"No elaborate romantic gesture."

He looked offended. "You take all the fun out of planning."

"Good."

At one, he met her by the west path with a small canvas bag. The cold had eased enough for the sun to show between the clouds, but the air still carried the promise of another frost by nightfall. He offered his arm at the muddy turn, and she took it without making a speech.

They walked toward the old orchard, a part of the camp that had been mostly empty since the harvest. A few late apples still hung on the higher branches, red against the bare grey wood. The ground was soft, but the path followed the edge of the fence and avoided the worst of the ruts.

"This is your plan?" she asked.

"You're difficult to impress."

"I am. Continue."

At the far end of the orchard stood a low stone wall. Rhys laid a folded blanket across it and took a small camera from his bag.

Thalia stopped.

"No."

"You haven't heard the idea."

"I see the camera."

"It's not a weapon."

"That depends on who uses it."

He turned it over in his hands. It was an old instant camera, scratched at the edges, with a strip of faded tape on the back. Someone had written APOLLO STORAGE in block letters across the tape.

"I borrowed it," he said. "The film's expensive. There are three exposures left."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted a picture of you."

She looked at him. The question should have been simple. She had pictures of herself in old family boxes, school records, the odd group shot taken at camp. A photograph wasn't a confession. But she disliked being told to smile for a person who would then possess a version of her she hadn't chosen.

"Ask properly," she said.

He lowered the camera. "Would you let me take a picture of you? You can keep the print, or we can put it away. I won't show anyone without asking."

She studied his face. It wasn't a bargaining tactic. He was offering her control over the image, which made it easier to consider the idea on its merits.

"What do you want it for?"

He looked down at the camera, then back up. "So when I go home, I can remember what you look like when you're laughing at me."

She gave him a look. "You could just remember."

"I could."

"Your memory is failing already?"

"Quite badly. I keep remembering the wrong things."

"Such as?"

"The way you look at me when you're trying not to smile."

That was unfair. Thalia turned toward the orchard so he wouldn't see exactly how effective the answer had been.

"One picture," she said.

"Only if you want."

"One. And you're not making me pose."

He held up the camera. "Agreed."

She sat on the wall with the blanket under her, her bad foot resting on the ground. He stood several paces back, adjusting the frame. She deliberately made a terrible face. The shutter clicked. The camera whirred, and a white-edged print emerged.

"That's your one," she said.

"You promised a picture, not an attractive picture."

"I didn't promise anything of the sort."

He handed it to her while the image developed. She watched her own face slowly appear, eyebrow raised, mouth twisting in a challenge. It was an entirely recognizable expression. She would have claimed to hate it if it had been taken by almost anyone else.

Rhys looked over her shoulder. "Perfect."

"You're blind."

"I'm an excellent judge."

"Of archery targets. Clearly not photographs."

He reached for the print, then stopped. She understood the question before he asked it.

"Can I keep it?" he said.

She looked down at the photograph again. She had never liked the idea of being captured without warning, her face made into evidence for somebody else's story. But she had chosen this. She had looked into the lens and made exactly the expression she wanted. There was no demand to look sweet or compliant or particularly beautiful.

"Keep it," she said. "But don't put it on the laundry slate."

He laughed. "I wouldn't dare."

They spent the next twenty minutes sitting on the wall. Rhys told her about the camera's history, how one of the Apollo campers had tried to use it during a rainy concert and ruined an entire packet of film. Thalia countered with a story about a camp photograph in which Annabeth had hidden behind a taller camper because she hated her hair that day. She found herself saying more than she'd intended about the younger girl, the exhausting years of keeping her safe, and how odd it had been to watch her become someone who could disagree with her openly and still come home.

Rhys listened without trying to turn the story into an explanation of Thalia's personality. When she finished, he asked about the picture.

"Do you want one together?"

She considered. They had two exposures left. A photograph of them would be a private record of something they hadn't yet named. She could imagine looking at it in a month and feeling foolish. She could also imagine wanting it.

"One," she said.

They set the camera on the stone wall, aimed it toward the path, and used the timer. Rhys sat beside her. The first time he leaned too far forward and blocked half her face; she shoved him back with her shoulder just as the camera clicked. The resulting picture showed a blur of movement, his surprised grin and the unmistakable evidence that she had been laughing.

She laughed again when she saw it.

"That's terrible."

"It's accurate."

"Give me that."

He held it above his head. She reached, then stopped, remembering the heel.

"Don't make me injure myself for a photograph."

"Then ask nicely."

She narrowed her eyes. "Please."

His grin widened. He gave it to her at once.

The final exposure remained in the camera. Rhys put it away without suggesting another. They stood and folded the blanket. The half hour had become nearly an hour, but neither of them seemed inclined to mention it.

On the path back, he took her hand. She let him. They passed two campers carrying a crate of apples, and one of them glanced at their joined fingers before looking away. Thalia felt the brief old impulse to let go, then found no real reason to obey it.

Rhys noticed the glance too. He didn't tighten his grip or challenge her to prove anything. He simply kept walking beside her.

At the fork she stopped and turned toward him.

"You can keep the first picture," she said.

"What about the second?"

"I'm keeping it."

"Fair division."

"And if you go home, you don't have to stare at it like an idiot."

He looked at her with mock gravity. "I'll do my best."

She stepped closer and kissed him. It was a small kiss in a patch of sunlight, away from the cabin and the familiar cover of lamplight. It left her feeling both conspicuous and entirely content.

When she drew away, he touched the pocket where he'd put her photograph.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't make a ceremony out of it."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking about it."

He laughed. She let go of his hand and started uphill, the shared photograph safe inside her coat. Later she would hide it in the back of her trunk, not out of shame but because it was hers. She liked knowing it existed. She liked even more that he had asked before making it.

---

On Sunday, the thirtieth minute of the afternoon was when Thalia discovered she had made a mistake.

She'd spent most of the morning imagining the next time she'd be alone with Rhys. The memory of the orchard photograph kept appearing whenever she tried to concentrate on something else, especially the ridiculous expression on his face when she'd shoved him before the shutter clicked. She had the print tucked inside a book in her trunk, and she had opened the book twice since breakfast without intending to read a word.

After lunch she went to his cabin.

He was there, but the room didn't have its usual welcoming disorder. The workbench had been cleared of bows. Instead it held three sheets of music, a folded letter, and a small pile of envelopes. Rhys sat with a pencil in his hand, frowning at a page of notes.

"You're busy," she said.

He looked up. His expression brightened before he glanced back at the page.

"A little. I promised my mother I'd finish this today."

"Finish what?"

"A song she wants to use at an event back home. The arrangement is a mess, and I'm trying to rewrite the middle part so it can be played by somebody who isn't sitting here with me."

Thalia stepped inside. She could have sat quietly until he finished. Instead she felt the disappointment arrive and take up all the room.

"I thought you were free."

"I didn't say I was."

"You said I could come by."

"I said you could come by when you wanted. I didn't promise I'd have the afternoon clear."

It was a fair distinction, and Thalia disliked hearing it. She stood by the doorway with her arms crossed. Rhys put the pencil down and turned fully toward her.

"What's wrong?"

"Nothing."

"That isn't nothing."

"I'm not interested in arguing."

"Then tell me what happened."

She looked at the music. The pages were covered in tight, carefully written notes, places where he'd erased a phrase and tried again. He had already spent hours on it. The work mattered to him. That was apparent even if he hadn't said so.

"I wanted to see you," she said.

"I'm glad you came."

"You're not acting glad."

He took a breath. She could see the effort it cost him not to answer sharply.

"Thalia, I am glad. I also need to finish something."

She could hear herself becoming unfair. She couldn't seem to stop. There was an old fear underneath it, one she recognized only once it was already speaking for her: that a person who wanted her when nothing else demanded attention might choose something else the moment it did.

"So finish," she said.

"I will."

She turned toward the door.

"Do you want to come back later?"

"No."

She was outside before he could reply. The walk down the north path was cold and unpleasant, and the heel complained every few steps. She told herself she had made the sensible choice: he had work, and she shouldn't interrupt it. Then she remembered her own words and knew the truth was less flattering.

She had wanted the afternoon to belong to her. When it didn't, she had punished him for having a life.

Back at Cabin One, the photograph lay inside her book where she'd left it. She took it out and stared at their blurred faces. Yesterday she'd liked that he could surprise her. Today she'd wanted him to be exactly where she'd imagined him, waiting to do exactly what she'd imagined they'd do. The contradiction made her angry, but for once the anger had nowhere useful to go.

She sat on the floor beside the trunk and considered what she ought to say to him.

She could write a note. No, that would be absurd. She could go back, apologize, and risk making another interruption. She could wait until tomorrow, by which time the apology would sound rehearsed.

After twenty minutes she realized she was doing the same thing she'd done the week before: inventing an elaborate problem to avoid a simple sentence.

She got up and went back.

The cabin lamp was on, and the window showed Rhys still at the workbench. This time she knocked. He opened the door after a moment.

"Thalia."

"I was rude."

He stood still.

"I wanted you to be free when I arrived. You weren't. I acted as if that was your fault."

He looked past her toward the darkening path. "Come in."

She did, but stayed near the door.

"I'm not asking you to stop working," she said.

"I know."

"And I'm not asking for an evening."

"I know."

She made herself look at him. He didn't appear angry, though he was more guarded than before.

"I just wanted you to know that I know I was wrong."

He took a slow breath. "All right."

"That's it?"

"What did you expect?"

"An argument, perhaps."

"I could provide one."

"Don't."

He smiled faintly. "Then we can leave it there."

She considered. "Are you still upset?"

"A little. I don't want you coming here and feeling as though you have to compete with everything else in my life. But I also don't want to be made to feel guilty because I spent an afternoon keeping a promise to my mother."

There it was. She felt the familiar impulse to defend herself and, this time, allowed it to pass.

"That's fair," she said.

He looked relieved by the answer.

"I've got about forty minutes left," he said. "If you want to sit, you can. I won't be interesting."

"You'll have to endure my disappointment."

"I'm prepared."

She took the chair by the stove and watched him work. It was not an exciting thing to watch. He wrote a line of notes, played them softly on the guitar, crossed out two measures and tried again. Occasionally he looked up to ask whether a transition sounded too abrupt. She gave an opinion. Sometimes he used it. Sometimes he didn't.

At one point he asked if she could hand him a pencil from the table. She did, and the ease of the small exchange pleased her more than it should have. She was no longer being invited to solve the whole problem. She was simply present, available for a little assistance when he wanted it.

When he finished, Rhys set down the pencil and stretched both arms above his head.

"That's the best I can do today."

"It's better than when I came in."

"You're grading again."

"Yes. You're welcome."

He laughed. She stood and crossed to him. She wanted to kiss him, but there was something she needed to ask first.

"Was I making it harder?" she said.

"At first."

"And now?"

"Now I'm glad you came back."

She rested one hand against his cheek and kissed him. He leaned into it, his arms coming around her, and she felt the odd relief of being able to repair a mistake without pretending it had never happened. She hadn't won the afternoon. She had returned to the person she'd hurt, and he had chosen to spend what remained of it with her.

They went for a short walk before supper, taking the flat path to spare her heel. He told her how the song would be used at home, how his mother had promised to borrow a piano if he could make the arrangement simple enough, and how irritated he was by one stubborn measure that still didn't sound right. She listened. She asked questions. At the fork he kissed her, then went back to mail the pages.

She watched him walk away and realized that she had enjoyed hearing about something that did not involve her. It shouldn't have been a revelation. Still, she thought it worth remembering.

---

Monday evening, Rhys cooked for her.

The invitation came in the form of a note folded around a piece of string. He had left it inside the spare quiver she was borrowing, where she found it after the day's instruction ended. His handwriting leaned at an angle, a habit she recognized from the slate by his door.

*Seven, if you want. No arrows. No audiences. And before you ask, I have learned how not to destroy a pot of stew.*

She read it twice, then folded it small enough to hide in her palm. It was a ridiculous thing to find herself charmed by, and she objected to the feeling on principle. Then she went back to Cabin One, changed her shirt, and arrived at seven with the note in her pocket.

The cabin smelled different. Not of the usual wax and old wood, but of onions, bread, and the faint spicy scent of something simmering slowly near the stove. Rhys had pushed the workbench against the wall, cleared the table, and set out two bowls. No flowers, no grand gesture. Just a room made ready for two people.

"You did all this?" she asked.

"I moved a table."

"And cooked."

"That's still under investigation."

She examined the bowls. "If it's terrible, I'm telling everyone."

"I'd expect nothing less."

He pulled out the chair for her, then seemed to remember she disliked unnecessary ceremony. He stopped halfway and looked embarrassed. She sat down anyway.

"You can be polite without making it a performance," she told him.

"I'm learning."

"Slowly."

He served the meal. It was better than she expected, which she found mildly infuriating. The stew had actual vegetables and a good broth; the bread was warm; he'd even remembered that she disliked an excess of pepper.

"This is good," she said.

He stared at her. "Say that again."

"Don't press your luck."

"I need witnesses."

"Nobody would believe you."

He laughed, and they ate together. The room had the intimate oddness of a place transformed by a single deliberate effort. A workbench that usually held a half-strung bow was now bare; the table usually covered with scraps of cord had been cleared so that they could look at each other without moving anything out of the way. She kept noticing the care involved, and the noticing unsettled her more than a dozen compliments would have.

"Was this supposed to be romantic?" she asked.

Rhys put down his spoon. "I was hoping it might be."

"You're not going to call it a date?"

"Would you object?"

"Probably."

"Then I'm not going to ruin a perfectly good evening by giving it a title."

She considered. He had a talent for refusing to make her choices for her, sometimes to the point of irritation. But here he had planned something because he wanted her to enjoy it, not because he expected a particular response. That mattered.

"It feels like a date," she said.

He smiled. "Then it can be one."

She took another bite to conceal how pleased she was.

After dinner he cleared the table while she sat on the cot pretending to inspect his guitar. She wanted to help; she also wanted to let him do the work he'd invited her to watch. It was surprisingly difficult to sit still when there was a task someone else could use her for. At last she took the guitar into her lap and played the first four notes of the tune he'd been struggling with on Friday.

Rhys turned from the sink. "That's not right."

"I know."

"You missed the second chord."

"Then come show me."

He wiped his hands and sat beside her. She made him demonstrate the transition twice, deliberately getting it wrong the first time just to see whether he would be patient. He was. On the third attempt she played it correctly and felt an absurd little thrill when he smiled.

"Perfect," he said.

"Careful. I'll think you like me."

"I've been making that fairly obvious."

"Have you?"

"Tragically."

She set the guitar aside and turned to him. It felt different after dinner, perhaps because they had made a small evening together rather than stumbled into one. The anticipation had lasted since she found his note. Now she was here, and she wanted the simplest part of the evening: his hands and his mouth, the way he looked at her when she stopped talking long enough to let him see what she meant.

"Come here," she said.

He did, and she kissed him.

There were no jokes for a while. She drew him close, one hand at his collar and the other resting against his chest, feeling the quick beat of his heart. He kissed her with an eagerness that made her smile, then slowed when she shifted to get comfortable. The heel no longer hurt much, but it reminded her of itself if she moved carelessly. She arranged the cushion under her leg and pulled him back toward her.

"Better?" he asked.

"Much."

"Good."

She kissed him again, partly because she wanted to and partly because it had begun to please her to see how readily he responded. His hand moved to the back of her neck, holding her close without trapping her there. She leaned into the touch and let the room fall quiet around them.

Later they sat beneath the blanket with the lamp turned low. She had expected the evening to be easy, and in some ways it was. But she was becoming aware of a question that had been gathering since their first night together, one she had avoided because the answer might change how she understood her own desires.

"Does it bother you when I leave?" she asked.

He considered. "Sometimes I wish you'd stay."

"That's not quite the same thing."

"No. It doesn't bother me that you have somewhere else to be."

She looked at the folded note on the table. "I like staying. I also like waking up in my own cabin."

"Then do both."

"You make everything sound simple."

"Only the parts that actually are."

She rested her head against his shoulder. "I don't want you thinking that because I spend the night here once, I owe you another."

"I don't."

"Or that because I want to be close now, I'll want exactly the same thing tomorrow."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "I want to know you. That includes days when you're tired or angry or not in the mood to be kissed. I don't want a version of you who exists only when we're alone."

She stared at the lamp. The answer landed more deeply than she expected. She had been measuring herself against an imagined standard of how an adult relationship ought to work, then resenting the standard for existing. Rhys had never imposed it.

"I can be unbearable," she warned him.

"I've noticed."

"You aren't taking this seriously."

"I am. I'm also not going to pretend I haven't already seen you at your worst."

"You haven't."

"Then we'll deal with it when I do."

The phrase was ordinary, but it contained no guarantee that every difficulty would be forgiven. He was offering to meet her as she was, with his own right to be upset, and to see what happened next. She liked that better than a promise of perfection.

She kissed his cheek. He turned toward her, and the next kiss was slower, less urgent, full of the strange comfort of being close without having to negotiate an entire future first.

At nine she stood and found her boots. Rhys helped gather her scarf, then walked her to the door. Outside, the frost had returned to the path, and the lanterns showed a long curve of light down the hill.

"Good dinner," she said.

"That's an official verdict?"

"Don't get greedy."

"Can I cook for you again?"

She thought about the warm bread, the cleared table, the note hidden in her pocket, and the way he'd watched her discover the meal.

"Yes," she said. "But next time I'm bringing something."

He nodded. "Deal."

She kissed him on the porch. It was a quick kiss, and she could have made it longer, but she liked leaving with the evening intact. There would be other nights. That was a hope, not a promise, and it seemed sturdy enough.

## Tuesday, October 31: The Things She Kept

Thalia discovered she had left her gloves in Rhys's cabin when she reached for them at the bottom of the hill.

She stood in the cold looking at her empty hands. She knew precisely where she'd put them: on the corner of his table during dinner, beside the folded note he'd written her. She had been so occupied with behaving as if a date were no great event that she'd forgotten an object she used every day.

There was nothing remarkable about forgetting gloves. She had forgotten worse things. Once she'd left an entire quiver beside a stream and returned for it in the dark, following her own footprints with a torch and a selection of curses. But now, as she turned back toward the north path, she suspected Rhys would see the gloves and decide she'd manufactured another reason to visit.

He would be wrong. Probably.

The cabin window was lit. She knocked and heard something scrape across the floor.

"One minute," Rhys called.

She waited. Beyond the porch, the north wind bent the grass beneath the fence. A minute passed. Then another. When he opened the door, he was wearing an old shirt with one sleeve rolled higher than the other and had flour on his wrist.

"You left your gloves."

"I know."

"I was going to bring them down tomorrow."

"I prefer having fingers tonight."

He stepped back and let her inside. "An understandable preference."

The gloves were on the table, folded one atop the other. She picked them up. Beside them lay the note he had written her and an open envelope addressed to his mother. The letter had been started but not finished. A line crossed the bottom of the page, as though he'd changed his mind in the middle of something difficult.

Rhys followed her gaze and turned the paper face down. He wasn't furtive about it, but the movement was definite.

"I didn't read it," Thalia said.

"I know."

"You looked as if you thought I would."

"No. I just hadn't finished."

She put on her gloves. There was no reason to remain; she'd come for one thing, found it and could go home. Yet the smell of bread in the cabin and the sight of him standing there, half ready for bed and half occupied with a private worry, made her reluctant to leave. He looked as though he'd been interrupted at an important moment. She disliked the thought of being the interruption, and she disliked equally the possibility that he might be pleased she was there.

"Your mother?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Anything wrong?"

"Nothing new."

He said it without meeting her eyes. That wasn't an invitation to interrogate him. She knew the difference, though she didn't always act on it.

"All right," she said.

He glanced up, surprised perhaps that she was willing to leave the question unanswered.

"Are you heading back?"

"Unless your stove has some objection."

"It has several, but none relevant."

She moved toward the door. When she reached for the latch, he spoke again.

"Actually, Thalia?"

She turned.

"Would you sit with me for a little while?"

It was the first time he had asked her for company without wrapping the request in a joke or a practical reason. He looked uncomfortable with the admission. The sight moved her more than she would have guessed.

She took off her gloves and put them down.

"Make me tea," she said.

"Terribly gracious."

"I am widely admired for it."

He prepared the kettle while she sat by the stove. She had brought no book, no equipment to repair, nothing with which to disguise the fact that she had chosen to remain. The cabin seemed larger than it had at dinner, its workbench covered with half-completed projects that would be waiting in the morning. When he handed her a cup, his fingers brushed hers. He sat on the cot rather than the chair opposite, leaving a space beside him.

She took it.

The first few minutes passed without conversation. Thalia listened to the rain beginning against the far window. Rhys leaned forward with his elbows on his knees, staring into his tea.

"She asked whether I could come before winter gets bad," he said at last.

"Your mother?"

"Yes. The back door's been sticking, and there are bills she doesn't understand. She says it's nothing, but she always says that."

"You could go."

"I know."

"That wasn't an instruction."

"I know that too."

Thalia studied his profile. He looked older when he worried, the familiar confidence from the range drawn tight around his mouth. She had been pleased by how independent he was, by the fact that he could like her without asking her to reorganize her life around him. Now she was seeing the other side of that independence. He had responsibilities she couldn't solve by being competent.

"Are you frightened she'll need more than you can give?" she asked.

Rhys looked at her.

"Yes," he said.

It took her a moment to recognize the answer for what it was. He hadn't softened it. He hadn't made a joke. He'd accepted her question and answered as a person who trusted her enough not to disguise an ordinary fear.

"I know something about that," she said.

"I thought you might."

She put down her cup. "Does she know you're thinking of going?"

"She thinks I'm already coming. I haven't corrected her."

"Then you'd better answer the letter."

"I will."

He didn't move.

Thalia looked at his hands, at the small scrape near his thumb, the ink on one finger from his unfinished draft. She had watched these hands work a bowstring and turn a piece of wood into something useful. Tonight they lay motionless around a cup.

She touched his wrist.

"Give me the paper," she said.

He raised an eyebrow. "You want to write my mother a letter?"

"No. I want you to write it while I sit here and make rude comments about your handwriting."

He smiled, reluctantly.

"That might work."

"Of course it will."

He fetched the letter and a pencil. Thalia stayed where she was while he crossed out another line and began again. He was slow at first. Then the words came more easily. He told his mother that he was worried about her, that he wanted to help, and that he would need a little time to arrange the lessons before he could leave. He wrote that he loved her without adding an excuse or changing the sentence into something about roof repairs.

Thalia saw the line. She looked away before he could notice.

When he finished, he folded the sheet and set it in the envelope. He seemed both relieved and annoyed, as though she'd persuaded him into doing something he ought to have done days ago.

"There," she said.

"An astonishing amount of work for one page."

"You should have started with the truth."

He gave her a sidelong look. "You'd know."

She accepted the hit. "Fair."

They were quiet. Then Rhys reached across the small space between them and took her hand.

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I barely did anything."

"That's what I needed."

The words remained between them. Thalia looked down at their joined hands and felt a familiar desire rise beneath the tenderness. She liked being wanted when she had something useful to offer. She was beginning to like being wanted when she was merely present, too. She leaned toward him, and he met her halfway.

Their kiss was slow, a deliberate turning toward each other rather than an escape from the conversation. His hand moved into her hair, and she held him there, pleased by the immediate warmth of his response. When she drew back, the worry had not disappeared from his face, but the strain around his mouth had eased.

"You've got flour everywhere," she said.

"I was making bread."

"On your wrist?"

"I'm innovative."

She caught his hand and brushed the flour from his skin with her thumb. He watched her without speaking.

"You know," she said, "I wasn't going to stay."

"I suspected."

"I came for my gloves."

"I know."

"And then you looked miserable."

"Very flattering."

She smiled. "I'm extraordinarily generous with my time."

He kissed her again. This time she laughed into it, not because anything was funny but because it was pleasant to be here with him, in a room that contained worry and desire and unfinished work and still felt welcoming.

Outside, the rain grew heavier. The gloves lay forgotten again on the table. Thalia noticed them when Rhys stood to turn down the stove and decided, without argument, that she would remain a little longer.

***

An hour later he found her reading the half-finished music on the workbench.

She had taken the page without asking, an old habit of handling objects that interested her as though they had been left out for public inspection. The tune was recognizable now. She could follow the first four measures and the difficult transition he had been fighting with on Friday. But a second voice ran below it, and the lower line didn't seem complete.

"What's this part?" she asked.

Rhys came over. "A harmony. I'm trying to make the melody work with the piano my mother can borrow."

"There's a gap."

"I know."

"Not a small gap. A whole measure."

"Thank you for the precision."

She showed him where the second line dropped out. "You could leave a rest and have the first voice hold the note."

"I tried that."

"And?"

"It sounds as if somebody forgot what came next."

"Maybe that's the point."

He looked at her. "You want me to write a mistake into it?"

"I want you to stop pretending a pause is always a failure."

He was silent long enough that she looked up from the page.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. That's useful."

"Naturally."

He picked up the guitar and tried the passage. The lower line fell away, leaving a small space before the melody returned. It was imperfect. It also made the return more satisfying, as if the music had been allowed to breathe.

"There," Thalia said.

Rhys played it again.

She watched him, pleased by the movement of his hands and the way the concentration changed his face. He was not performing for her. He was working something out, letting her watch. She remembered the first time she'd seen him at the target shed, how attractive she'd found his quiet attention to a task he cared about. That hadn't changed. It had acquired meaning.

When he looked up, she was smiling.

"Do you want to try it?" he asked.

"No. This one's yours."

"You're allowed to help."

"I just did."

He laughed and set down the guitar.

It was past ten. The rain had stopped, leaving a bright wet silence beyond the shutter. Thalia stood, found her gloves and pulled her coat over her shoulders. He came to help with the collar, and she caught his hand before he could retreat.

"I want to kiss you," she said, because it had become easier to be plain about some things.

He smiled as he moved closer. "Then stop announcing it."

She did.

The kiss lingered until she felt herself beginning to forget the time. She had no particular objection to forgetting. But the room at Cabin One would be cold by now, and Annabeth had a habit of waking early when she was worried about a drawing. Thalia had promised to help her check the east wall in the morning. She wanted to be there.

She stepped back and straightened his collar where she'd wrinkled it.

"Good night," she said.

"Good night."

At the door she glanced once more toward the letter on the workbench.

"Send it tomorrow."

"I will."

"And don't decide to protect me from the answer."

Rhys looked at her. He nodded, serious now.

"All right."

She went down the steps. The path shone under the lanterns, and she had her gloves this time. Halfway down the hill she caught herself humming the unfinished melody. There was something she liked about a tune that could make room for silence without losing its shape.

## Wednesday, November 1: A Room with Her Name in It

Thalia had never wanted a room of her own as badly as she did after she began spending evenings in someone else's.

Cabin One was home. It contained her bed, her books, her weapons and a collection of small things Annabeth insisted were important enough to preserve. It had a view of the upper meadow and a door that stuck whenever the weather turned wet. Thalia knew exactly where each board complained and which window latch needed lifting before it would close.

It was also a place where people came looking for her.

By eight that morning, two campers had knocked to ask about the winter archery list, an instructor had left a message about the targets, and Annabeth had demanded that she look at the east wall before the next freeze. Thalia had answered all three questions and then stood in the middle of the room trying to remember what she'd intended to do when she woke.

The photograph from the orchard was inside her book. She took it out and looked at it again.

Rhys was laughing. She was turning toward him, half off balance, with her hand caught in the front of his coat. Neither faced the camera. It wasn't a particularly flattering image of either of them. It was also the only photograph she owned in which she could remember exactly what she'd been feeling when it was taken.

She liked being able to remember that.

She put it on the little shelf beside her bed. Then she stepped back.

Too visible. Annabeth would notice it the moment she came in. Thalia took it down and slid it into the book again.

A minute later she was angry with herself.

She wasn't ashamed of him. She wasn't ashamed of wanting him, or of having spent the night in his cabin. But she didn't want the relationship displayed as something to be examined every time somebody entered her room. She had spent enough of her life answering questions about what sort of daughter Zeus had produced. She could already imagine the comments about the son of Apollo she'd been seen walking with, about whether the great Thalia Grace was turning sentimental.

She wanted people to know only what she chose to tell them. She also wanted the photograph where she could see it.

The contradiction was inconvenient and remained unsolved by moving the object in and out of a book.

Annabeth appeared in the doorway carrying a rolled plan.

"Why are you staring at your shelf?"

"I'm making a decision."

"About whether to dust it?"

"About whether you're becoming unbearably nosy."

"That one's been settled."

Thalia sat on her bed. Her sister put the plan on the table and began sorting through measurements. She had brought fresh pencil marks and a diagram of the low wall near the east path. For twenty minutes they argued about drainage with the affection of two people who had been having the same argument in different forms for most of their lives.

Then Annabeth noticed the book.

"That's the one you've been carrying everywhere."

"It's a book."

"You haven't turned a page."

"Perhaps it's difficult."

"Perhaps there's something in it."

Thalia gave her a level look. Annabeth's mouth twitched.

"I wasn't going to ask," she said.

"You were doing an excellent impersonation of asking."

Annabeth slid the plan across the table.

"I've been trying to work out what happened to you."

Thalia stiffened before she could help it. "Nothing happened to me."

"That's what you keep saying."

"Because nothing happened that requires an investigation."

"Then why do you keep expecting one?"

The question landed cleanly enough that Thalia was silent. Her sister looked back at the plan instead of pressing, which made the silence worse.

Thalia turned the book over in her hands. "I've been seeing Rhys."

"I know."

"I know you know."

"Good."

"And I don't want you asking about everything."

Annabeth nodded. "I wasn't planning to ask about everything."

"What's 'everything'?"

Her sister gave her the look she used when a mathematical problem was making itself deliberately difficult.

"The things that are yours."

Thalia sat still.

That was not an answer she had expected from Annabeth. Part of her had prepared to protect the younger girl from details she had no business knowing; another part had prepared to defend herself against teasing. The offer of privacy made both preparations unnecessary.

"Thank you," she said, a little awkwardly.

Annabeth looked up. "You can still tell me when something matters."

"I know."

"Good."

They returned to the east wall. The conversation was easier afterward, not because every unspoken thing had been settled but because neither sister had tried to claim the whole of the other's life.

At noon Thalia moved the photograph onto the shelf.

It stayed there.

***

That evening she went to the range after the last class had finished. Rhys was there alone, rolling the target covers with the unfriendly precision of a man who had discovered that a thing designed to be folded refused to be folded.

"You've got the corners wrong," she said.

"I knew you'd come."

"Then you ought to have prepared something less embarrassing."

He looked at her. "How was the east wall?"

"Still standing."

"Good."

She helped him fold the cover, mostly by standing on one edge while he pulled the canvas tight. The fabric settled into a neat rectangle on the third try. Rhys strapped it shut and carried it into the shed. Thalia followed, thinking about the photograph on her shelf and the fact that Annabeth would see it when she returned to the cabin.

Rhys set the bundle down.

"What?" he asked.

"Nothing."

"That's been your answer all week."

"Then you're not listening hard enough."

He leaned against the doorframe. "I'm listening."

Thalia studied him. He had dark circles beneath his eyes. The letter to his mother had gone out that morning, and he had spent the afternoon teaching. She wanted to pull him close, not because he looked particularly inviting in an old work shirt covered with bits of straw, but because he was standing there waiting for her to say something and she liked the sight.

She took his hand and drew him nearer.

He smiled before she kissed him.

The kiss was brief. A second followed, slower, and when she drew back she found that he'd rested a hand at her waist as though the movement had become familiar. She liked that it had. She liked the way his thumb moved once against her coat without asking for an entire conversation about what the gesture meant.

"I put our picture on my shelf," she said.

He blinked. "The one where you shoved me?"

"The one where you nearly fell over because you're a terrible photographer."

"That one."

"Annabeth will see it."

Rhys looked at her. "And?"

"And nothing. I wanted it there."

His expression softened, then brightened in a way she was beginning to recognize. He didn't ask whether she wanted to tell everyone, or whether she had stopped wanting privacy. He understood that she'd chosen a photograph on a shelf, not issued a public proclamation.

"I'm glad," he said.

She glanced toward the range. No one was there.

"Come with me," she said.

"Where?"

"The low path."

He fetched his coat.

They walked without an errand. The sun had gone below the trees, and the path was mostly dark except where a few lanterns had been lit early. Thalia told him what Annabeth had said, leaving out enough to keep the conversation between sisters their own. Rhys listened, occasionally asking a question that showed he understood where the difficult part lay.

"She's growing up," he said.

"I've noticed."

"That can't be easy."

"She's been doing it for fourteen years. It should be less surprising by now."

He laughed. Thalia looked at him sideways.

"Don't start analyzing me."

"I wasn't."

"You're thinking about it."

"I'm thinking about the fact that you keep calling her a child and then describing arguments in which she wins."

"She does not win."

"Of course not."

Thalia stopped beside the low stone wall.

"You're insufferable."

"You came looking for me."

"Repeatedly. An error in judgment."

He came closer. She caught the front of his coat, smiling, and kissed him before he could find another answer.

For a while they stood together in the evening chill. His hands were warm, and the winter air was cold enough that she could feel the contrast at the edge of her scarf. The path belonged to the camp, but there was no one close enough to interrupt. She liked the private moment made inside an ordinary place.

When she stepped back, Rhys's hair had been blown across his forehead. She brushed it aside, a gesture she had done before in the cabin but never on the path.

He smiled. "What was that for?"

"It was in your eyes."

"Terrible hazard."

"I'm responsible for public safety."

He laughed, then caught her hand and kissed her knuckles. She pulled a face at the absurd gallantry, but she didn't withdraw her hand.

At the fork she told him she was going home.

"Not coming by?"

"Not tonight."

He nodded and squeezed her fingers once before letting go.

She looked at him. "You're allowed to be disappointed."

"I am."

"Are you?"

"A little. I had hoped you'd stay."

The admission made her happier than it should have. She reached up and kissed him again.

"Tomorrow," she said. "If you're free."

"I'll be here."

She turned uphill. The path to Cabin One was familiar, and the door would probably still be sticking. Annabeth would be bent over the east-wall plan when she arrived. The photograph would be on the shelf, visible to anyone who entered. Nothing about those facts needed to be defended.

For once, being wanted and going home felt like parts of the same life.

## Thursday, November 2: The Hour Without an Excuse

Thalia brought supper in a paper bag. That was how she justified arriving at Rhys's door with a clean shirt beneath her coat and no intention of returning to Cabin One before morning.

She'd spent a full twenty minutes choosing the shirt, then hated herself for making a ceremony out of something so small. She liked him. He liked her. They had survived several evenings together and one particularly stupid argument about whether he ought to stop working the moment she appeared. She could walk into his cabin without inventing a reason now.

The food was a concession to old habits. If she arrived carrying something useful, she could pretend for the first five minutes that usefulness was what brought her.

Rhys opened the door and looked first at the bag, then at her face.

"Should I be worried?"

"Only if you're allergic to bread."

"I've managed so far."

"There's cheese, too. Clarisse had extra."

"Did she give it to you?"

"She put it on the table and turned around. That's nearly the same thing."

He stepped aside. There was a small fire in the stove and a lamp on the workbench. He had cleared enough space for the two of them to eat, but his repaired glove still lay on the cot and a coil of practice string hung from one of the bedposts. It was unmistakably his room, neither transformed for her benefit nor neglected because she might come.

She liked that better than she expected.

When she set the bag down, he took off her coat. He had been doing that lately, and she had stopped pretending she resented it. His fingers brushed the back of her neck as he lifted the collar away. She felt the touch for longer than it lasted.

He noticed.

"Cold?"

"Not anymore."

His hand remained near her shoulder. He was close enough that she could see a pale scrape beneath his jaw, probably left by a hurried shave. She wanted to touch it. Instead she turned to the bread.

"You'd better eat. I risked my reputation to bring that."

"What reputation?"

"Be careful, Calder."

He caught her hand before she could open the bag. It wasn't a dramatic gesture. He simply kept her there for a moment, looking at her as though he'd been considering something since she arrived.

"You can put the food down."

"I have put it down."

"Then leave it alone."

She looked at their hands.

"Oh," she said.

"Yes."

He was smiling when she turned toward him, and she kissed the smile away. It was not the first time she'd taken the initiative, but tonight she felt the pleasure of doing so with no impulse to disguise it as a dare. He drew her close. The backs of her fingers found the open collar of his shirt. She could feel the warmth he'd carried from the stove, the quick shift in his breathing when she caught his face between her hands.

He kissed her with the same eager certainty, and she laughed under her breath when he backed into the table.

"Careful."

"You're the one moving."

"Then you should keep up."

He turned them away from the table. She went willingly, still smiling, until he kissed the corner of her mouth and her answer disappeared. There was no advantage to be gained by pretending she wasn't affected. She was. He knew it, and the knowledge made him bolder without making him careless.

Her hands slid up to his shoulders. He drew her closer, pausing only when he felt her adjust the position of her injured foot. She shifted to the steadier leg and caught the front of his shirt again. The change took a second. Neither made it into a discussion.

The lamp was still bright. She noticed the look on his face and realized she didn't want him to turn it down yet.

"What?" he murmured.

"Nothing."

"Convincing."

She touched the scrape at his jaw. "You cut yourself."

"A terrible wound."

"Disgraceful for a son of Apollo."

"You're welcome to file a report."

She brushed her lips against the corner of his jaw, close enough to feel him stop talking. That pleased her enormously. She had spent weeks learning how to interrupt his lectures on arrow weight; this was a much better method.

"Where's the report form?" she whispered.

He laughed, then kissed her again. The laugh faded into something quieter as she kept him near. His hand found the curve of her back beneath the coat she had abandoned. She could feel the difference between the practiced touch of an instructor steadying a student and this, a hand that lingered because he wanted her close and knew she wanted the same.

The desire between them had stopped feeling like a question they had to answer. It had become a familiar fact, one that still caught her off guard with its intensity. She could be irritated with him in the morning, amused by him at supper, and completely distracted by the way he looked at her now. None of those versions of herself canceled the others.

He rested his forehead against hers.

"The bread is going to get cold."

"It already is."

"Then the cheese."

"Cheese is supposed to be cold."

"You've thought of everything."

"Almost."

She loosened the button at his collar with fingers that were steadier than she'd expected. It was such a small movement that she wondered, briefly, why it made her feel so exposed. Perhaps because she had chosen it openly. She wasn't hiding behind a joke or waiting for him to guess what she wanted.

Rhys looked down at her hands, then back at her face. She recognized his smile, that particular private smile he never wore on the shooting line. She kissed him again before he could say anything sentimental.

His answering enthusiasm nearly sent the two of them into the workbench. She caught herself against his shoulder and began laughing so hard she couldn't continue.

"That," she said, "was dreadful."

"You attacked me."

"I kissed you."

"With alarming force."

"You're a very weak person."

"Completely defenseless."

He caught her around the waist and steadied her. Their laughter took its time fading. That was one of the things she liked most, though she would never have said it aloud: a moment could become awkward or ridiculous without being spoiled. He didn't need to maintain some impossible image of confidence, and she didn't need to pretend she was always in control.

She took his hand and led him away from the workbench.

The cot creaked when she sat. She glanced at it with disgust.

"Have you considered buying furniture that doesn't complain?"

"It has survived years of Apollo campers."

"That isn't a recommendation."

He sat beside her. She turned toward him and kissed him, this time with no table or bench left between them. He met her eagerly. The room seemed unusually quiet, though the fire was still alive and wind rattled the shutter outside. She slipped her fingers beneath the edge of his collar, not trying to hurry him, simply enjoying that she could touch him without turning the touch into a problem to be solved.

He kissed her cheek and lingered there. She tilted her face toward him. When he moved to her neck, she caught a breath and closed her eyes. The ordinary sounds of the cabin continued around them, the stove ticking, the wind moving through the trees. She was aware of each one because she wasn't being pulled out of the world. She was here, choosing what happened next, and entirely glad of it.

"Thalia," he said quietly.

She opened her eyes. He was watching her with an expression that held no uncertainty about his own desire, only a kind of astonishment that she had chosen to share it with him.

She smiled and drew him nearer.

He kissed her again. The lamp threw a bright strip across the blanket; beyond it, the room fell into warm shadow. At some point her clean shirt slipped from the pocket of her coat and landed beside the bed, unnoticed. They had stopped being interested in their carefully arranged plans.

When she reached over to turn down the lamp, he caught her hand long enough to kiss her palm. She laughed softly, touched his face once more, and lowered the wick.

The rest of the evening was private.

***

Later the food was still on the table, and Thalia had acquired Rhys's spare blanket because she had decided his original one was insufficient. He was sitting on the edge of the cot trying to untangle the shirt he'd dropped behind it. She was watching him with the comfortable malice of someone who had no intention of helping.

"Is it stuck?"

"No."

"It looks stuck."

"It's caught on the bedframe."

"A structural problem. Have you informed Annabeth?"

"Please don't."

"She'd draw a plan."

"She'd tell me to move the bed."

"She's usually right."

Rhys freed the shirt and looked at her. His hair had been flattened on one side and was standing up on the other. It would be unforgivable to let him see how much she liked him like this, so she gave him her most disapproving expression.

"You look terrible."

"You're welcome."

He climbed back beside her, and she stopped pretending she didn't want him there. The blanket covered both of them. She rested against his shoulder, feeling the warmth of his skin and the lingering sense of closeness that had nothing to do with making conversation.

She wasn't embarrassed. That surprised her. There had been moments earlier when she wondered whether she'd be embarrassed afterward, when the intensity settled and they had to look at one another as ordinary people again. Instead she was preoccupied with the fact that she liked the ordinary person just as much.

"You know what I'm thinking?" she asked.

"That my bed is a disgrace?"

"Besides that."

"That the bread is cold?"

"Everything is cold. Your powers of deduction are extraordinary."

He turned toward her. "Then tell me."

Thalia considered making the thought into a joke. She had already been bolder tonight than on the first Monday; she could be honest about this too.

"I don't feel as though I've changed into somebody else."

Rhys's expression became serious. "Was that what you were worried about?"

"A little."

"Why?"

She thought of how she had been taught to defend herself, to notice the position of every person in a room, to keep her own needs out of the way until she had taken care of everyone else's. There had been years when giving someone the power to make her happy would have felt reckless.

"Because I like it here," she said. "I like you. And I didn't know what that would make me."

He studied her a moment.

"Still the person who stole my last piece of bread yesterday."

"It was unattended."

"Still the person who corrects my stance when I'm trying to have a quiet morning."

"Your stance needs work."

"Still the person who gets angry when somebody tells her she's tired."

"Don't start."

His smile returned. "And still the person I wanted to see tonight."

She looked at him. No speech about transformation or destiny, no claim that intimacy had repaired anything. Just a man who knew her well enough to list a few of her worst habits and looked pleased that she'd come anyway.

She kissed him, softly, because she wanted to.

He drew her closer, and she settled against him again. A few minutes passed without either speaking.

"Your mother," she said eventually.

"Yes?"

"Will she mind you being away?"

He considered. "She'll be glad when I arrive. She'll complain if I interfere with her kitchen. And she'll tell me to come back here once we've sorted everything out."

"You sound certain."

"I'm certain about her complaining."

Thalia smiled. Then the smile faded. "I don't want to be another thing you have to juggle."

"You're not."

"You say that now."

"I said it yesterday and last week too."

"That's a very long record."

He touched the back of her hand. "I like having you in my life. It's not an account I have to balance."

She looked at him. He wasn't promising to make her the center of every decision. That was why she could believe him.

"All right," she said.

The words were enough. He rested his cheek against her hair and she let herself stay there. The room would still be cold when she got up. The range would still need instructors, and Annabeth would be drawing plans that somebody had to inspect. None of it needed to be settled tonight.

Rhys reached for the bread bag. It had been sitting on the table for hours.

"Are we going to eat?"

Thalia looked at the bag. "We probably should."

He opened it, examined the bread and broke off a piece. She took half from his hand. The cheese was still good, and the entire meal tasted better for having been forgotten. They ate sitting beneath the blanket, dropping crumbs despite their best efforts. Rhys got one caught on his collar and Thalia picked it off.

He gave her a look.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Don't give me the face."

"Which face?"

"The one where you're going to tell me you've noticed something sentimental."

He grinned. "I was thinking I could get used to this."

She considered the possibility. It was tempting to make a joke about his terrible bed or the fact that he had nearly knocked over the table again. Instead she rested her head on his shoulder.

"So could I," she said.

He didn't ask her to repeat it. She was grateful.

Outside, the wind had turned. The cabin held its warmth. Later, when they washed the cups and put out the lamp, Thalia left her clean shirt in the small drawer beneath the cot rather than taking it home. She hadn't planned that when she packed it. She liked the possibility of coming back and finding a small piece of her life already waiting.

She didn't tell Rhys what she'd done until the following morning, when he discovered the folded shirt and called after her from the doorway.

"Grace!"

She turned at the bottom of the steps.

"Leaving things behind on purpose now?"

She looked at his hand on the drawer, the grin he'd failed to hide, and felt the pleasant thrill of being known.

"Maybe," she said.

Then she walked away before he could see how much it pleased her.

## Friday, November 3: After the Hall Closed

The pool was empty when Thalia arrived.

This was a rare condition. During the day, the Tempest Hall belonged to children splashing at the shallow end, instructors counting heads, Percy arguing about whether a game was officially a swimming lesson, and Clarisse trying to make rules stay in the places she'd written them. At night there were usually two adults left to check the drains and put away the equipment. Tonight there was only Rhys, standing ankle-deep at the first step with his sleeves rolled and a measuring pole in one hand.

Thalia stopped at the threshold.

"Are you conducting an experiment?"

"Checking whether the winter cover fits."

"While standing in the water?"

"The cover wouldn't fit on dry land."

She looked at the heavy canvas spread along the near edge of the pool. Three cords crossed its width. Someone had written numbers along the hem, and one corner had been marked in bright chalk. It was a practical job. She had come here partly because she knew he would still be working, and partly because it amused her to find him trying to finish one more task before he was willing to leave.

"You know," she said, "the pool is still going to be here in the morning."

"An unproven assumption."

"Calder."

He looked up and smiled. "I have to check the far edge, then I'm done. Want to help?"

Thalia considered pretending she had somewhere better to be. Instead she took off her coat, left her boots beside the bench and rolled up the cuffs of her trousers. The water was cold enough to make her gasp when she stepped onto the first tile.

"Gods."

"I warned you."

"You said you needed help. That was not a warning."

"I thought you knew about water."

"Percy knows about water. I know about weather."

"A useful distinction."

He handed her the free cord. They worked their way along the shallow steps, pulling the cover into place and measuring how much slack remained at each corner. It wasn't difficult work, but it required both of them to move together. If Rhys pulled too soon, Thalia had to adjust her grip; if she tightened the wrong line, the canvas wrinkled on the far side. After a few minutes she began anticipating his movements, and the work found a rhythm that felt strangely familiar.

She thought of archery, of two people teaching from opposite ends of a range without having to watch each other constantly. It was the kind of competence that made her like him.

"Stop pulling," she said.

"I've stopped."

"You're still moving."

"I'm breathing."

"Do it less."

He laughed. The canvas slipped from her hands, and a wave struck the edge of her trousers. She glared at him.

"That was your fault."

"Clearly."

"Your amusement is expensive."

"I'll put it on the winter budget."

She pulled the cord back into position. He held the other end steady, and the cover settled neatly across the water. By the time they were finished, her toes were numb and his trousers were wet to the knee.

"Excellent," she said. "You've invented a new way to make me miserable."

"I had help."

She stepped out of the pool, sat on the bench and reached for her boots. Her socks were damp. She looked at them with such disgust that Rhys stopped toweling off and laughed.

"Don't," she warned him.

"I haven't said anything."

"Your expression is commentary."

He took a dry towel from the shelf and put it beside her. She accepted it without a fight. She was learning that care didn't have to arrive dressed as an insult, though she reserved the right to insult the person delivering it.

The room was quiet now. Without the ordinary crowd the Tempest Hall seemed larger, all hard surfaces and pale reflected light. The pool lay still beneath the fitted canvas. Somewhere in the back corridor water dripped into a drain at regular intervals.

Thalia dried her feet and put on her boots. Rhys sat on the next bench, leaving a polite distance between them. It was ridiculous, considering how much of the week they had spent together, but the public setting had a way of restoring old habits even when nobody else was present.

She looked at him. His hair was damp where he'd splashed water over his face, and the open collar of his work shirt had darkened at the edge. He was trying to wring out a towel with one hand.

"Come here," she said.

He looked up.

"Why?"

"Because you're making a terrible job of that."

He glanced at the towel. "I thought it was doing well."

"Utterly incompetent."

He moved closer. She took the towel from him, folded it and put it down without making any real attempt to improve it. He watched the performance with growing amusement.

"You've done nothing."

"I've made the situation more dignified."

"Of course."

She caught the front of his shirt and kissed him.

He responded at once, surprised enough to laugh softly before the kiss became serious. She had been thinking about it since she arrived, but there was something especially satisfying about doing it here, in a room where they usually had to act as instructors rather than people who wanted to be close. There was no audience tonight. The water behind them was still, the lanterns were low, and nobody was waiting for a demonstration.

She kissed him again. His fingers moved to the back of her neck, warm against her cool skin. She liked the contrast. She liked the quick, unmistakable pleasure in his expression when she leaned into him instead of making another joke.

He drew back a little.

"We should go," he said.

"You're suddenly very concerned about time."

"We've closed the hall."

"I noticed."

"And I'd rather kiss you somewhere without a tiled floor waiting to break my back."

She looked at the hard benches, the cold water and the damp towels between them.

"That's a compelling argument."

He grinned. She caught his hand and helped him gather the last of the equipment, then followed him out into the cold.

***

The walk to his cabin felt different after the pool.

They had already spent the evening working together, and the memory of the kiss lingered between them, making the ordinary conversation about cover tension and missing towels sound faintly absurd. Thalia had always been good at keeping her attention on a task. Tonight she found her attention moving back to him, to the warmth of his hand in hers and the prospect of a closed door ahead.

He noticed her looking.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You're wearing the expression."

"Which one?"

"The one that means you've already decided what happens next and you're waiting for me to catch up."

She smiled. "Have I?"

"Have you?"

She drew him to a stop beneath the eighth lantern and kissed him briefly.

"Better?"

"Very informative."

"Then keep walking."

He laughed and obeyed.

At the cabin he set the equipment aside, lit the stove and turned to find Thalia watching him from the middle of the room. She had taken off her coat and loosened her hair from its tie. The damp ends curled slightly against her neck. Her heel was comfortable, the training day finished, and she was entirely tired of making excuses.

"You look pleased with yourself," he said.

"I'm considering whether to be."

He came closer. She could see the question in his face, not a demand for permission but a quiet readiness to meet what she offered. She crossed the remaining distance and kissed him.

He held her close. The kiss deepened, unhurried at first, then less so, and she felt an answering eagerness rise in her that had nothing to do with proving anything. She knew how his hands moved when he drew her toward him, the small hesitation before his smile vanished into another kiss. The familiarity made the desire stronger rather than weaker.

She drew back just long enough to tug the loose collar of his shirt into place.

"You've ruined it," she said.

"I haven't touched it."

"That makes it worse."

He kissed the corner of her mouth. She had a reply ready; it disappeared when he kissed her again. She smiled against him, then caught his hand and led him away from the stove.

The room held the warmth they'd been carrying toward it all evening. She wanted him, and the way he followed her made it unmistakably clear he wanted her too. The lamp went low, the winter coats stayed where they'd fallen, and the rest of the night needed no audience or explanation.

***

Afterward the pool seemed very far away.

Rhys was sitting on the floor against the cot, trying to untie the wet knot in his bootlace. Thalia had stolen the warmest blanket and had no intention of giving it back. There was a small pile of their discarded work clothes near the chair, none arranged in the manner of a person who expected inspection. The room smelled of wood smoke and soap and the faint dampness of towels brought in from the hall.

"You should have dried those before we left," she said.

"The towels?"

"Your boots."

"They were dry."

"They are now."

"That sounds like success."

She shook her head with exaggerated disappointment. He looked up and smiled, the open, unguarded smile she'd begun to recognize as belonging entirely to these moments when nobody else was present.

"What?"

"You're complaining from beneath both blankets."

"I've earned them."

"I'll have to take your word for it."

He stood, gave up on the knot and sat beside her. She moved closer without pretending otherwise. Her body still held the pleasant fatigue of the evening, and she liked the quiet closeness that came after they had stopped trying to make conversation.

"You know what I thought when you came into the hall?" he asked.

"That I was there to rescue your terrible cover?"

"That you looked as though you'd been thinking about me."

Thalia considered lying. There was no reason to.

"I had."

He smiled. "Good."

"Don't make too much of it."

"I wasn't going to."

She looked at him. He had been gone only a few hours earlier, teaching and checking equipment while she was busy with her own work. Yet she'd wanted to find him at the end of the day. That desire was becoming a constant part of her ordinary life, something she could enjoy without treating it as evidence that she'd lost control.

"I like that I can want you and still do my work," she said.

He considered. "I was hoping so."

"And I like that you can tell me when you're busy."

"I do that."

"Sometimes you look apologetic."

"Sometimes I'm disappointed."

"That's allowed."

He laughed softly. She rested against him and listened to the stove.

There was one thing she'd been thinking about since the night he wrote to his mother. She hadn't asked because the question sounded like an attempt to manage his future. Now, with both of them warm and quiet, it seemed less dangerous to say.

"When you go home," she began, "will you miss this?"

He turned toward her. "The cabin?"

She gave him a look.

He smiled. "Yes."

"How much?"

"Enough that I won't pretend I don't."

It was a better answer than any grand declaration. She kissed his shoulder through the blanket and settled beside him again. There would be evenings when he was far away. There would be mornings when she woke in Cabin One and had to find her own reasons to get through the day. The prospect still hurt. But it was possible, she was learning, to want someone without turning absence into a punishment.

He reached for her hand. She took it.

Outside, the wind shook a little snow from the roof. The sound was brief, followed by silence. The cabin held its warmth. They stayed awake longer than either intended, talking about the terrible pool cover, the music he'd promised to finish and the next lesson they would teach together.

Before she slept, Thalia thought of the hall's empty benches and the way he'd looked at her when she asked him to come closer. She had spent years convincing herself she could manage every room alone. The pleasure of choosing to share one was still new enough to surprise her.

## Saturday, November 4: The Light in the Window

On Saturday morning Thalia returned Rhys's scarf.

She had borrowed it on Wednesday, worn it through two cold nights and discovered that it had begun to feel like part of her wardrobe. It was dark blue wool, badly matched to Percy's coat and useful enough that she was reluctant to surrender it. She had washed it the night before, dried it by the stove and folded it carefully before breakfast.

Rhys was in the practice shed when she found him. He was examining the repaired window with the expression of a man who had been told an old enemy had returned to make trouble.

"Still sticks?" she asked.

"Only when it rains."

"That's the entire purpose of a window."

"I'll inform it."

She handed him the scarf. He looked down at it.

"You didn't have to wash it."

"I borrowed it."

"You were keeping it warm."

"I'm capable of returning property."

"Obviously. Your record is excellent."

She narrowed her eyes. He smiled. She realized, with the now-familiar mixture of irritation and pleasure, that he had become very good at making her want to kiss him when she was trying to be cross.

She was about to do exactly that when an instructor came around the corner carrying a stack of targets.

"Rhys, do you have a minute?"

He turned. "Of course."

Thalia moved aside. The two began discussing the afternoon session, an argument about distance markers and whether the younger campers should be allowed to use the larger targets before the winter demonstration. It was a familiar conversation, and Thalia could have joined it. She knew more about the subject than either person was likely to say aloud.

She didn't.

Instead she watched Rhys explain his position, patient and clear, while the other instructor grew increasingly frustrated by the practical constraints. He wasn't being gentle merely because she happened to like him. He was capable of making a difficult decision in public without treating disagreement as an insult. She had noticed that weeks ago, before she understood why his company had become important to her.

When the discussion ended, he looked back at her.

"Sorry."

"Don't apologize for working."

"I thought you came to see me."

"I came to return your property."

"Of course."

She glanced at the folded scarf in his hand. He was still holding it as though it had acquired some private significance by being worn by her.

"Keep it somewhere sensible," she said.

"I was planning to."

"And don't let the other campers use it to wipe grease off a bow."

"A very serious risk."

She moved close enough that he could hear her without the rest of the shed listening.

"I came to see you too."

His smile changed.

She kissed him, briefly and deliberately, then stepped back with her best expression of professional indifference. The door behind him opened at exactly the wrong moment, and she found herself looking directly at a student who had clearly seen the whole thing.

The boy stopped.

Thalia lifted an eyebrow.

"Do you need something?"

He looked from her to Rhys, opened his mouth, closed it again and held up the target book.

"Scores."

"Then give us the scores."

The boy obeyed at once and fled toward the range.

Rhys waited until the door closed before laughing.

"Don't," Thalia said.

"He looked terrified."

"He should have been carrying the book in the first place."

"An important distinction."

She tried not to smile. It didn't work.

***

The afternoon brought a harder question.

They had agreed to spend some time together after the work was finished, but Rhys's lesson ran long. By the time he came to the lower pavilion, Thalia was sitting on the steps beside an empty mug, having already decided twice to leave and once to stay.

He stopped beside her.

"You're waiting."

"Clearly."

"Sorry. A student broke a bowstring and then decided he'd broken the entire bow. We spent half an hour explaining the difference."

"Did he believe you?"

"Eventually."

She stood. Her heel was tired but no longer painful in the way it had been the previous week. The support wrap had become a nuisance she wore because it helped, not because someone had ordered her to prove she was injured.

They walked toward the lake. The light had turned the water a flat grey, and the wind came across it without interruption. Thalia tucked her hands into her coat pockets. Rhys walked beside her, keeping pace without making a remark about the fact that she was moving more slowly than she had before the injury.

There was a question she had been avoiding since the night he wrote to his mother.

"What happens when you go home?" she asked.

He looked over. "I go home."

"Calder."

"I know. I'm being irritating."

"Successfully."

He considered. "I help my mother. I deal with whatever needs dealing with. Then I come back if I can."

"If you can."

"Yes."

She stopped.

He turned toward her. "Thalia, I want to be here. I'm not trying to find a way out."

"I didn't say you were."

"You're looking at me as if you expect me to."

She looked away toward the lake. It was difficult to explain how old the fear was, how quickly it rose whenever someone she wanted near her began discussing a future beyond her sight. She had spent too many years treating departure as an emergency, and she had learned to act before she had time to find out whether the emergency was real.

"I don't want to become someone who measures everything by whether you stay," she said.

"Then don't."

"That's not very helpful."

"Would it be better if I promised I would never leave?"

"No."

"Then I can't solve it with a promise."

She turned toward him. He wasn't being cold. He was refusing to tell her something false merely because the truth was uncomfortable.

"I know," she said.

He reached for her hand. She took it.

"We can make plans," he said. "We can change them. We can tell each other when something matters. But I can't promise the world won't interfere."

She looked at him. "You sound like you've been thinking about this."

"I have."

"Why?"

"Because I like you enough that I want to do it properly."

She was quiet.

A minute passed.

The old part of her wanted an absolute answer, something she could grip like a weapon. The newer part, the part that had learned how to sit beside him in the cabin without counting every minute, understood that a relationship did not become safe by eliminating uncertainty. It became worth choosing because both people kept returning to it honestly.

"All right," she said.

He squeezed her hand.

They walked back along the lake, talking about the winter demonstration and his mother's latest letter. Thalia found herself listening more than speaking. She liked knowing that he had concerns that did not begin and end with her. She disliked the idea of being absent from his life, but that was not the same thing as wanting to own it.

At the fork she stopped and turned toward him.

"I've been thinking about tomorrow."

"Sunday?"

"Yes. The weather's supposed to clear."

He smiled. "A rare event."

"Very."

"Any plans?"

She considered telling him everything: that she wanted to go to his cabin in daylight, that she'd spent several evenings imagining what it would be like to be there when the sun was on the floor instead of the lamp, that she wanted to kiss him without worrying about how early she ought to get home. The thought made her smile.

"I might visit," she said.

"Very mysterious."

"Don't ruin it."

He laughed and kissed her. This time she held him close long enough that the conversation stopped mattering. The wind moved through the trees and made the ends of her hair lift against his cheek. She liked the small, ordinary sensation of the cold world around them while they stood together in the warmth of their coats.

When she drew back, he was smiling.

"See you tomorrow?" he asked.

"Maybe."

She started uphill before he could make another comment.

At Cabin One she found Annabeth organizing a stack of drawings. Her sister looked up, noticed the color in her cheeks and the way she was still smiling, and wisely decided not to say anything.

Thalia took the orchard photograph from her shelf and looked at it one more time before returning it to its place. Tomorrow she would come up the north path in daylight. She wanted to. There was no need to make the choice more complicated than that.
