## Chapter 219: The Current Beneath

The trouble began with a missing red stake, which Annabeth considered an unusually stupid reason to have to leave a warm cabin before breakfast. She had put the stake in herself two days earlier, driving it into the frozen margin above the lake where she could check whether the bank was moving. It was not an elaborate instrument: a length of painted wood with two pencil marks and a notch she had cut with Beckendorf's knife. It was also the only marker on that stretch of shore whose height she knew for certain. The weather had been cold and dry. She had every reason to expect it would still be there.

Percy was trying to persuade the stove to surrender a piece of toast without burning his fingers when she told him. He looked up, holding a knife he had been using as a makeshift hook, and made the grave expression he reserved for information that did not deserve it.

"A kidnapping," he said. "In broad daylight. Have you questioned the gulls?"

"There's no stake. I went out after supper and the place where I set it was empty."

"Have you questioned the gulls who might have been in the area after supper?"

Annabeth pulled her gloves onto the table, one finger at a time. The window behind him had misted at its edges, though Thalia had wedged the stubborn latch into place and the worst of the draft no longer came through. For once the cabin was reasonably comfortable. Thalia had fallen asleep reading the mystery novel with the wooden horse on its cover, and Ianthe had left a paper packet of tea outside the door before dawn. The three of them were still learning where their things belonged. Annabeth kept expecting to find an unfamiliar pencil in her drawer or a coat hung from the wrong peg, and the irritating surprise of it was slowly becoming part of home.

"Percy."

"I'm taking this seriously. I think the first thing we do is notify the coast guard."

"We are the coast guard."

"That seems like a terrible system. No wonder the stake's missing."

She was laughing before she could help it. Percy abandoned the toast, crossed the floor, and kissed her on the cheek, warm from standing by the stove. He smelled faintly of smoke and the rosemary bread Silena had made the night before. His hair had a flattened patch near the crown, proof that he'd slept badly and not bothered to do anything about it. In their other life Annabeth could have told him the exact hour he had started worrying, merely from the way he slept and the jokes he made afterward. She could still make an educated guess, but she had become cautious about turning that knowledge into certainty.

"What's going on?" she asked him. "You looked at the door three times before I said anything."

He reached over her for the butter, then thought better of it when she moved her papers out of his way. "Clarisse and Leandros had words at the equipment shed last night. Nothing spectacular. Nobody threw a sword, which I consider a win."

"That's a remarkably low standard."

"Cabin Three has a proud tradition of low standards." He took a bite of toast, considered the blackened corner, and put the rest back on the plate. "Dad sent a note. He's not visiting yet. Something about the council in Atlantis. Leandros pretended he didn't care, and Clarisse pretended she didn't see him reading the note twice. I spent an hour trying not to make it worse."

Annabeth looked at him over the table. He had been doing that a lot since Leandros arrived, balancing the other boy's longing for Poseidon against Clarisse's resentment and the uneasy gratitude Percy felt toward a family whose shared childhood he couldn't remember. When he spoke about Atlantis, his voice carried a fondness that never quite stopped sounding borrowed. She knew why. She was the only person alive who remembered the other Percy, the one who had grown up with Sally and thought his father was a deadbeat until the gods started arguing over a stolen bolt. This Percy had been raised in Atlantis after Sally died at two, and his memories of those years did not belong to him. Everyone loved the boy he had been there. He had never met that boy himself.

"Did Leandros say what he wanted?" she asked.

"Not to me. Which is fair. I'm a walking reminder that Dad had time for someone else's kid." He shrugged, then looked annoyed with himself for shrugging. "I know that's not how it happened. Still."

She reached across the table for his hand. Percy held it with his thumb resting between her knuckles, as familiar as breathing. There were things he had told her in the darkness of Camp Jupiter, in New Rome, and in the underworld that would never fit into a conversation about a family letter. Some days she thought those memories made them older than everyone around them. Other days she looked at him with butter on his cheek and knew they were still two fourteen-year-olds with more feeling than sense.

"You could have told me," she said.

"I am telling you."

"You know what I mean."

He smiled, and for once it wasn't much of a joke. "I figured you already had enough to worry about. You've been looking at that shoreline map like it's personally offended you."

"It has. The shore is doing something it didn't do last week, and now the one stake I trusted has disappeared."

"Then I'll come help you find it after breakfast."

"You've got work with Clarisse."

"I've got work with Clarisse every day. It follows me home. I could carve its name on a bunk." Percy squeezed her hand and released it. "Come on. If the lake's stealing your equipment, that sounds like a family matter."

He left before she could tell him that he didn't need to rearrange his morning for her. Annabeth finished her tea and gathered the map, feeling the familiar, exasperating tenderness he could provoke by doing something kind and pretending it was a joke. Her boots stood beside Thalia's cane, and Ianthe's neatly folded spare cloak had appeared on the end of the bench. The household was getting crowded. It had also become a place where she didn't have to be alone with everything that troubled her, though accepting help still made her feel as if she had surrendered a little authority.

They met Chiron on the path below the Big House. The morning had barely brightened. Rain from the previous week had left the ground polished and dangerous along the shaded edge, and frost broke under Percy's boots with a sound like thin glass. Chiron had a bundle of papers beneath one arm and a face Annabeth knew well enough to recognize as concerned. He glanced at the roll of map sticking out of her coat and sighed.

"I was looking for you," he said. "Beckendorf tells me the north shore marker has gone missing."

"Someone must have pulled it out."

"Possibly. The bank has changed since yesterday. Eli found a fresh crack near the old mooring stones, and I'm closing that section until we're sure the ground is stable. A younger camper nearly took the lower path before we noticed."

Annabeth felt the irritation drain away. The marker was annoying; the crack was something else. The old north shore didn't slope much, but the soil there was held together by roots and stones that had been laid down years ago. If water had found a channel behind the stones, the flat-looking ground could give beneath someone's weight without warning. She had seen sinkholes collapse under a road once, in another life, and had never forgotten the awful speed with which the solid world could become empty.

"Where's the crack?" she asked. "Above the mooring stones or below them?"

"Above. Beckendorf marked it, but he wants someone to examine the way the water moves around the old landing. I asked Leandros to help. He knows what to look for near a dock, and he has been watching that section of lake."

Percy gave a small nod, accepting it before Annabeth had finished considering the assignment. She was less pleased. Leandros might know boats, but he was capable of turning the simplest conversation about measurements into a contest over who had the right to speak. Two days earlier he'd spent ten minutes insisting that one arrow on her map should carry his name because he'd been the first to suggest it. Annabeth had respected the observation. The demand attached to it had made her want to tear the arrow off the page.

"I can inspect the ground," she said. "If you need somebody to check water movement, that doesn't make him responsible for the structural assessment."

Chiron studied her. "I did not say he was responsible for it. You will examine the ground from the upper path, and Leandros will examine the water from the safe end of the landing. Beckendorf will be there for any decision about equipment. No one steps onto the cracked bank."

It was all entirely reasonable. Annabeth disliked how much she wanted to argue anyway. She could feel Percy watching her, not critically but with the wary affection of someone who knew she was already planning a speech. She closed her mouth before she delivered it. Chiron looked relieved, which annoyed her even more.

"Fine," she said. "I'll get the measurements."

Leandros was waiting beside the boathouse when they reached the lake. He wore an old dark coat and a knitted cap pulled low over his ears, and he had a small waterproof notebook tucked beneath his arm. His mother had packed the notebook when he'd left home; Annabeth had heard him say so once, with an embarrassment he tried to hide behind a complaint about the quality of the paper. He was not waiting on the cracked bank. He stood above the rope line with his boots planted on solid gravel, looking out toward the water.

"If you're here to tell me the current is imaginary, save it," he said when he saw Annabeth.

"What a lovely morning to start an argument."

"You usually manage to make one out of a map."

"That's because you keep trying to make the map a monument to yourself."

Percy stopped between them and stared out at the gray lake as if suddenly fascinated by a duck. Annabeth knew she had been unfair. Leandros's ears had reddened beneath his cap, though he gave her the hard smile he used when he wanted her to know she hadn't won anything.

"Good," he said. "Now that we've covered our usual material, can we look at the shore before someone puts a foot through it?"

It was the right question, and Annabeth resented him a little for asking it first. Beckendorf came around the shed with two measuring rods and a coil of line, and the argument evaporated beneath the sheer necessity of deciding where they could stand. He had marked the crack with three yellow strips of cloth. A dark seam ran just beyond the rope, parallel to the water, no wider than Annabeth's thumb in one place and wider than her hand farther down. The ground beneath the seam had settled unevenly. One of the old mooring stones was tilted toward the lake.

Percy looked at the stone, then at the water. "If you want it moved, I could—"

"No," Annabeth and Leandros said together.

He raised both hands. "I was about to offer moral support."

"Offer it from the upper path," Beckendorf said. "And nobody goes near the stone until we know what's holding it up."

The wind blew cold off the lake. Annabeth knelt on the safe gravel to spread her map beneath a flat piece of wood, anchoring the corners with pebbles. Leandros stepped closer, stopped when he saw where she had laid it, and pointed from above rather than touching the page. He was looking at the old landing, not her drawing. She watched him follow a streak of pale foam winding away from the stones, frowning as though it had said something in a language only he understood.

"That isn't going where it should," he said.

"Where should it go?" Annabeth asked.

"Out along the open side. The wind's from the west, but the surface is curling back toward the bank. Something's feeding water under the landing, or pulling it out. I can't tell from here."

"We had rain upstream," Beckendorf said. "The creek's high."

"Then it may be coming through the old drainage cut." Annabeth followed the line on her map. She'd drawn the cut as a shallow arc behind the landing because that's what it had been when she surveyed it. Now the crack ran almost along that arc. The last storm might have undermined the soil from behind while the surface looked nearly dry. She felt a sudden lift of excitement, the pleasure of a problem turning from a vague worry into something she could test.

Leandros leaned over her shoulder. "That line doesn't belong there," he said.

Annabeth turned her head. "Excuse me?"

"The water doesn't pass straight through the stones. It bends around the far end, then comes back. I've watched it twice. If you draw it like that, someone will think it's safe to stand where the bank is weakest."

He pointed again, more precisely. His finger stopped above the map instead of landing on it. She followed his gesture to the real shore and watched a pale scrap of reed turn in the water, caught by a pull she hadn't noticed. The line on her map was wrong. Not spectacularly wrong, not so wrong that anyone had been put in danger by her drawing, but wrong in exactly the way that would matter if they were deciding where to put the next marker.

She hated being corrected, particularly when the correction was useful.

"All right," she said. "Show me."

Leandros blinked. "What?"

"Show me where you think it bends. Don't stare at me as if I've asked you to surrender your entire family inheritance. If you're right, I need to change the map."

Percy laughed from behind them. "For the record, I think the inheritance is mostly wet."

Leandros didn't respond. He crouched on the other side of the board, careful not to disturb the stones that held the paper, and began tracing a route in the air with one scarred finger. His explanation was neither concise nor especially elegant. He used the wrong word for the drainage cut twice. He insisted on demonstrating the direction with a twig, dropped it, and had to retrieve another. Annabeth started to interrupt, then stopped because the point of his second demonstration was something she hadn't noticed. The current curled beneath the landing and returned toward the bank in a narrow loop. If rainwater had hollowed out the soil, that loop might be taking the loose earth away faster than anyone realized.

"That's why your stake disappeared," he said at last. "If it was set near the notch, the bank could have taken it when the water came up."

Annabeth looked at the absence of red paint where she'd measured the ground two nights ago. She had assumed somebody had pulled the stake out because the alternative was more frightening. She disliked discovering that the alternative was also more plausible. But she could feel the clean satisfaction of having learned something in the place where her annoyance had been.

"Maybe," she said. "I want to verify where the notch was before we blame the lake."

"Naturally." His mouth twisted. "Wouldn't want the water to get away with an unsupported accusation."

Percy made an appreciative noise. Annabeth tried not to smile, failed, and looked down at the map before either boy could notice. She didn't think she had ever heard Leandros make a joke at his own expense before. It wasn't a particularly good joke. That made it worse, somehow. Beckendorf took a pair of stakes from his sack and asked them both to explain where they wanted the temporary boundary. For the first time that morning, Annabeth and Leandros answered without contradicting each other.

---

Leandros had never understood why people called the lake quiet. It made noise all the time: a soft sucking against the stones under the landing, the slap of a wave that had nowhere impressive to go, the faint ticking sound of loose pieces of ice grinding against each other near the reeds. Sea water talked in bigger gestures. At the marina where he'd grown up, a changing current could rattle chains along half a row of boats, and his mother would stick her head out of the office and tell him to get off the dock before she came out and dragged him in herself. Here the lake whispered, and almost everybody mistook whispering for silence.

He stood on the safe side of the rope while Beckendorf carried the new stakes up from the boathouse. Annabeth had gone to the Big House for an older map that showed the drainage work, and Percy had offered to help make the temporary boundary visible from the main path. That left Leandros with Clarisse, which was how most mornings ended when he thought they'd started well. She had been inspecting one of the ropes with the irritated concentration of someone who expected it to disappoint her.

"Your knot's slipping," she said.

"It's not my knot."

"It's your knot from yesterday. I watched you tie it."

"Then you're calling it a knot, which means it's done its job."

Clarisse pulled the loose end and the line sagged between her hands. "It means it's a bad knot."

He wanted to tell her the cord was cheap and the post slick with frost and nobody had mentioned it was supposed to hold a warning boundary in a crosswind. All of those things were true. They were also the excuses he'd been making ever since he came to camp, and he was weary of hearing himself say them. He took the rope from her, untied the slip, and set a different hitch that would tighten against the post instead of sliding along it.

"There," he said. "Happy?"

"Ecstatic. I might write a song about it." She tested the line and nodded reluctantly. "You were right about the water turning by the landing. Beckendorf said the reeds were circling against the wind."

Leandros looked at her suspiciously. Praise from Clarisse usually arrived disguised as a threat. "He said that?"

"I just told you he did. Try listening to a sentence without looking for the knife hidden in it."

He almost answered that she was the last person who ought to complain about hidden knives, but thought better of it. Clarisse's bluntness infuriated him because it was so difficult to argue with. When she was wrong, she tended to be wrong loudly, and when she was right she didn't wait for anyone to applaud. He had seen her work through a cold evening with wet gloves, moving rope coils off the lower shelf so no younger camper would have to do it in the dark. She didn't seem to think this made her a leader. She simply noticed a job and did it. He couldn't decide whether he admired that or resented it.

"I didn't want the bank to be unstable," he said. "I wanted to be right about the current."

"Then you can be right about the current and sorry about the bank. I manage two thoughts all the time."

"Only two?"

"Careful. I'm in a good mood."

He laughed despite himself, and she gave him a sharp look as if the sound had taken her by surprise. It struck him suddenly that she was trying, too. Not to like him, not to make the cabin feel like a family because somebody had told them it ought to, but to get through a day without assuming every interaction was a challenge. He had made that unnecessarily difficult for her from the moment he arrived.

By the time Annabeth returned, carrying a stiff roll of paper almost as long as her arm, the sky had brightened enough for the lake's surface to lose its flat gray color. She had Percy with her, and both were talking at once. Percy was insisting he had discovered a faster way to get the old map from the attic. Annabeth claimed that climbing into the attic through the wrong hatch didn't count as faster if he then had to climb back out to find the right one. The argument was affectionate and so well rehearsed that Leandros could have predicted both lines before they reached him.

He didn't know what to do with his reaction to that. He liked Percy in flashes, especially when his brother forgot about the difference between them and said something ridiculous. The rest of the time he watched him receive affection as easily as other people received the morning weather. It was unfair, and Leandros knew it was unfair, because he had no idea how often Percy had gone without affection in whatever life lay behind his strange memories. He only knew what he could see: Annabeth tugging the back of Percy's coat into place without being asked, Percy taking the heavier end of her rolled map as though he had been carrying it all his life, both of them smiling before they even finished speaking.

"Morning, Lord Captain of the Missing Stake," Percy called.

Leandros rolled his eyes. "You think of that all by yourself?"

"Had help from the coast guard. Very clever people."

Annabeth shook her head and spread the map across a dry board. "Ignore him. I did."

"That would make you unique," Percy said. "I don't think anyone's ignored me successfully for more than forty-five minutes."

"That's because you start singing."

"Only when I need attention."

Leandros was struck by a private, shameful flicker of envy. Percy could ask for attention like that, as a joke, because he trusted it would be given. He himself had spent years trying to become exceptional enough that somebody would offer it without being asked. At the marina he had been the boy who could read the tide and find a mooring in fog. Here he was one more demigod among people whose powers made his knowledge seem embarrassingly ordinary. Annabeth bent over the old map, a pencil held between her teeth, and he hated how keenly he wanted her to notice that he had found something none of them had seen.

The old drawing was a mess of faded lines, water stains, and cramped handwriting. Somebody had mapped the drainage cut before the newer landing was built, then crossed out the original route and added a second. Annabeth weighted one corner with a stone and began comparing the old and new positions. She forgot about Leandros immediately, which irritated him until he recognized the look in her eyes. It was the same thing he felt standing at the end of a dock at night, listening for a change in the water. She was pursuing an answer, and for that moment it mattered more than anything anyone thought of her.

"Here," she said. "The cut used to run under the western stones. That's why the seam is further inland than I expected. The old map has a note about moving the outflow during the landing repair, but nobody drew where it went afterward."

Leandros leaned over the board. His shoulder almost touched hers, and both shifted automatically, the smallest concession to sharing the space. He wasn't accustomed to being able to look over a map with somebody whose first instinct was to ask a better question rather than tell him he was wrong. He'd spent so much time defending himself around Annabeth that it seemed absurd to notice now how focused she was when the argument turned to something real.

"If the channel was filled with loose soil, it could be washing out again," he said. "Especially if the creek's higher than usual."

"Not enough to explain the whole crack." Annabeth marked the place where the two lines diverged. "The weight of those stones would push the bank down once the soil moved, but I want to know how far the hollow goes."

Beckendorf took a careful look and frowned. "I'm not having anyone put a rod into that seam while the ground is wet. We'll inspect from the upper path and get Chiron to see the older plans."

"There's another way to tell whether the water is moving underneath," Leandros said.

Three faces turned toward him. He had wanted their attention, and now he found himself wishing he'd waited until he was sure.

"My mother used to test the old pilings at the marina when the mud was thick. She'd float a strip of bright cloth past the pier, watch where it went, and measure how long it took to reach the open channel. If it suddenly sped up or slowed down, there was something under the surface changing the flow. You don't need to go onto the bank at all. You can do it from the solid part of the landing."

Percy grinned. "My brother's proposing science with laundry. I approve."

Annabeth ignored the joke. Her pencil moved to the edge of the page as she calculated. "A floating strip would only tell us about surface movement. We'd have to repeat it in several positions and account for wind, but it might show where the return flow starts." She looked up at him. "Can you tell when a strip is actually caught in a current and when it's just being blown by the wind?"

"Yeah," Leandros said, too quickly. Then he corrected himself. "Usually. You watch what the water's doing around it. The ripples are different. We could anchor another piece of cloth just above the surface so we know what the wind is pushing."

Annabeth's expression changed. It wasn't praise, not quite, but her interest sharpened in a way that made him stand straighter. "That's worth trying."

He had been called clever before. His mother used to say it when he solved a problem that had frustrated the men who worked for her, and he would glow for hours even while pretending it meant nothing. From Annabeth, three plain words felt irritatingly similar. He turned away to find the spare cloth before his face gave him away.

"You're going to need a proper timer," she called after him.

"I know where Chiron keeps the clock."

"I didn't say you didn't."

"Good. I'd hate for us to have our first peaceful morning."

He heard her laugh behind him, quick and surprised. Percy laughed too, and the sound should have made Leandros feel included. Instead it made him feel as if something he didn't understand had opened beneath his feet.

---

The test should have taken half an hour. By the time the first strip floated into the shallows, they had spent almost forty minutes arguing about where to stand, which cloth would be bright enough to see, and how to keep the wind from making nonsense of the measurements. Annabeth wanted to use a repeated timed release from the old stone landing. Leandros objected that the launch point was too close to a snagged branch and would put the cloth into a little eddy that had nothing to do with the underbank channel. Percy observed that both of them seemed determined to make the experiment last until spring. Beckendorf told them all to stop talking at once so he could set the boundary line without somebody tripping over it.

In the end Leandros was right about the snagged branch, and Annabeth was right about the timing method. Neither admitted it particularly gracefully. Beckendorf secured the clock and let Leandros release a narrow strip of red cloth from a safe corner of the landing. It drifted outward, paused, then curved toward the bank where Annabeth's stake had disappeared. The second strip took almost the same path. On the third attempt the wind freshened and carried it farther toward the open water, so Annabeth wrote the time down with a large question mark beside it.

"That's the bit you can't trust," Leandros said, pointing to the third line.

"I know. That's why there's a question mark."

"Good. I was starting to worry you'd forgotten what one looked like."

"I'd say you're welcome to draw your own, but yours would probably insist on being credited."

He laughed. It escaped before he could decide whether he wanted to encourage her. Across the landing Percy was crouched beside Beckendorf, fastening a new warning marker far enough from the crack that no curious camper could reach the dangerous ground. Percy glanced up at the sound, smiling faintly. Leandros felt an odd flicker of embarrassment, as if he'd been caught doing something he didn't yet have a name for. It made no sense. He and Annabeth argued all the time. Their argument had merely become, for a moment, enjoyable.

Annabeth crouched to inspect the cloth floating against the old stones. A gust lifted the loose end of her scarf, and Leandros saw that it was Percy's. Percy wore it often when the nights were particularly cold. He watched Annabeth tuck it more firmly into her collar and felt ashamed of the fact that he had noticed whose scarf it was before he noticed the direction of the wind.

"You're making that face again," she said without looking at him.

"What face?"

"The one that means you're about to tell me my map is wrong."

He almost told her he hadn't been thinking about the map. Instead he took the notebook from under his arm, held it open to the drawing his mother had made years ago of the marina's outer pilings, and showed Annabeth how the current had curved around the submerged timber there. It was the first time he had volunteered a page from his own book without waiting to be asked or trying to establish who would get credit for it. She studied it with such unguarded interest that he immediately wanted to take it back.

"Your mother drew this?" she asked.

"She did the measurements. I copied the sketch. Badly, apparently."

"It isn't bad." Annabeth traced a line above the page without touching it. "It's clear. You can see where the current divides around the piling, even though the surface looks almost straight. How old were you when you learned to read water like this?"

"Twelve, maybe. Younger for some of it. I'd been helping since I was tall enough to carry the lines without tripping on them."

"You must have been insufferable."

"Must have been?"

She smiled down at the drawing, and he laughed in spite of himself. It was not a polished smile. There was ink on the side of her thumb and a faint white mark at the corner of her mouth where she'd bitten it while thinking. Leandros looked away before she could catch him noticing. He had spent much of his life convincing himself that he only admired girls who never challenged him. The thought seemed suddenly ridiculous, so ridiculous that he wondered how many other things he'd said about people had been equally foolish.

Annabeth handed back the notebook. "You should keep a copy of that diagram with the shore records. Somebody might need it when we check the landing again."

He heard an accusation in the offer before he could stop himself. "So everyone can use it and nobody has to remember who drew it?"

She sighed. "Leandros. I just told you your work is good. Do you really need me to stand here and present you with a medal?"

He flushed, irritated first at her tone and then at himself. She was right that he'd gone hunting for an insult in a compliment. But the old hunger had not gone away merely because he knew its shape. "No," he said after a moment. "I suppose a medal would get wet."

She glanced up, surprised. "Almost certainly. And then you'd blame my map."

"You probably drew the medal wrong."

"Now you're pushing it."

He grinned. She shook her head and bent back over the notes. The moment went by quickly, leaving no obvious mark on either of them, but he knew he would remember it. It bothered him to think she might not.

---

Annabeth had no difficulty admitting that Leandros had seen the current before she had. She had a great deal of difficulty admitting it without making a face. On the walk back to the Big House she kept turning over his notebook diagram in her head, irritated by how much it had clarified the one piece of the shore she couldn't understand. The old landing had been built for a different stream course. The water had found the path people tried to cover, as water generally did, and the shore had begun to settle over the unseen hollow. It was an elegant problem once she stopped looking at it as an accusation.

It was also a problem that might have injured someone if Chiron hadn't closed the path. She returned to the old map and circled the inland side of the crack, then wrote a recommendation for Beckendorf to examine the footing after the next frost. She wanted to ask him about a temporary raised walkway. She wanted to find the old construction record and calculate how much soil might have moved. She wanted, irrationally, to go back to the lake and stand beside Leandros while he explained the water again, because he knew how to see a part of the problem she couldn't yet see for herself.

That last wish made her close the notebook.

Thalia was at the cabin table when Annabeth got back, drawing little arrows in the margin of a book instead of reading it. Her cane stood beside the chair, and her splinted foot was propped on a low stool. She had been allowed a few arrows on level ground every other day, not the full archery session she wanted, and the restriction had put her in a temper that waxed and waned with the weather. Annabeth found the temper easier to live with than the strained politeness with which people sometimes treated Thalia now. Her sister had always been sharp. Being injured hadn't changed that.

"You've been at the lake," Thalia said without looking up.

"How did you guess?"

"You smell like cold water and you're carrying a map with mud on the corner. I have powers of observation. Sometimes people forget that."

Annabeth set the map on the table, careful to keep the damp side away from the open book. "We found out why the north bank's settling. Or part of why. The old channel's been undermining the stones, and Leandros knew a way to test the current without stepping onto the crack."

"Was he unbearable about it?"

"Less than usual."

Thalia looked up. "That bad, huh?"

Annabeth smiled despite herself. She loved her sister's ability to dismantle a sentence with six words, especially when those six words weren't aimed at her. There were moments when she wished she could go back to the days before Zeus's volatile visits and Ianthe's arrival, when Cabin One had been messy in familiar ways and Thalia's protectiveness had been the chief problem between them. Those days had never been as simple as she remembered. Thalia had spent years trying to hold a family together with her own two hands, and Annabeth had loved her for it while resenting the feeling of being held.

"He was useful," Annabeth said. "Actually useful. He knew a kind of current I didn't know how to read. I don't think he even realized how much his mother's old diagrams helped."

Thalia raised an eyebrow. "You sound impressed."

"That's allowed, isn't it?"

"Didn't say it wasn't." She went back to her book and shifted her heel carefully to the side. "It's just that when you first met him, you said he needed someone to introduce him to the concept of other people's opinions."

"He does."

"And now he's a genius?"

"I didn't say that."

"Good. Because I was getting worried about your standards."

Annabeth threw a folded cloth at her. Thalia caught it, laughing, and for a few minutes the old ease returned between them. Annabeth described the floating cloth experiment, deliberately emphasizing how badly Leandros had explained the first part and how much time Percy had spent making jokes about the nonexistent coast guard. Thalia asked practical questions about the shoreline and warned her not to go down the closed path again until Beckendorf cleared it. Annabeth told her she'd already agreed to that. Neither tried to turn the conversation into proof that one sister trusted the other more than yesterday.

Ianthe arrived with damp hair and a cup of tea, looking irritated enough to be an honorary Grace. She had spent the morning at the drying rack, observing wind movement without touching any of the equipment, and had apparently been told by Beckendorf that the notes were useful but incomplete. Annabeth knew the look. Ianthe wanted to produce something nobody could criticize, preferably before anybody had asked for it. She had inherited from Zeus a gift for turning attention into a weapon, though she was still learning how to put it down.

"What's wrong with the north shore?" Ianthe asked, setting her cup beside the map.

"A part of the bank is settling," Annabeth said. "The path's closed until Beckendorf has checked the footing."

"Can I see where?"

Annabeth unrolled the map and showed her. Ianthe studied the paired water routes, paying particular attention to the note beside the older line. The note had been written in Annabeth's cramped handwriting, with Leandros's observation recorded as its source. Ianthe frowned.

"He was the one who noticed the current?" she asked.

"Yes. Why?"

"Nothing. I thought he would spend the whole morning telling you that maps are useless."

"He tried that yesterday. Apparently he can do more than one thing."

Ianthe smiled unwillingly. "You don't have to sound so surprised."

"I know. I just wish people would make it easier."

Thalia snorted. "Listen to yourself."

Annabeth couldn't argue. She'd spent the morning wanting Leandros to take her work seriously, then bristled when he offered something she hadn't known. She'd been angry that he wanted credit and then surprised when he cared enough about an observation to put himself at risk of being laughed at. The fact that she could see her own hypocrisy did not make her like it. She was tempted to go back to the lake solely to prove she'd learned the lesson, which was another way of making the morning about herself.

Instead she helped Ianthe interpret the wind records and left the lake until the afternoon. Ianthe had marked the hours correctly but had failed to note the brief gust that turned the flag near the rack. Annabeth showed her how to distinguish the reading from the possible cause without making either sound like a verdict. Ianthe listened, wrote something in the margin, and complained that she would never finish a chart if every observation needed an explanation. Thalia suggested she start with the weather and leave the philosophical crisis for another day.

They ate a late lunch together. Percy came in with muddy boots and an exaggerated tale about rescuing a rope from Clarisse's clutches, followed by Clarisse herself, who informed everyone that Percy had, in fact, dropped it. The cabin filled with the ordinary confusion of people arriving while someone was still trying to set the table. Silena had brought hot soup from the kitchens, and the smell of roasted onions drove every other thought from Annabeth's mind for almost ten glorious minutes.

Percy sat beside her and stole the corner of her bread. She stole it back, dipped it in soup, and ate it before he could complain. Thalia made a face at both of them. Ianthe looked as if she was trying to work out whether the entire household operated on an incomprehensible set of rituals. In a way it did. The rituals weren't especially grand. They consisted mostly of arguments over food, jokes that had been made too many times, and the knowledge that somebody would move closer if you grew quiet.

At one point Percy put his hand over hers beneath the table. Annabeth looked at him, expecting another joke, and found him watching her with a seriousness that startled her.

"Did the lake thing scare you?" he asked under the noise of the conversation.

"A little. Someone could have fallen through the bank before we noticed."

"Yeah." He rubbed his thumb along the side of her finger. "You were so focused on the map that I wasn't sure you'd heard Chiron say the path was closed."

"I heard him. I know where I can stand."

"I know you do. I'm not trying to run your day." Percy sighed and stared at their joined hands. "I just get a little nervous when the ground decides to vanish under somebody."

She understood what he didn't say. Their shared past was full of places that had opened beneath them, literal and otherwise, and he was allowed to hate the thought of losing her again. She squeezed his hand and leaned her head briefly against his shoulder. He turned and kissed the top of her hair, making a small noise of satisfaction as if this had been his plan all along. It would have been easier if loving Percy made every other confusing part of her life disappear. It never had. Love wasn't an eraser, and she had known that long before she knew how to say it.

"Chiron asked me to finish the shore report," she told him. "I'm going back up after lunch. Leandros might have some more notes from his mother."

Percy nodded. "That makes sense. He looked happy this morning. I haven't seen him happy much."

Annabeth felt an unexpected flicker of guilt, small and ridiculous. She had not done anything wrong. She had admired another boy's ability to read a lake. Percy had admired it too. The shame came from the fact that she had been thinking about Leandros when Percy was trying to talk to her, and there was no rule against thinking about someone. She knew that. She disliked how quickly her mind wanted to construct a defense for something nobody had accused her of.

"He's good at this," she said.

"He is. Maybe I should ask him to show me how he reads the currents. I know what the water does when I ask it to move. That's not the same as knowing how it moves when nobody asks."

"I think he'd like that."

Percy looked surprised. "You do?"

"Why not?"

"He acts like I'm the last person he wants to teach anything."

"Because he thinks you already know everything."

"That's hilarious. I barely know where Silena keeps the good knives."

Annabeth laughed. Percy smiled, and the warmth of it made her want to pull him into a proper kiss and put the morning out of her head. She did. His arms went around her with easy familiarity, and Thalia made a theatrical sound of disgust across the table. Annabeth didn't care. She kissed him again, shorter this time, just because she could, and felt nothing but love for him in that moment.

Later she would remember the kiss and hate the part of herself that wanted to measure it against feelings she hadn't even admitted to having. For now she took his bread, accused him of stealing hers first, and let the afternoon wait.

Leandros found Annabeth at the north shore after lunch, standing well above the warning rope with her coat buttoned to the throat and a measuring rod planted in the gravel beside her. He had expected her to be at the Big House, buried in books. He had brought his notebook because he wanted to show her the second diagram from the marina, but now that he'd found her alone he couldn't decide whether coming back had been a mistake. People tended to interpret his presence as a demand, especially when he arrived carrying evidence.

She looked up before he spoke. "I thought you'd be helping Beckendorf with the coils."

"Finished what he asked me to do. Didn't rearrange anything without permission. You can put it in my permanent record."

"I'll be sure to make the ink red."

He smiled before he meant to. Annabeth's voice had a way of being sharp without being cruel when she was genuinely amused. He was beginning to think he'd spent weeks provoking her because he'd wanted that amusement pointed at him, though he couldn't have said why. He opened the notebook to the diagram he had marked before lunch and held it out to her.

"My mother kept a record of the outer pilings after the harbor wall was rebuilt," he said. "The first sketch I showed you was from that. I found the one with the measurements on the next page. You said you wanted to know how far the hollow might go. I thought the method could help."

Annabeth took the notebook. She held it close enough to study the small figures but carefully enough that she didn't bend the cover. Leandros watched her expression shift from skepticism to interest, then to the fierce concentration he recognized from the morning. He felt an ugly spark of satisfaction when she made a note beside one of the figures. It was the first time since arriving at camp that he had given her something she seemed to need.

"Your mother was thorough," she said. "This is excellent. Did she do the calculations herself?"

"Most of them. She had help from a man who'd worked the harbor for thirty years. She used to say he could tell you which boat was coming home by the way the water hit the pilings, and he couldn't add three numbers without taking off his shoes."

Annabeth laughed. "I want to meet her."

The words surprised him. He looked at her, expecting the usual teasing, but she was still reading the notebook as though she had forgotten he was standing there. He pictured his mother in the little office by the marina, hair escaping its clip, pencil behind one ear, arguing with a supplier over the price of rope. She would like Annabeth. The thought arrived without warning and made him unreasonably uncomfortable.

"Maybe you will," he said. "If she comes to visit."

"Does she know how much you've been helping with the shoreline?"

"Not exactly." He took the notebook back when she offered it. "I've told her we're doing repairs. I haven't written much about the family side."

Annabeth looked at him for a moment, then away toward the lake. "That part must be harder to explain."

He felt the familiar prick of defensiveness. "Don't start talking like you know what it's like."

"I don't. I didn't say I did."

"I know." He hated the automatic reply. He could hear himself becoming the boy who fought over every word, and he wanted, absurdly, to be someone else in front of her. "Sorry. My mother wanted me to find people who'd understand why the sea matters to me. I arrived here and spent half my time fighting with a sister I hadn't even known existed. That's not exactly what she was imagining."

Annabeth stood silent for a moment. Wind made the ends of her scarf flutter against her coat. Leandros saw that she'd tucked it carefully into the collar, protecting it from the wet gravel. It was Percy's scarf; he knew that now without having to think about it. His brother's things seemed to turn up everywhere, even in the moments when he was far away.

"Maybe she doesn't need you to have everything figured out," Annabeth said. "Maybe she'd rather know you miss her."

Leandros looked at the water. He had missed his mother every day and resented the fact that admitting it made him sound young. The marina had been home long before he knew that Poseidon was his father; being claimed had not made that home less real, and coming here had not made him stop wanting it. He'd tried to explain that to Percy once and heard himself sounding like a child who couldn't decide where to live. Annabeth didn't ask him to decide. That was part of why her answer frightened him.

"I do," he said. "I miss her."

She nodded, and he was unexpectedly grateful that she didn't make a speech about it. The silence between them held for a few breaths, filled by water moving against the shore. In the distance they could hear someone working a loose latch on the boathouse door, the faint metallic knock carrying over the still air. It would have been an ordinary pause with anybody else. Leandros found himself wanting it to last.
