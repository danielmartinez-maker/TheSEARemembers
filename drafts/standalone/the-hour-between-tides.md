# The Hour Between Tides

Annabeth had not meant to leave Cabin One.

That was the thought she held onto while she crossed the sleeping camp with her coat half-buttoned and her hair still loose from where she'd given up trying to braid it in the dark. She had woken because the wind had changed. At least, that was what she told herself. The shutters had rattled once against the frame, and then the room had settled again, leaving only Thalia's steady breathing, Ianthe turning over in her bunk, and the restless, irritating fact of Annabeth being awake with too much room in her own head.

The shore path was cold under her shoes. Frost had silvered the grass and edged the low stones with a pale shine. The cabins behind her stood black and still, their windows dark except for a stray lamp in the Big House, where someone had either forgotten to put it out or was awake for reasons of their own. The camp at night never felt empty to Annabeth. It felt suspended, as if all the things people said and did during the day remained hanging in the air after they went inside, waiting to be picked up again in the morning.

She shoved her hands into her pockets and kept walking until the trees opened and the beach appeared below.

The lake was almost black at midnight, though the moon had laid a narrow silver path across the surface that trembled whenever the water moved. The wind had dropped by the shore. The waves came in gently, hardly deserving the name, each one flattening itself along the pebbled edge with a hush like someone trying not to wake a house. Annabeth stood still for a moment and breathed.

This was why she had come out. Not because she was troubled. Not because she was thinking too much. Not because there was one particular face she had spent a stupid amount of time trying not to picture since supper. Just for the quiet. Just because the lake at night made her feel as if the world had enough shape again for her to fit inside it.

Then she saw someone already on the beach.

He was sitting on one of the driftwood logs near the waterline, elbows on his knees, looking out over the lake with the sort of stillness that meant he had not expected company and had no idea yet whether he wanted it. Leandros turned only when her shoes knocked loose a scatter of pebbles coming down the short slope. Even in the moonlight she could tell from the set of his shoulders that he recognized her before she spoke.

“Do you always sneak up on people at midnight,” he asked, “or am I getting special treatment?”

Annabeth stopped three steps from the log. “If I wanted to sneak up on you, you wouldn't have heard me.”

“That sounds less reassuring than I think you intended.”

“You're the one sitting alone on the beach like a suspicious figure in a ghost story.”

He glanced behind himself at the empty lake. “I could say the same thing about you.”

“Yes, but I was here second, so I get to be judgmental.”

Leandros gave a short laugh. It was quiet enough that the sound barely carried past the surf, but it changed him. During the day he always seemed braced for something, as if expecting correction before anyone spoke. Now, with no audience and no task between them, he looked younger and more tired and less interested in pretending otherwise.

Annabeth hesitated.

If she had been sensible, she would have said good night and gone back to the cabins. That would have been the sensible thing. There were dozens of sensible things Annabeth could think of at any given moment. It had never guaranteed she would do one of them.

“Are you using the whole beach,” she asked, “or can I stand here without starting a territorial dispute?”

He shifted over on the log and tapped the empty section beside him. “I think we can avoid war for one night.”

“That would be new.”

“Don't sound so disappointed.”

She almost said no thanks. Instead she sat.

The driftwood was colder than it looked. Through her coat she could feel the damp stored inside the grain, the chill of something that had spent too long near the water. Leandros had a wool cap pulled low over his hair and his hands tucked beneath his arms for warmth. He glanced at her once as she settled beside him, then back at the lake, as though eye contact at this hour required more intention than either of them was ready to admit.

For a while they said nothing.

Annabeth was fine with silence when it belonged to thinking. Most people grew nervous around it and filled it with useless words. Leandros, at least tonight, seemed content to let the quiet hold. The beach stretched away on both sides, pale stone, darker sand, a line of reeds trembling near the inlet. Somewhere farther upshore a night bird called once, then again, and stopped.

“What are you doing out here?” she asked eventually.

Leandros shrugged under his coat. “Couldn't sleep.”

“That answer usually means you don't want the real one.”

“You asked a midnight question. I gave a midnight answer.”

She turned slightly toward him. “That's not a real category.”

“It is if I'm the one making it.”

Annabeth should have found that annoying. She almost did. But there was no edge in his voice tonight, no challenge he was trying to force her to answer. Just a tired sort of honesty in the admission that he didn't want to explain himself immediately.

So she looked back at the water and said, “Fine. Then I couldn't sleep either.”

He let that sit for a moment. “Is that your midnight answer?”

“Yes.”

“And if I asked for the real one?”

“You'd be disappointed. It's mostly me thinking too much.”

“That hardly seems disappointing.” He bent to pick up a flat white stone near his boot and turned it over in his fingers. “Thinking too much is one of your main hobbies.”

Annabeth gave him a look. “You say that as if you've made a study of me.”

“Not a study.” He tossed the stone lightly from one hand to the other. “More of an observation.”

“That's worse.”

“Probably.”

The answer should have ended there. Instead it stayed between them, warmer than the air, and Annabeth became sharply aware of how close they were sitting on the log. Not touching. Not enough for anyone to remark on if they had somehow been seen from the cabins. Just close enough that if either shifted carelessly their shoulders would brush.

She was the one who moved first, drawing one knee up onto the log and turning enough to face him properly.

“You've been strange around me all week,” she said.

Leandros let out a breath that might have been a laugh, except there wasn't much humor in it. “Only around you?”

“More around me than usual.”

“And you've been easy to deal with?”

“I didn't say that.”

“No. You rarely say things that kind.”

“That wasn't kind,” Annabeth said. “It was accurate.”

He looked at her then, fully, and the moonlight made his expression harder to read rather than easier. She could see the line of his mouth, the tiredness under his eyes, the way the cold had reddened the tip of his nose. He had a scar just under his chin she had only noticed once before, pale against his skin where the daylight caught it differently. He wasn't handsome in the easy way Percy was handsome, warm and open and impossible not to love. There was something less comfortable in Leandros. Something sharper. More guarded. A face that looked as if it had learned to expect disappointment and had become proud to make up for it.

Annabeth hated how interesting she found that.

“I know I've been strange,” he said at last.

“Why?”

He turned the stone over again. “Do you want the midnight answer or the real one?”

“The real one.”

“That seems unwise.”

“Leandros.”

His mouth moved very slightly at the sound of his name in her voice. It might have been annoyance. It might have been something else. “All right,” he said. “Because every time I talk to you, I leave either irritated or thinking about what you said hours later, and I don't particularly like either experience.”

Annabeth stared at him.

That was not what she had expected. She had expected deflection, perhaps a complaint about maps, maybe a roundabout speech about being corrected in front of people. Not that.

“Irritated,” she repeated.

“Often.”

“And yet you're here.”

“That is, unfortunately, part of the problem.”

She should have laughed. She could almost hear the line in Percy's voice, turned into a joke, made harmless. But Percy was not here, and Leandros was looking at her as if he had just admitted something against his better judgment and was waiting to see whether she would use it against him.

Annabeth looked away first.

The lake glittered in broken silver. Her pulse had changed. She hated that she could feel it, quick and alive under her skin like something trying to get her attention. She dug her nails into the sleeve of her coat and told herself firmly that all he had done was say she lingered in his mind after arguments. That was not a confession. It was barely even tenderness. It could still be dismissed.

So why didn't she want to dismiss it?

“Maybe you keep thinking about what I say,” she said, “because I'm usually right.”

“See, that's exactly the kind of thing I mean.”

She smiled despite herself. “I was helping you.”

“You were proving my point.”

“Same thing, sometimes.”

He shook his head, and she saw his own smile this time, reluctant and brief. It made him look less defended. Younger, maybe, though not in a way that made him seem weak. Just less armored.

“You're impossible,” he said.

Annabeth folded her arms. “Funny. I was about to say the same thing.”

“I got there first.”

“I don't think that's how conversation works.”

“I'm not sure you care how conversation works if you're winning it.”

“That sounds like bitterness.”

“It probably is.”

Again, the line could have become an argument. That was the pattern they had set for each other from the first week. Push, answer, bristle, push back harder. Tonight it didn't. Something in the dark and the water and the lateness of the hour had taken the sharpest part out of it. Or maybe the sharpest part was still there and they were simply too tired to hide behind it.

Annabeth rested her elbows on her knees. “Why couldn't you sleep?”

The question came out quieter this time. Not an interrogation. Not even really a demand.

Leandros was silent long enough that she thought he might refuse after all. Then he said, “Because sometimes I lie down and I can feel how far away home is.”

She did not answer right away.

There were a dozen easy responses. A dozen clumsy ones. She could say she was sorry. She could ask whether he meant Atlantis or his mother. She could tell him that homesickness was ordinary and survivable and nothing to be ashamed of. Every one of those would have sounded prepared. Polite. Slightly wrong.

So she asked, “Your mother?”

He nodded.

“I thought this place would fix something,” he said, still looking at the water. “Not everything. I'm not stupid. But I thought at least there'd be a feeling that I was where I ought to be.” He gave a small, humorless shrug. “Most days it just feels like I'm somewhere new enough to be making mistakes in public.”

Annabeth could hear how hard it had been for him to say that without turning it into a joke or a challenge. She knew something about pride. She knew what it cost to speak plainly when pride had been doing the work of keeping you upright.

“Camp doesn't fix things,” she said. “It just puts you close to other people whose things are broken too.”

Leandros glanced at her. “That's almost comforting.”

“Don't sound so surprised. I can be comforting.”

“No,” he said, and there was more warmth than mockery in it now. “I know you can.”

The words settled low in her chest.

Annabeth should not have noticed them that way. She knew that even as she noticed. Percy knew she could be comforting. Thalia did. Grover, Clarisse, Ianthe, Silena. That was not special. It was not intimate. It was not supposed to feel like being seen in a place she had not realized was exposed.

She hated the heat rising under her skin. Hated it more because the night was cold enough that she could not blame the temperature.

“You're staring,” Leandros said quietly.

“I am not.”

“You are a little.”

“That sounds like confidence from someone who hasn't looked away either.”

He smiled again, but this time neither of them laughed.

The distance between them seemed to change without either moving. Annabeth was suddenly aware of details she had no business being aware of: the rough edge of his coat sleeve where a seam had been repaired, a salt-faded thread on the cuff, the slow way he breathed when he was trying not to say too much. She had spent enough of her life reading rooms, battlefields, faces, that she knew when something had shifted before anyone named it.

This had shifted.

Her heart beat once, hard enough to be annoying.

Percy flashed through her mind then, sudden and vivid. Percy smiling at breakfast. Percy reaching for her hand under the table because he knew without asking when her thoughts had started running too fast. Percy warm and familiar and loved, not in theory but in fact, in the deep settled way that had already survived more than most people survived in a lifetime.

The memory should have been enough to send her back up the path.

Instead it made everything hurt.

Leandros must have seen something change in her face. His own expression tightened, not with anger but with the same reluctant understanding she had just had herself.

“We should probably go,” he said.

“Yes,” Annabeth answered, though she didn't move.

Neither did he.

The water touched the shore and withdrew. The moonlight broke and mended itself on the small waves.

“This was a bad idea,” Leandros said.

“Sitting on a beach?”

“You know that's not what I mean.”

Annabeth did know. She also knew that if she answered honestly, the whole night would stop pretending to be harmless. Her mouth had gone dry. She tucked her hands under her arms because suddenly she could not think of anywhere else to put them.

“Then what do you mean?” she asked anyway.

He shut his eyes for a second. “I mean being here with you when it's like this.”

“Like what?”

“Don't make me say every part of it.”

There it was. No joke this time. No room to misunderstand him on purpose unless she chose to.

Annabeth was so intensely conscious of him that it felt like standing too close to a fire and pretending not to feel the heat. She had wanted honesty. She had asked for it. Now it sat between them and she wanted, desperately and absurdly, to be able to put it back.

“You think I don't know?” she said.

His head turned sharply toward her. “Do you?”

The question went through her like cold water.

Because yes. She did know. She had known for longer than she had admitted. In every argument that lasted half a beat too long. In every moment when his attention on her felt too focused to be accidental. In the stupid, electrified awareness she carried away afterward and could never explain to herself in terms she liked.

And if she knew it in him, then she had to know it in herself.

Annabeth stood up too fast.

The log shifted under the movement. Pebbles crunched beneath her shoes. She took three steps toward the water before stopping because there was nowhere useful to go. The beach ended at the lake. The lake ended at the dark line of trees on the far side. There was no path through this part except straight through it.

Behind her, Leandros rose too, slower.

“I shouldn't have said anything,” he said.

“Maybe not.”

“That's fair.”

She turned back. “Don't agree with me just because I'm angry.”

“I'm not agreeing because you're angry.”

“Then why?”

“Because you look like you're deciding whether to throw me into the lake.”

Annabeth almost laughed. The sound caught in her throat and became something else. She pressed a hand to her forehead, furious with herself.

“This is unbelievable,” she said. “I came out here because I couldn't sleep.”

“And now you definitely won't.”

“That isn't helping.”

“I know.”

“You keep saying that.”

“Because I do know.”

He had come closer without her noticing, not right up against her, not enough to crowd. Just enough that if she lifted her head she no longer had to raise her voice. The moonlight fell across one side of his face and left the other in shadow. Annabeth could see the tension in him now too. He was not composed. He was not enjoying this. He was enduring it with the same helpless awareness she was, and somehow that made it worse.

“Tell me to go,” he said.

The words were low and steady and impossible.

Annabeth looked at him.

All she had to do was say it. He had given her the exit. If she told him to leave, he would. She believed that. Then she would walk back to Cabin One and lie awake until dawn and hate this night and maybe, with enough time, force it into a shape she could live with. That was what should happen.

Instead she said, “Do you want me to?”

Leandros's mouth parted very slightly. “No.”

The honesty of it struck her like a blow.

Something inside Annabeth gave way then. Not judgment. Not love for Percy. Not the knowledge that this was a terrible idea. Those things remained exactly where they were. What gave way was the last thin layer of distance she had been holding between herself and wanting.

She took one step toward him.

He did not move.

Another.

Now they were close enough that she could feel the heat coming off him in the cold air, close enough that his eyes searched her face with an intensity that made her pulse trip again. He still had not touched her. The restraint of it felt unbearably intimate.

“Annabeth,” he said.

She did not know what warning or question might have come after her name, because she never let him say it.

She caught the front of his coat in one fist and kissed him.

The kiss was not soft. It was not careful. It was not anything she could lie to herself about later. It was midnight and cold air and weeks of friction turning suddenly into heat. Leandros made a rough, startled sound that vanished when he kissed her back, and for one suspended, reckless instant the whole world narrowed to the pressure of his mouth and the hand that finally rose to her face as if he still could not quite believe he was allowed to touch her.

The lake went on moving in the dark.

And then there was nothing else.
